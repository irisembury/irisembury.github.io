The government of Canada [has announced](https://www.cbc.ca/news/business/canada-sovereign-wealth-fund-explainer-9.7179217) we're going to develop a general sovereign wealth fund. A sovereign wealth fund just means a bunch of financial assets or investments owned by the government itself. A lot of people think they're a good idea to encourage the public feeling invested in the broad economic success of the country, and they can be used strategically by governments in various ways. And as soon as our PM, Mr. Carney, made this announcement, the opposition converged on a simple talking point: Canada has debt, therefore this isn't a real sovereign wealth fund, because a real sovereign wealth fund requires wealth, not debt. Norway has a sovereign wealth fund because they're a net surplus nation. Canada runs a deficit, therefore we can't have a sovereign wealth fund. Why is this so? Why can't a country both hold debt and hold investments at the same time? I have no idea. And unfortunately, none of the people making this retort made any effort to explain or elaborate. I've heard people talk about wanting a sovereign wealth fund in Canada before, and people wanting one in the U.S. as well (which [it supposedly committed to doing earlier this year](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/02/a-plan-for-establishing-a-united-states-sovereign-wealth-fund/)). And I don't remember anyone saying a requirement to have a sovereign wealth fund was we had to solve our debt and become a net surplus nation first. That seems like something everybody "decided" in response to Carney's announcement. And to be sure, a government holding certain funds it manages in this way is not that uncommon, not even a totally new thing for Canada, and certainly not limited to surplus nations. The opposition's talking point is made up, shallow, contrived, meant only to offer the appearance of a rebuttal for people who will pay attention to the news for no more than a few seconds. The only real question was where the money to spend on making the investment was going to come from. And obviously for Canada, since we don't have a bunch of government-owned oil companies to draw revenue from (like Saudi Arabia, say), the principal is going to be either taxation, borrowing, or both --- the same way we pay for literally everything. If the government expands healthcare, or builds a pipeline, or increases our military spending, that's all paid for with taxes or borrowing. And the government gave us its answer: they planned on borrowing money for seed funds, because Canada is considered a very safe debtor internationally, so we can borrow at very low rates. Then part of what we're going to do with that money is invest broadly in our own national infrastructure and energy economy, since we're also planning on expanding in those areas. So it will be a strategic tool for the country and only grow more effective over time. The one component of it that sounded unusual --- which isn't to say bad, necessarily, but unusual --- is their plan to let anybody invest in the fund, meaning if you throw your money in, you'll get a share of the returns for that money, like any other investment. After the government announced it was going to be seeded with borrowed money, the opposition talking point became comparing it with a credit card. The whole plan was compared with a person being in credit card debt, and then taking out even more credit card debt to invest in the stock market. The opposition leader, Mr. Poilievre, called it the credit card budget, and said Liberals are just [putting "more bills on the national credit card"](https://www.cbc.ca/player/play/video/9.7180620). What makes this appealing rhetoric is that normal people view credit card debt as bad, but the reason credit card debt is bad is that it's given to you at such high rates. It's not inherently bad to hold any debt at all. It's possible to hold too much of it, and for it to become a problem, but within some limit debt is good. It lets you spend future money before you could access it otherwise. That's why people borrow money. It's why every successful person has debt, it's why every big company has debt, it's why most countries have debt. The idea that debt is bad because credit card debt is bad is a supreme act of oversimplification. The pitch by the Canadian government is that Canada is that this is an unusual time for Canada. We're in a very difficult spot financially, mostly but not entirely due to U.S. tariffs disrupting our trade relations. And we're in desperate need of investment and expansion. Now is a reasonable time to seek immediate relief in the form of borrowed money, because we have impeccable access to cheap credit, and our country has a bright future and will be able to pay it off. And in Mr. Carney, we have leadership with the exact sort of expertise one would want at a time like this. If the opposition wants to convince us we're on the wrong path, they're going to need to do better than simplistic, kneejerk analogies and actually grapple with the substance of what the Liberals have put forward.
