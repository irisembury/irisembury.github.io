Now that the U.S. election season is over and that country has once again walked down the orange brick road, let's talk about something I was putting off pondering about publicly while the race was still on. Close your eyes and think "politician." Not a specific politician, but politician as a category of person, like if we were casting for a TV show and we needed a generic politician character. Chances are pretty good you thought of a white guy, probably in his 40s or 50s, and with a simple first name --- something like John, Rob, Scott, James, Rick. It *can* be less common, but nothing that people will mispronounce --- or worse yet be *corrected* about the pronunciation of. And he just looks normal. Clean-cut, short hair, suit and tie. Not too effeminate. Not somebody who crossdresses on weekends. Not too gruff either. He shouldn't look like a mechanic or construction worker. But not too effeminate. We need a normal, balanced amount of masculinity. A dad. A young grandpa. That's the template of the kind of person we trust to run the world. The further you stray from this template, the more you have to justify yourself, or the more you have to compensate for. "Yes, he dresses like a dork, or he has a weird name --- *buuuut*, he has [other qualities] that make him stand above the rest despite that!" It's not impossible for a person outside the template to get elected, of course, but if there's nothing remarkable about you, nothing impressive and above-average that you bring to the table, then why would people choose you instead of a normal white guy? In a race between the median normal white guy and the median of something else, people are just going to pick the white guy. The electorate has a normal-white-guy bias. The normal white guy is the default politician. If you're a gay dude, then you better be the best goddamn gay around. A model gay. Bonus points if you're the kind of person we can't even tell is gay at first, and who doesn't weird anyone out. Don't kiss your husband in public, if you have one. But even this is a tall order to justify. Some people will just plainly not support you if they know you're out there man-bedding. In the U.S. today, the go-to example of a gay politician has become Pete Buttigieg. He even has a simple-enough first name. Pete. Really, just try to reduce how much people have to think about the buttagidige part at the end, and now that it's been acknowledge, let's not to much of a spotlight on his gayness. It's a downgrade. He doesn't bring enough to the table to compensate for his gayness. If you're black, you have an even higher bar to get over. Americans are most comfortable with black people when they're in same-race relationships, because that keeps out any sort of weirdness. In the public's view, women and military stuff don't splice. We've had successful female politicians in other countries, though, and we can understand the dance by examining them. To make it big-time, you have to be tough enough to be taken seriously, but you can't be too hard because then you're get called a shrill bitch, and you can't be too sexy or come off as too emotional because then men won't take you seriously. In the 1960s, a third party called the New Democratic Party (NDP) was founded. Under the leadership of **Jack Layton** --- a normal white guy with a simple name --- it grew a lot in the early 2000s, culminating in the 2011 general election, their best performance ever, and the Liberal Party's worst ever. The NDP moved from 4th to the 2nd largest party in the country, after the Conservatives. Layton was seated as the Official Opposition. This was a huge moment for them, and for Canadian politics in general, and the first time in Canadian history the Liberals weren't one of the two largest parties. Jack Layton had some of the same energy and appeal as Bernie Sanders, but with the added appeal of not being Jewish. He was an old white man who came off as principled, authentic, and caring. But he was also a more effective politician, genuinely trying to expand and grow the party. He was normal, a good speaker, he went on the streets and talked with people. He didn't do anything stupid or controversial. Even relatively apolitical people in my life knew about him and had a good impression of him. Great vibes. Then tragically, he died. Cancer. The year before the election, in 2010, he had cancer but seemed to recover. He became the Opposition Leader in May 2011. In July, he announced that he had another, different type of cancer, but he was fighting it, and then a month later he was pronounced dead, in August. We never found out what type of cancer it was. As far as politicians go, this man was beloved. Everybody mourned. Fast-forward to today, and the NPD is back to being 4th party in the House. A few years after Layton's death, in the 2015 general, the Liberal Party came back stronger than ever under Justin Trudeau's leadership. They went from 36 seats to a majority with 184 seats. The NPD lost more than half of theirs. And while they tried for a round with Tom Mulcair in between, for the last 7 years now the NPD party leader has been a man named **Jagmeet Singh**.
