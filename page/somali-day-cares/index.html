*How did we get here? Why are we talking about Somalis? Why are there Somalis in America? (And yes, as many people have been learning due to the national attention the Somali community has recently been getting, the proper ethnonym and demonym for somebody from Somalia is "Somali," never Somalian --- like how it's Israeli or Bengali, not Israelian or Bengalian.)* After the regime of the U.S.-backed dictator Siad Barre collapsed in 1991, **Somalia** fell into a chaotic civil war, and many Somalis fled the region as refugees. Today there's around a quarter-million Somalis in America, nearly half (about 100,000) of them ending up in the Twin Cities metro area (Minneapolis and Saint Paul, the two largest cities in **Minnesota**). After making it to the U.S., many Somalis became citizens, and now there are thousands of Somalis born and raised in the U.S., or who have been here for decades. Insofar as the Somali community has problems, they are not *illegal immigration* problems. On December 12th, the Minnesota governor, **Tim Walz**, wrote in [the Minnesota Star Tribune](https://www.startribune.com/mn-fraud-scandals-trump-administration-concerns/601544952) about how his state's "generosity" had been "taken advantage of" by "bad actors and criminals" that harm public trust and threaten the state's ability to effectively deliver various programs and services. He says Minnesota cannot have its culture of generosity without "high standards for the integrity of public funds." He points out how the general problem of people misusing public money has been exacerbated as an unintended consequence of how the federal government's 2020 pandemic response prioritized getting funds out rapidly. Walz describes this as a perfect storm for fraud to occur. Indeed, there was widespread defrauding of public funds over the last few years all across the United States and in other countries with similar levels of pandemic spending. In response to fraudulent uses of public funds becoming more common over the last few years, Walz says Minnesota has hired more auditors, investigators, and law enforcement, and will be continuing to pursue the issue. This wasn't the first time we've heard about this problem in Minnesota particularly. There was [reporting in the summer of 2024](https://www.mprnews.org/episode/2024/09/24/autism-centers-across-minnesota-leads-to-an-fbi-investigation-into-fraud-and-neglegence) about the FBI investigating Medicaid fraud, and it's not the first time the topic has come up. It was quickly promoted by right-wing media figures like Elon Musk and JD Vance, turning it into a viral hit overnight. His video didn't find or uncover anything new or interesting. In the first part of the video, he personally visits a few locations listed as offering childcare services, notes that he doesn't see any children around *at that moment*, and thus concludes any money they've received must've been fraudulent, adding it to a "fraud counter" at the bottom of the screen. When a woman at one location closed a door, not wanting to talk to him, he added a caption labelling her a "Somali fraudster," despite there being yet no evidence or indication that any of the people he encounters on his brief visit to these places had done anything wrong. This is the tone of the video in general: a lack of any real evidence, but a presumption of guilt nonetheless. Near the start of the video, Nick introduces an older man known simply as **David**, describing him as somebody "who's been doing his own investigation for years now" --- though we're given no indication he has any particular experience, credentials, or insight into the matter beyond having made a few Google searches. As Nick and David drive around town, David many times brings up these facilities being "licensed for" a certain number of children (80, 120, 40, and 74, varying by location). He seems to think that if a day care is licensed to care for up to 40 children, this means anybody must be able to stop by at any time and see exactly 40 children in waiting. He hopes the audience will believe it's suspicious that this was not his experience. How do we know how much money they've received? David has some sheets of paper that he claims show the amounts given. Where did he get this paper? Who knows. Sources. The video doesn't get into it. It turns out Demuth and her caucus likely sent David these pages for the purpose of getting him to make this video. The more we learn about the situation, the less hopeful one feels these details are going to turn out to be accurate, and as of right now we have no way of confirming any of it. He claims that many of these small operations received millions each. If true, that would seem oddly high and may raise some questions, though it wouldn't show these aren't real day cares, that they don't actually supervise children, or that the employees working at them had participated in any wrongdoing. At one location, they show up while the facility is closed, which they know but try to hide from the audience, blurring details in the background. A man approaches them curiously and says he used to work at this building in the past. David asks him more questions ("where are the children?"), but the audience doesn't get to hear how he answers. Like every confrontation in the video, things are selectively edited with frequent cuts to maintain a certain tone, and then we move on before getting to the bottom of anything. At another location, the people on site clearly don't want to talk to three people (Nick, David, and a cameraman following them around) showing up to ask them leading questions. One woman asks them why they have to explain theirselves, and expresses that she felt scared about the confrontational nature of the trio's presence. Nick is able to briefly peer inside one of the buildings as somebody opens a door to speak with him, and he says that he saw a child. With that, he and David leave --- undeterred, still believing whoever runs this facility is a criminal, and adding all money they supposedly received to their fraud counter. Though it's not the central point of the video, right before an interlude to promote a paid sponsor's product, Nick draws our attention to the **flag of Minnesota**, which, he says, was recently changed and now resembles the flag of Somalia, as they're both blue and both feature a star. Though Nick is careful with his words here, and doesn't make any claim other than pointing out the coincidence, he's deliberately giving fuel to a now-popular online claim, which is that it's *not* a mere coincidence: the flag was changed with the intention of making it look like the Somali flag, and this is an expression of the power and influence of the Somali community, making Minnesota their own. !images src:media/flag-somalia.png | caption:Flag of Somalia (1954) src:media/flag-minnesota.png | caption:Flag of Minnesota (2024) src:media/flag-minnesota-2024.png | caption:Minnesota's previous flag (1957--1983) src:media/flag-minnesota-flags-commission.png | caption:Other suggested flag designs (2023) !images src:media/minnesota-shape.png | caption:The shape of Minnesota The shape on the left side of Minnesota's new flag is intended to represent the geographical shape of the state on a map. The star, meanwhile, came from the fact Minnesota has long been called the North Star state. When the government was accepting public submissions for the redesign their flag and seal in 2023, [over 70 percent of submissions (1,785 of 2,527) featured the North Star in some way](media/2023-state-emblem-redesign-commission-report.pdf). And the state's motto, adopted in 1861, is *l'Étoile du Nord*, meaning the North Star (the only U.S. state motto given in French). The older Minnesota flag, being replaced, also featured a prominent star (around the outside of the seal) over a blue background. To say that Minnesota's flag was based on the Somali flag would be simply unfounded (there's no evidence suggesting anybody involved in the process had this intention in mind), but we also have a clear, obvious explanation for where the modern design came from historically: an amalgamation of all the previous design concepts. Moreover, it's questionable how one thinks the Somali community would be so influential a group that they would effect something like changing a flag to suit their own purposes. They're about one percent of the state's population, and poorer than average. Returning to their fraud-hunt, David says the Somalis have 14 different home healthcare companies in the same building. This is related to providers defrauding Medicaid, which Gov. Walz also mentioned in his article. That is, broadly speaking, a real problem. What does Nick Shirley have to contribute? He walks into one of these buildings and shows us that multiple different healthcare-related companies have office space here, and seems to find that puzzling. Nick then wanders the halls for a few minutes, recording the doors of each office. "This is the strangest place that I've ever been to get healthcare," he reflects aloud --- without even attempting to explain, or even understand for himself, why it might be "weird" for multiple small companies to operate in the same large building that's renting commercial office space. !images src:media/shirley-companies-listing.png | alt:Shirley looking at companies in some building src:media/receptionist.png | alt:An unknown receptionist whom Shirley confronts Nick eventually wanders into one of the rooms and asks a receptionist, "Who can we talk to about getting the best healthcare?" David answers: "Every year, I would say 50 to 60 million, at least." As far as the audience can tell, these numbers are picked from a hat. They've demonstrated nothing. The video goes on, but each encounter is similarly pointless. They go to a building, accuse the people there of fraud, the people there don't want to talk to them, and they conclude these people must be fraudsters and leave, triumphantly. The video is an example of a new type of "influencer journalism." Nick had already made up his mind before going out and visiting any location, before talking to anybody, before reviewing any numbers or documents. He and David were happy to make accusations and ask leading questions, but ultimately had no interest in getting to the truth of the matter, portraying anybody involved fairly or accurately, or informing their audience about what's going on or why.
