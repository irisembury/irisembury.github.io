--- title: Thoughts on India and Indians date: 2025-10-24 mirrors: substack:177071182, tumblr:798351257128615936 --- There's been a lot of racism directed at Indians recently. Indians have been one of the main immigrant group Canada has been receiving over the last decade, so now anybody who wants to translate their feelings about immigration into racism will often pick Indians as their main target. It's been increasingly over and uncomfortable to witness. I remember, when I was younger, that if you mentioned an Indian immigrant, people would likely think of a doctor, pharmacist, or engineer. They were a "model minority" for a time. What changed? The number of them coming in suddenly and massively increased, and the immigrant pool started skewing younger and toward students rather than people with established skills and careers. Today Indians are associated with low-skill positions like driving delivery and working retail. Earlier this year there was disagreement on the American right about immigration policy regarding a classification called the H-1B visa. This triggered another enormous flare-up of anti-Indian racism online of a different sort from what's most common in Canada, where instead of being low-class wage earns Indian workers are seen as competitors in the American tech sector who come in to undercut white people working as software devs and the like. If you browsed social media during this time you could hardly avoid white people talking about how much they hated Indians, making jokes about curry and tech support, mocking stereotypes about Indian accents and being bad at flirting. It became even more generalized online when there's no specific target, but Indians or Indian society in the abstract. The amount of vitriol now directed at what are effectively innocent people has only had the opposite effect on me personally, making me more curious to learn and understand more about India and the lives of these people who have been moving into our communities recently. The first thing I try to emphasize now is that it's rather crazy we stereotype or generalize Indians at all considering what a broad group of people the term refers to. India is an enormous place and very internally diverse. Its population is about twice that of Europe, over four times that of the United States. Its total land area is smaller, but their land is still larger than people seem to realize: it stretches from London to Moscow, from Oslo to Athens. It's taller than the U.S. north to south, and driving across it would be like driving from New York to Los Vegas. And this is only to speak of the present-day Indian borders, not counting Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka, all former Indian territory. To think of everybody from India altogether as a single group would be like meeting a few immigrants from Spain and using them as your model for understanding all of Europe from Russia to Greece to Portugal. !images src:media/30-02.png | alt:India overlayed onto Europe (from www.thetruesize.com) src:media/30-01.png | alt:India overlayed onto North America (from www.thetruesize.com) Indians also don't have a common language. It technically has hundreds, though only a dozen or so have speakers numbering more than one percent overall. Nonetheless, there is no single Indian language much less a singular Indian accent. They all sound very different from one another. The most popular language, Hindi, is only spoken by about a quarter of the population. !images src:media/india-languages.png | alt:The majority languages spoken across India India is very religiously diverse, both in quantity of religions and in how different they are from one another. In Europe and America, religious diversity is mostly just different flavours of Christianity. India has been the birthplace of four major world religious (Buddhism, Hinduism, Sikhism, and Jainism) and Indian society was impacted significantly by Islam after Muslims came from the west in the 13th century. For hundreds of years India was a land of religious pluralism where Hindus, Muslims, and other groups worked alongside one another in government and the emperor patronized multiple religions. Today, even after Muslim regions were split into Pakistan in the west and Bangladesh in the east, India proper still has over 200 million Muslims, making it one of the top countries in the world by Muslim population (which is still only 15 percent of their 1.4 billion people). Sikhism, whose followers are known for their beards and turbans, is followed by only about one percent of the country's population, but they're well-represented in the West because they've emigrated at such high rates. In Canada roughly a third of Indian immigrants have been Sikhs. ## Indian history .Not all the way back to bronze age, but about how things were before the British arrived. In 1206, the Delhi Sultanate was established by Muslim conquerors in northern India. This meant the territory ruled by the sultan from his seat in their capital of Delhi. The naming format is similar to "the Roman Empire," meaning the territory ruled by the emperor from his seat in the city of Rome. This state grew and shank over the years until, while on the decline, the city of Delhi was conquered by **Babur** in 1526. This is considered the beginning of the Mughal Empire, with Babur as the first Mughal emperor and all subsequent emperors his kin. The state Babur started would eventually consume nearly the whole Indian subcontinent, and was for a brief time the wealthiest place on Earth. !images float src: media/indus-river.png |caption:Location of the Indus river The residents of the empire referred to it as **Hindustan**. This comes from the Sanskrit term for the Indus river, *Sindhu*, which runs from western China through modern Pakistan into the Arabian sea (we associate this with the historical Indus Valley Civilization which existed there from 3300 to 1300 years before Christ). The name *Sindhu* morphed through Persian to *Hindu* by the Mughal period, then later morphed again to *India* through Greek and Latin by the time it reached Europeans. As such both names (India and Hindustan) literally mean "land of Indus." (The religion we now know as Hinduism was not yet called that and would only be named such in the 19th century, to mean "the beliefs of the people from the land of Indus.") In the 15th century, Europeans had some concept of India --- despite that none of them had ever been there, meaning nobody knew exactly where it was or what it looked like --- because it was possible to buy Indian-origin goods from Arab traders. To them, "India" was a term for a faraway land in the east that produced fabric, spices, and gemstones. In practice it could refer to anything south of China. What we today call the Indian subcontinent was referred to as India Major. This is why the countries and islands of southeast Asia became known as the East Indies, as they were all grouped in as part of that same general civilization. (The West Indies were so called because when Europeans first landed there, they believed they were on uncharted islands east of Japan that would eventually meet up with the East Indies should they continue west, as one big archipelago.) The term "Mughal" comes from the Persian for Mongol, as Babur was a descendant of Genghis Khan, though Babur did not call himself this and used "Moghul" as a term for nomadic tribes unlike himself. The title in its current sense was only popularized after European traders and diplomats returned home from their meeting with Jahangir in 1609 referring to him as "the Great Mogul," intending to show respect for his proud imperial ancestry and to convey the immense wealth and power the Hindustan empire represented. At the time, Jahangir would have called himself the Padshah or the Shahinshah (Persian for "king of kings," roughly meaning an emperor). Word of an empire ruled by the Great Mogul quickly spread across Europe and soon the name Mughal Empire was formalized and stuck. // Babur (1526--1530) Akbar (1556--1605) Jahangir (1605--1627) Shah Jahan (1628--1658) Aurangzeb (1658--1707) The Mughal Emperor known as **Abkar** (r. 1556--1605) has a name you may recognize from the phrase *Allahu akbar*, Arabic for "god is great," as Akbar was only a title meaning *the great*. This is like how we know of the first Roman emperor primarily as Augustus, which was not his birth name but a title given to him later in life meaning *the great* or perhaps *the majestic*. It was near the end of Akbar's reign that British traders first arrived in India and the prospect of trade in the far east led to Elizabeth I signing a royal charter in 1600 to create the East India Company. The first Mughal Emperor to meet a European person was **Jahangir** (r. 1605--1627), son of Akbar, who in 1609 met British merchants at his court. The Britons came not as conquerors but as humble traders, nor could they have conquered anything if they wanted to. Britain was smaller, less wealthy, and these traders were far from home. They offered gifts and sought permission from Jahangir to build a trading outpost on his land. Jahangir agreed. What followed was a period of great peace, prosperity, and development in India. They led the world in manufacturing and industry. It was here that a lot of great Mughal architecture was built, including [the Taj Mahal](media/taj-mahal.png), built in the mid-17th century on the order of Shah Jahan (r. 1628--1658). The grandest emperor was **Aurangzeb** (r. 1658--1707). After his death, Hindustan began a period of unravelling and fracture of imperial authority akin to the fall of Rome. This created the opportunity for new alliances to appear. It had by then been over a century since British traders began operating in the region, and French traders had permanent settlements there as well. In the decades that followed, many Indian governors and princes would choose to ally with British and French officers against other Indian states. There was not a strong nativist divide by which Europeans were foreigners nor the Indians united. Things were more local and personal. The East India Company gradually expanded its influence and outmaneuvered other powers, taking over the subcontinent one step at a time. The British parliament eventually passed the *Government of India Act 1833* extending the Company's charter for another 20 years, but also restructuring it to act more as an administrative entity rather than a primarily commercial enterprise. Following discontent with the Company's rule (see the Indian Rebellion of 1857), Britain passed the *Government of India Act 1858*, officially abolishing the Company entirely and transferring all control to the Crown, officially designating India part of the British Empire. This became known as the British Raj (from *raj*, the Hindi term for *rule*). It's unjust that we now forget India's great history, the heights to which it climbed, and the fact we once looked up to them, a history that is humbling to reflect upon. At first contact, we were the smaller power humbling approaching them, and we took advantage of the opportunity made by their natural downfall. Europe did eclipse the rest of the world after the Industrial Revolution, but this is no fault of anybody else, and we should not assume that other peoples could not have made similar advancements eventually, nor that our position of economic and political superiority is permanent. ## British Raj Europe was significantly affected by Britain's imperial relationship with India. Now as its rulers, Britons continued purchasing Indian goods like wheat, jute, tea, rice, cotton, leather, and dyes. There was mutual interest in each other's culture. Indian writers and artists travelled to England and vice versa. Indians learned about Christianity, and there were many voluntary converts. During this time, there were also many words from numerous Indian languages that came to English as loanwords, some of which you've undoubtedly used but wouldn't have guessed. A select list: mantra, guru, avatar, karma, khaki, bungalow, cot (portal bed), bandana, pundit, punch (as in fruit punch), thug (from *thugee*, the name of robber-gangs who attacked travellers), loot (as in to steal), tank (both as in a tank of a water and later military tanks), and shampoo. There was chauvinism on the part of some Britons who viewed their culture as better than that of India. The more progressive Whigs in Britain were often most guilty of this due to their view of social progress and their belief that Enlightenment thought put them ahead of the rest of the world. Thomas Macaulay, a British politician who played a major role in India's education policy, infamously wrote that a single bookshelf of a European library was worth more than all the literature in India, Asia, and Arabia put together. This feeling of superiority was used in part to justify British rule, to explain why Britain was ruling over a country on the other side of the planet. Britain had a self-image of an enlightened country that would deliver good governance alongside the saving faith of Christianity. If anything Britons were doing the rest of the world a favour. To fulfil that narrative, we had to believe India would be terribly mismanaged without Britain, and one way this was achieved was through the outlawing of certain types of barbarism, most memorably a practice called sati. When a married man died, we throw his body onto a funeral pyre, and sati was a term for when we throw his wife on there too, burning her alive alongside him. Why would any culture do something like this? It sounds obviously antithetical to any sort of real community. The answer is it may have only been done in special circumstances as a sort of ritual. It may have been voluntary but done when women had very few other options in life after her husband was gone. Perhaps to die with him was heralded as a form of romantic and honourable suicide, which was venerated culturally. And perhaps it wasn't even really a thing in the way we understand it, and we just mistranslated some old texts that had something to do with widows and funeral pyres. There is debate today about to what extent sati ever really happened and for what reason. Importantly, at the time Britain officially banned it, no European had ever actually witnessed sati happening. It was something we heard rumours about, then proudly declared would not be allowed going forward. This was presented to the British public as an example of the moral deficit of the Indian population (they burn people alive!) and a moral victory by the British, a demonstration of our superiority. The legacy of this idea has lived on in people's minds to this day. Indeed, you may see sati occasionally brought up online by people whose goal is attacking modern India, hundreds of years later. In the intervening period, religious tension in India grew, partly resulting from British policy meant to pit groups against each other. Instead of uniting against the British, the smaller Muslim population viewed Hindus as the main threat to their interests and cozied up with the British government, who would protect them from the Hindu majority. As time went by and the world economic system moved toward free trade between nations, Britain was one of many countries that found the benefit of maintaining large empires was dwindling, whereas the cost of keeping colonies under your thumb was only getting higher. Around the world people were becoming wealthier, more educated, growing discontent with being ruled from afar, and more interested in local autonomy. Even if opposition to colonial rule could be stopped --- and in the beginning, it was --- eventually it would be so expensive to continually stamp out dissent that it simply wasn't worth it anymore. By the 1900s Britain was at peace with the reality that their empire was not going to last indefinitely and shifted toward building diplomatic ties, developing what we now know as the British Commonwealth. In India, the move toward independence was led by Mohandas Gandhi, whose strategy of nonviolent resistance the British were unable to put down without sacrificing all semblance of moral superiority and comfort. This had an impact on how we in the West see protest and political change. Gandhi was admired for his efforts and methods and directly cited as an influence on the U.S. civil rights movement decades later. His spiritual influence on the direction of India notwithstanding, Gandhi was never directly involved in politics. He had no impact on law, government, or India's foreign relations. To understand that side of things the person to look to is **Jawaharlal Nehru** (1889--1964). Nehru studied law in England, attended Cambridge, and travelled across Europe before returning to India and becoming involved in politics in the 1920s. In 1947 he became India's first Prime Minister after the country became independence, a position he held until his death in 1964. Nehru was fiercely committed to the development of democratic norms, parliamentary procedure, and independence courts. He, more than anybody else, moulded the Indian Republic as we know it today. During the process of negotiating its independence, India also underwent partition. The Muslim population, led by **Muhammad Ali Jinnah**, believed the Muslim population would become marginalized in a Hindu-majority country after the British left. For their own sake, Jinnah believed the Muslims ought to declare independence, naming their proposed new country *Pakistan*. The name had multiple meanings, most notably deriving from *pāk*, Persian for pure, thus "land of the pure and clean." Jinnah died in 1948, only a year after his vicion became reality. He was widely admired even outside of the Muslim population and is now reversed in Pakistan as father of the nation. There were calls in India to pursue a similar path and to become an explicitly religious country, the Hindu version of Pakistan. Nehry rejected this. Though he was from a Hindu family, Nehru was not a religious man and believed India was home to many different peoples and faiths, including Muslims. While their new neighbours declared their countries Islamic states, Nehru stood as a defender of secularism, humanism, and religious pluralism. Nehru and his party, called the Congress Party, were self-described socialists, but avoided aligning too strongly with either side during the Cold War. This may be why people in the West don't associate India with socialism, despite socialism being an express goal of their government for decades. Nehru didn't agree with the Communist approach that had taken over Russia and China. What he did believe in was national independence and democracy. Whereas Communist parties came to power by coup and were governed as one-party states, Nehru was always freely and fairly elected. Instead of taking the excuse that India wasn't ready for democracy (as most of its population was illiterate, living in rural villages with little to no knowledge of politics), Nehru sought to change these conditions, massively improving literacy rate and organizing groups to canvass across the country, village by village, educating thep eople about government, elections, and providing information about political parties and their platforms. This undertaking is not as celebrated as it should be, and it's why today India boasts being the world's largest democracy. After independence there was conflict about what should be India's official language. At first they simply made it Hindi, the most popular language and the one spoken in their capital, but this led to fierce conflict, including violent protests and riots in the southeastern Indian state of Tamil Nadu. The other linguistic groups, in this case Tamils, strongly opposed being required to learn Hindi. Interestingly, the compromise they eventually settled was to make English the second official language of politics alongside Hindi (Official Languages Act, 1963). This is still in effect today: the Indian House of Commons, called the Lok Sabha, is conducted in a combination of Hindi and English. ## Indira Gandhi The next big leader of India after Nehru was his daughter, Indira Gandhi (1917--1984, of no relation to Mahatma Gandhi). She first became Prime Minister in 1966. Now largely associated with Margaret Thatcher, the nickname "Iron Lady" was first a term applied to Indira. It was around the beginning of her tenure that India went through a Green revolution, as high-yield variety seeds and mechanized farming equipment massively increased Indian agricultural output, changing the country's economic landscape. India had to balance economic interests with their goal of sovereignty and self-sufficiency, which a modernizing economy appeared to threaten. The world economy was transitioning alongside the neoliberal ideas of the 1970s and 80s; foreign companies were becoming interested in Indian agriculture, and control of land and capital began accumulating to the detriment of the small farmer (though this was still limited at the time as India had been very protectionist for decades). In 1984, Indira was assassinated. There were Sikh separatists wanted by the Indian government who hid out in the golden temple, believing it would be off-limits due to its cultural significance, being a holy site to the Sikh religion --- but Indira Gandhi signed the order to have the army attack it to apprehend them (Operation Blue Star). Indian forces attacked various other Sikh temples across the region. It turned out the Sikhs were armed and fought back and things escalated into a much worse conflict than anticipated. The attack gravely offended Sikhs everywhere --- including two Sikh men who worked as bodyguards for Indira, who shot her in retaliation for the attack. They were both caught and swiftly executed. In the years after Indira Gandhi's death, India would significantly change course. They embraced economic liberalization more fully, started building an urban middle class, and invested significantly in education, especially in the computer technology sector --- in a way, the origin of the "Indian tech support" stereotype that Americans know today. In economic terms, this change in strategy was a big success. # India today and going forward !images float src:media/india-china-population.png | caption:India and China population comparison India's population in 1900 was about 400 million. It only exploded to over a billion in the last hundred years. At some point over the last century, population growth outpaced infrastructure development, and as a result many Indian homes lacked toilets, among other things. This has resulted in a stereotype in the West about Indians and public defecation --- but this is also a problem in many other developing countries, like in impoverished regions in China, Africa, and South America, and even happens in some American cities. There isn't a reasonable justification for why India is especially associated with this problem. In recent years, India overtook China to be the world's most populous country. And unlike China, it hasn't closed itself off from the rest of the world's internet. As their population becomes more internet-connected, your internet experience will become affected by the presence of Indian users (in a way it's not affected by Chinese users, who are hidden away on their own websites, can't access any of ours, and are less likely to speak English). For humanity, this is a great relief. The ability to be so directly connected with people on the other side of the world is an incredible privilege that all generations before us could only dream of. And for world politics, the fact India has not gone down the path of China continues to be a great relief. India has some important similarities with China, most obviously being a developing economy with an extremely large population, but it's also more open to working with us and more comfortable with Western notions of freedom and democracy. It's possible for us to end up in conflict with India and push them away, but our goal should be the opposite. We should do what we can to maintain friendly relations. Many Indian people today are aware of the poor reputation that some of their people have given their country internationally. That is, they're self-aware of their own problems, embarrassed by some of their own countrymen, and they want to be better. Even if you've personally had some negative experiences with a few Indian immigrants, remember that you're talking about a population four times the size of America. I couldn't that say I hate Americans based only on having a bad experience with one, two, or even a dozen of them. There are many different types of Americans, different regions within America, different subcultures, different religions, different politics, different personalities, different ways of seeing the world. We have an easier time understanding this and perceiving the complexities of a thing we're familiar with, instead of one we're not.
