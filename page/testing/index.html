!meta title: A case for abortion date: 2026-02-13 The way I think about questions of legality in criminal law is to consider the most direct implications of the question. Do I endorse violence being used in an instance of noncompliance? That is, violence to deter an illegal action, or to compel a legal obligation. For abortion, imagine you're in a hospital and a doctor is about to perform an abortion on a woman who has requested it. A police officer is there and he defers to your judgment: should he forcibly intervene to stop this abortion from taking place? That is the question which discourse about abortion seeks to answer. And my answer is no. I believe it would be immoral to interfere and stop them. I am in favour of abortion being legal. Always. With no limitation and with no rationale necessary: if you are pregnant and you no longer wish to be, then you should be able to end your pregnancy at any time, with some considerations. The detail often mistaken is that to end a pregnancy does not necessitate the death of a fetus, as it may be able to survive on its own. If it can, then we have an obligation to take every step necessary to facilitate that life. If, on the other hand, the fetus is unable to live on its own, then alas, it will die. Unfortunate, but it can't be helped. ## What I am claiming, and what I'm not Before I go on, I should make it clear what I am claiming and what I'm not. Let's divide actions into good, bad, or neutral, morally speaking. Then divide good and bad actions by whether or not they're also legally mandated or prohibited, respectively. This isn't every possible concept one could use when discussing the morality or legality of actions, but it gives us the ones necessary to make the clarifications that I want to make. And you might think pregnancy is a virtue, but don't insist on it becoming a legal obligation. That is likewise acceptable. The only question that is crucial to interrogate is its legality. ## Terms clarification #### Pro-abortion In the United States, the common terms used to describe a person's attitude about abortion are pro-choice and pro-life, which both imply a framing biased toward their own perspective. I prefer to say pro-abortion and anti-abortion, and I mark myself as pro-abortion. I have also sometimes remarked that abortion is good, in a colloquial manner of speaking. This does not mean I want people to get abortions for the sake of doing so. To get an abortion is typically a grim decision, as unpleasant for the mother as for anybody else in society. If the total number of unwanted pregnancies were decreased to zero, and as such no abortions were ever performed, this would obviously be a good outcome. I'm in favour of abortion in the same way that I'm in favour of chemotherapy. This is a fitting analogy in numerous ways. To get chemotherapy is unpleasant, but it's what people choose because it's better than the alternative that is dying of cancer. And though chemotherapy is unpleasant, we prefer to live in a society where it's an option rather than one where it's not. And if I described myself as pro-chemotherapy, nobody would interpret this to mean I want people to take chemo drugs for no reason, or that I want more people to get cancer for the purpose of getting chemo, or that I'm going to throw chemo parties and talk about how great and wonderful it is to be on chemo. It would be easily understood that I'm pro-chemotherapy in the sense that I favour it being an option for people to use in the event they have cancer. Likewise I'm pro-abortion because I want it to be an option for women who no longer wish to be pregnant. #### Fetus In discourse about abortion, we often use the term "fetus" as an imprecise catch-all term for any prenatal human life. This is not intended to minimize or trivialize the value of what is being discussed, which is a human offspring on its way to being a baby, a stage of life that we adults all went through ourselves at some point. I was a fetus at some point. The fetus is a clump of cells, perhaps, in the most literal sense, but it has the potential to become so much more than that. I am not callous about what is at stake here. The term, so used, is also not technically correct. From conception, a human goes through numerous stages, typically given as zygote, then blastocyst, then embryo, then finally a fetus, before the end of the first trimester, as it's designated for most of pregnancy until birth. Though abortion can happen at any stage of pregnancy, and most happen during the embryonic stage, not the fetal stage, we're accustomed to talking about fetuses because it's during this stage that we perform the most controversial abortions, whose legality is most affected by policy disagreement. For my purposes here, the distinction between a fetus, an embryo, or any other prenatal life is unimportant, and any time I reference a fetus, you can read this as a general reference to any developing human prior to birth. # The right to security of person—or, The right of bodily integrity I'll begin with a few simple questions to establish common ground about our intuitions. Then I'll expand on and apply these intuitions through analogy. And finally I'll speak to some considerations typically raised in opposition to what I propose. And for now, I'll set aside any questions about the relative moral status of the fetus, because this is unnecessary for the argument I intend to pose. For the sake of discussion, and until otherwise specified, I shall consider the fetus to be a full person, of equal or comparable moral weight to an adult. As you may be able to infer from this note, if you're familiar with the existing philosophical discourse about abortion: the argument I make is not identical to, but is similar in approach, to those posed by Judith Jarvis Thomson in her influential 1971 essay, *A Defense of Abortion*. Why can the government not force you to take experimental medication, to accelerate the rate of medical research? It would be easy if you were a prisoner, especially. We could go to prisons, hold people down, inject them with things, and observe the results. And, if somebody is dying in a hospital bed from organ failure, why can the government not force somebody else to donate the organ tissue they need? We could find matching donors from government records and go to their houses, or find them at hospitals, or prisons. These are both measures that would save lives. I doubt anybody denies that they would save lives. And yet they are also intuitively horrific---things that we all know should not be accepted, but which might be featured in a novel describing a possible dystopian future. We don't accept policies like this because we must consider the possibility that we, ourselves, may be the ones who are subjected to them, and because they conflict with a rather fundamental desire that we all intuit, which is for our **security of person**, that is, the right to maintain the **integrity of our body** (integrity: the state of being whole, complete, unimpaired). This is sometimes mistakenly expressed as the right of bodily *autonomy*---but, insofar as autonomy refers to your ability to act freely or make your own decisions, there are valid reasons to disrupt a person's autonomy. We interfere with your autonomy by sending you to prison, for example. But to imprison a person, while a serious measure that we shouldn't take lightly, can be justified, and does not feel horrific in the same way that forcing somebody to undergo medical experimentation does. There is no justification for that. This likely also explains why no modern country engages in sterilization or torture as forms of punishment, and is likely related to why nearly all Western countries no longer engage in the death penalty for any crimes (the only exception being some parts of the United States). If that much is agreeable to you, then you should extend this approach and reasoning to the topic of pregnancy and abortion. Pregnancy is an arrangement whereby another being is dependent on your body for their survival, and the continuation of pregnancy causes you to incur definite bodily harm, as well as risk of further, more serious harm, and possibly even death in some cases. It's therefore something which, although perhaps virtuous, should be a voluntary relationship only. A person has no legal obligation to continue allowing a fetus to sustain itself at their expense, and if they express they no longer consent to the arrangement, we should allow them to detach theirselves, even if that means the fetus will die. This is because the fact we might save the fetus's life is not sufficient to justify imposing pregnancy onto a person unwilling---just as the fact that forced organ harvesting or forced medical experimentation would save lives was not sufficient to justify those practices either. From here, the argument in favour of abortion becomes whether imposing a restriction against abortion, or imposing a legal duty to continue pregnancy, can be positively justified. The opponents of abortion believe it can, while those of us who are in favour of abortion find their rationale unconvincing. It's not the most obvious thing in the world, and reasonable minds could disagree, but I find it unconvincing. I also find it worthwhile here to note the absence of division in our society about the wrongness of other measures that would impose onto our security of person. It was always and without much hesitation understood by all parties to be a line too far. The fact abortion remained for so long a glaring exception to these intuitions is most likely explained by how **the people making decisions about matters of law in the past were entirely men**, and women were not only disregarded but generally treated as though merely instrumental in the process of birthing children for their husbands. Relatedly, consider how husbands were historically considered to have a right to sex with their wives, with or without their wives' consent, and marital rape was therefore not a valid concent. It seems only natural that a society which doesn't grant women the right to decline sex in their own homes would not pause to consider whether they ought to have an even broader social right to security of person, and one that may interfere with men's ability to acquire children to boot. It has been said many times before, and I'll say it again: **if men could get pregnant, the right to abortion would be codified immediately, and this would not be a discussion**. # Is abortion an act of killing? The simple justification often given for restricting abortion is that abortion is the killing of another human, which is something there is a legitimate public interest in restricting. That's why it's against the law to commit murder. This is often posed as though obvious or common sense, a starting point that's expected to be uncontroversial. The implication is that people who are pro-abortion believe that abortion is an act of justified killing. I don't accept this framing. The question is not whether you can kill the fetus. The question is whether you can choose to not save the fetus or if you have an obligation to save it. This is worth understanding because there is a significant and undeniable moral difference between killing a person and not saving them. And this is why my position is unchanged if we imagine, in place of the fetus, a fully grown and healthy adult whose moral worth is beyond question. Suppose you're walking by a large, powerful river and you see a man has fallen in who calls for help. If you were to jump in and pull him out, saving him from drowning, this would be a great act and you would surely be applauded for it---but in making such an attempt you would be putting yourself at great risk of harm or even death, so it's understandable and defensible if you choose not to. Nobody would say that you've killed this man by not saving him. And you don't have a legal duty to jump in to make such an attempt. Though you chose, ostensibly, to let him die, you weren't his cause of death. And this is the same category, morally speaking, in which we find abortion. Some people object to this framing. They say allowing pregnancy to continue is the neutral path, or the path of inaction, and that getting an abortion is an active choice to interfere with pregnancy in such a way that causes the death of the fetus. It's therefore absurd of me, they say, to compare abortion with standing at a riverbank doing nothing, and to compare pregnancy with jumping into the river to save the drowning man. The action--inaction dynamic is reversed. But the cause of death in the case of abortion is more complicated than if you were to go outside and shoot a man with a gun. The fetus is unable to live on its own, unaided by the womb of its mother, because it hasn't yet developed the bodily systems necessary to do so (most urgently, the ability to inhale and process oxygen). This is the only reason it dies, not because we make some concerted effort to prevent it from surviving. In some abortions, performed after the first trimester of pregnancy, where a fetus has grown beyond a certain point, a doctor may deliberately kill the fetus before the procedure through lethal injection. This is done because, in those cases, we know the fetus cannot survive on its own and death would otherwise be certain. The alternative would be for the doctor to remove the fetus from its mother and allow it to immediately suffocate and die on a table in the operating room, in a way that's more drawn-out, and more traumatic for the mother and medical staff, than dying by lethal injection prior to evacuation. This, in particular, is what we may call an act of justified killing, because it seems kinder to all involved than letting the fetus die as it would otherwise. Think of how Jon Snow killed Mance Rayder with a bow and arrow, but only because Rayder was about to be burned to death. Snow didn't have the power to save Rayder's life, but he did have the ability to save him from the fate of being burned alive, which is a far worse death than an arrow through the heart. If this is what somebody is referring to and opposing when they speak of abortion being killing, they're free to argue for that alternative, in those cases, and insist that if the fetus is going to die anyway, doctors mustn't be allowed to use a lethal injection to make the death more pleasant for the mother and medical staff present. Why anybody would be motivated to make such a change, I don't know. But this is also beside the point. Even if this change were made and doctors were no longer allowed to use lethal injection for any abortion cases, the procedure would still be an abortion, and the fetus would still die, albeit more unpleasantly. And it would still be so that the procedure, in its nature, is not killing a fetus so much as it is choosing to let it die. To return to the analogy of the drowning man in a river: a fuller, more comprehensive analogy may be to imagine if there was a coil of rope nearby, that you've quickly hooked one end onto your waist and thrown the other into the river for the man to grab onto. But after he grabs the rope, you realize his weight combined with the force of the river is too much, and you fear being pulled in yourself, so you unhook the rope from your waist. Though unhooking the rope from your waist is an action, it only returns you to an initial state of inaction, prior to when you entered into an arrangement that you weren't compelled to enter. And here again, it's clear nobody would say you killed this man. We would say you didn't save him. Perhaps you began saving him, or made an attempt to save him, and then stopped, but you were not the cause of his death, as if you had walked up to him on the street and fired a gun at him. So this is more fully like what's happening when a person enters into an arrangement to allow a fetus to use their body to sustain itself (pregnancy) but then later withdraws their agreement from that arrangement (abortion), allowing the fetus to die due to its inability to save itself. If a fetus were capable of living on its own but then we inflicted upon it the condition that leads to its peril, then it could be said we're killing the fetus. Likewise, if the reason the man were drowning is because I threw a fishing net onto him that made him unable to swim, then we could say I was the one who killed him. But this isn't the case. It's not the fault of the mother nor the medical staff that the fetus doesn't yet have working lungs. It is the natural state of the fetus. It is by its nature something that cannot live on its own unless it remains attached to its mother. #### But isn't the fetus's dependence on its mother our fault? A problem with the drowning man analogy is that it begins with the man already in the river. How did he get there? Some people argue that insofar as pregnancy is like saving a drowning man, the mother, by getting pregnant, is like the one who pushed him into the river in the first place. And by doing so, she has justified us imposing onto her a legal duty to continue this pregnancy until completion. This is an interesting thought, but it may also expose the limits of the analogy. Let's suppose you did push a man into a river and he drowned. We wouldn't say the crime was you not jumping in afterwards to save him. The crime was pushing him into the river to begin with. Even if you did jump into the river and save him, it would still have been a crime that you pushed him in, and caused him to undergo this unpleasant and possibly traumatic event, where he was at risk of injury or death. What if you pushed him in and then failed to save him, despite your best effort? He didn't agree to roll those dice. So, what does this look like when cast back into speaking of pregnancy and abortion? It would not be the abortion that's comparable to pushing a man into a river, but getting pregnant to begin with. If a woman gets pregnant and then has an abortion, would you say her becoming pregnant was the immoral action? To become pregnant is, one could argue, to put the fetus into a situation of being at high risk of death or injury, and being dependent on others for survival. But of course, this is only because there was not a conscious being to offer or deny such agreement. It didn't exist yet. The fetus was not once an independent being whom we forced into a state of dependence. It came into the world that way. Its dependence is part of its nature, an inevitability, something the mother couldn't have prevented. So we cannot fault somebody for causing this dependence, unless you want to argue it's immoral to bring new life into the world at all. (If a moral system were to lead to such a conclusion, that alone would be dispositive evidence we should discard it.) To explore this aspect of abortion while keeping it analogous with the drowning man in the river, we may have to modify the latter by supposing it was indeed your fault the drowning man fell into the river, but that it was only by falling into this river the man was alive in the first place. Perhaps this is the holy river of spawning, the sacred location where a godlike being conjures new men, but only when a woman requests it do so. But at this point, most people would say the analogy has been tortured beyond being useful. There is not really anything else in life that mirrors abortion perfectly. There is always some way in which it's different from other scenarios, if you examine the comparison deeply enough or consider enough different facets of it. The purpose of invoking the drowning man in the river is to provide a simple way to understand the difference between killing and not saving, and to express my own view or attitude by analogy---as, despite the difference between the two scenarios that we've now identified, I'd still say that how I feel about one about the same as how I feel about the other. #### Is the mother tacitly agreeing to assume the responsibility of pregnancy by choosing to get pregnant? Earlier I said pregnancy should be a voluntary relationship, and that it would be immoral to force somebody to continue a pregnancy they no longer agree to. Some people counter this by arguing the mother has tacitly given her consent to the arrangement by choosing to get pregnant, and that, once given, this consent cannot be reasonably withdrawn. But I can't think of any other scenario where a person's consent to something which affects the integrity of their person could not be withdrawn after an initial commitment. If a person agreed to donate part of their liver but later decided they don't want to, for example, nobody would propose going to their house and forcing them to submit to the operation. Even though others may be left to die as a result, it's their choice to make. I also question whether it can be assumed that a mother has consented to pregnancy. If she has explicitly stated she's having sex for the purpose of becoming pregnant, then perhaps we could say this, but otherwise pregnancy is normally something that happens more or less randomly, and often despite some effort to prevent it. We also wouldn't have access to information about a person's state of mind at conception when she comes into a clinic and requests an abortion, so no policy could be based around this type of assumption. What some people intend to say, though, is that by merely having sex at all, a woman consents to any pregnancy that results because she understands that pregnancy is a possible outcome of sex. But this is not how anybody talks about consent in any other scenario. If a person understands there's a risk of somebody mugging them when they go out at night, that doesn't mean you'd say they consented to getting mugged. # When does virtue become obligation? What about a situation where you do have an obligation to save a person who's dying? Let's say you're walking outside one day and come across a large, industrial machine, say a garbage compactor, and you notice that a person has fallen inside. She calls out to you and explains her predicament: in a few minutes the machine will activate and crush her to death unless you save her. To do so, all you have to do is press the emergency stop button on the side of the machine. It's a large red button in plain sight, which will not only disarm the crushing mechanism but also allow her to open the side door and escape. In a scenario like this, one could make the argument that to press the button would not only be a good action, but that to *not* press the button would be indefensible, a moral failing or defect on your part. It feels like we have a duty to act, and that it might be right to punish somebody for not acting. This could be compared with abortion if you believe a pregnant person has a moral duty to carry through with pregnancy in order to save the life of her fetus from the demise it will face otherwise. The fetus is like a person trapped in a machine and you have an obligation to save them. This prompts us to imagine two different categories of morally good actions. First, those which are good but not necessary, and which instead represent you going above and beyond. Though you would be applauded for these actions, you would not be condemned for not doing them. This is sometimes called "supererogation". And second, actions which are good of you and which you have a duty or expectation to perform. If you fail to do these things, you might be condemned for that shortcoming. We could simply call this a moral duty. There isn't a general answer, as all people believe in some moral duties that should be mandated and others that shouldn't be. For the matter of letting somebody die: in some countries, like France, to do nothing to save a person dying in front of you might result in legal trouble, as you have some legal duty to help a person who's in imminent danger where there's no risk to your own safety to do so. In other countries, like the United States, you may find there's no legal obligation to do anything at all, even if you could've saved their life with the mere press of a button. Which country's approach is correct is a matter of opinion and both opinions have merit, but that isn't really the focus here. Even if we decided it can be a moral duty to save a person's life in the case of the garbage compactor, there is a clear distinction to be made between a scenario where saving a person can be done with the mere press of a button and one in which saving a person requires putting yourself in harm's way or taking on significant risk of injury or death. This difference exists in our intuition (as "common sense") as well as in law. Just as we cannot forbid self-defense because the law cannot reasonably compel a person to give up and allow theirselves to die, so we couldn't reasonably demand that you save another person's life at the expense of your own. The law cannot punish people for mere self-preservation. And abortion falls easily into the latter category. It is not a frivolous matter akin to pressing a button. The experience of being pregnant imposes great costs onto a pregnant person: pain and discomfort, fatigue, loss of mobility, and increased risk of numerous conditions and infections. The experience of labour, or giving birth, is likewise painful and risky, with many different complications that may arise. The complications from pregnancy or childbirth can result in the death of the mother in some cases. And even in the case of a successful pregnancy, the mother's body sustains permanent damage resulting from the changes it undergoes as part of the experience. # The personhood question Suppose we invent a machine that could remove a fetus from its mother easily, without risk or pain, at any stage in pregnancy from the moment of conception; and allow the fetus to live and grow in a medical chamber that fully simulates a real human uterus until maturity, at which time it can be removed, "born" into the world as a baby. If this were so, then for many people the issue of abortion would be put to rest: though some people may dislike the impersonal or artificial nature of such a machine and prefer natural pregnancy, any woman who wants an abortion may terminate her pregnancy if she so wishes provided she uses this machine to do so, because the fetus will be given a full and fair chance at life. The issue of the potential death of the fetus will have been resolved. However, one question would remain, which is: at what point, if not from conception, do we gain an obligation to respect the life of the fetus in the same way that we do other people? The way many people phrase this question is: at what point does a human gain legal personhood? This will affect how we answer other questions. Do we have an obligation to keep alive any fetus removed by this machine, no matter its stage of development? And, if a woman were to take a poison pill that killed the fetus inside her by inducing miscarriage, despite this machine being available to her, should this be a crime, akin to if not murder? I have conflicting thoughts here, which I'll share tenuously. Like many people, I find intuitive appeal in saying that a blastocyst that has not yet developed a brain or nervous system seems less important than a more fully developed human. A blastocyst doesn't even have a conscious experience of the world, so why does how you treat one matter more than how you treat a rock or a chair? Then again, it also feels wrong to say they have no value whatsoever and could simply be thrown in the trash without regard. Perhaps they have value *to us* without being moral patients theirselves, in the same way that I might value a tree I grew in my yard, and feel very sad if you were to cut it down---but without us considering the destruction of a tree to be immoral *to the tree* (though it may be immoral insofar as it's *my* tree and thus the destruction of my property), or to consider cutting down a tree to be murder. For me, though, this final question is not so important. It's possible that in the future, we will indeed choose to preserve every single prenatal human life we can, even from the zygotic stage. And perhaps we won't. I defer to the decision of generations future. # Recap There should be no legal restriction on abortion itself. This means no person should ever be forced to remain pregnant if they no longer consent to being pregnant. However, there should be a legal duty by medical staff to try and preserve the life of a fetus if they're able to. The term we use for a fetus that's capable of living on its own without access to its mother's uterus is **viable**. If a fetus is viable, then we have an obligation to it, but if a fetus is not viable, then it won't survive, try as might, so unfortunately it may be allowed to die as the unfortunate side-effect of the mother not choosing to continue allowing her body to be used in keeping it alive (just as, say, we allow people who have failing organs to die if nobody chooses to donate their organs to save them). That's how I think society should be. And to be clear: that is how a lot of places already are, including my country and much of the U.S. from what I understand. It's a very reasonable state of affairs.
