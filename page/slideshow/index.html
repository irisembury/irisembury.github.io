some questions to establish common ground - set aside questions of the relative moral status of fetus - fetus = full person - same moral weight to a full adult ### Consider: - Why not engage in forced **medical experimentation**? - Why not force people to **donate organs**? These measures would both **save lives**. Yet they are intuitively horrific. ### Why don't we accept policies like this? - We consider the possibility that *we* may one day be subjected to them - veil of ignorance (John Rawls) - They conflict with a fundamental desire we all share - security of person - bodily integrity ***What does "integrity" mean?*** - in this context, does not mean dignity/respect - **integrity** = whole, complete, unimpaired - "structural integrity" --- "Bodily autonomy" - There are legitimate reasons to restrict your autonomy - We restrict autonomy when we send you to prison - Imprisonment does not restrict your bodily integrity - No modern country engages in **sterilization** or **torture** as punishment - We have stopped practicing the death penalty !image-span 400 media/death-penalty-map.png --- *What is pregnancy?* - another being is dependent on your body for their survival - causes you **definite harm** (i.e. harm that is guaranteed or always happens) - and the **risk** of further, even more serious harm - possibly **death** This should be a **voluntary relationship** only The fact we **might** be able to save the fetus's life is not sufficient to justify imposing pregnancy onto somebody who doesn't agree - just as the fact **forced medical experimentation** - or **forced organ harvesting** was not enough to justify those practices either --- The discourse that remains is: ### How can you justify imposing pregnancy onto people? --- ## The absence of debate about the wrongness of these other things .(other things that infringe on your right to security of person) - Everybody obviously 'knew' that something like forced medical experiment was wrong - Abortion has been the one, glaring exception to this intuition *Why?* - Everybody making decisions about law in the past were men - Women seen as instrumental in making children - Men had a "right" to sex regardless of their wives' consent - "marital rape" not a valid concept - A society that doesn't even allow women to deny sex in their own home doesn't consider if women should have broader social rights ### If men could get pregnant, the right to abortion would be codified immediately --- # Is abortion an act of killing? - The simple anti-abortion argument: Abortion is killing a human being, ergo we should restrict it - akin to murder - This is expected to be agreeable, common ground. - Implication: pro-abortion people think abortion is killing, but an act of **justified killing** *I don't accept this.* The question is whether you can choose to **let the fetus die**, or to **not save** the fetus. Or if you have an obligation to save it---even at the expense of your own rights. !image-span media/nriver1.png - Suppose you see **a man drowning in a river** - If you jump in and save him, this is a great act you would be applauded for - But this puts yourself at great risk of injury or death, so it's understandable if you choose not to do this - If you don't save him, nobody would say you killed him by not saving him. **Not saving** isn't the same as **killing** ## Objections *Some people argue:* - Allowing pregnancy to continue is the neutral path, or path of inaction - **Abortion** is an **active choice** to *interfere* and *cause* the fetus to die - So it's absurd to compare **abortion** with standing by doing nothing (not saving) and to compare pregnancy with jumping in to save somebody (the implications of my argument above) - action vs. inaction is reversed - It's more like abortion is throwing a fishing net onto a swimmer to make him drown *The problem is...* - The **cause of death** in abortion is not so simple - The fetus is unable to live on its own - has not developed bodily systems necessary to do - it literally can't inhale/process oxygen - **This** is why the fetus dies---*not* because we make a concerted effort to prevent it from surviving --- In *some* abortions, a doctor may kill the fetus by lethal injection - **This** is an act of killing, but... - This is done because the fetus cannot survive on its own anyway - Its death is certain - The alternative: removing the fetus and letting it suffocate on a table in the OR - This is an act of "justified" killing - Is it not? --- *Let's consider* - Jon Snow killed Mance Rayder with a bow and arrow - Jon couldn't save Mance's life, but he saved Mance from being burned alive - This was a worse fate than an arrow through the heart **Do we condemn Jon as a murderer?** --- If this specific act (pre-abortion lethal injection) is what somebody opposes let them argue against it. But without it: - the procedure would **still be an abortion** (still removing fetus from its mother) - the fetus would **still die** We can distinguish between these two actions: 1. **performing an abortion** (removing fetus from its mother) - not an act of *killing* more than *allowing to die* 2. **lethal injection** *before* the abortion - it's not going to survive the abortion anyway - saving from worse fate So opposition to the lethal injection *is not opposition to abortion itself*. --- ### Returning to the analogy of the drowning man in the river !image-span media/rope.png - Imagine there were a coil of rope nearby - You connect the rope to yourself - Throw in the other end for the man to grab onto - **Letting go of the rope is an action, a decision, a thing you're choosing to do** - It wouldn't be okay for a mother to stop feeding her children and let them starve. - It seems reasonable to say we can have **obligations** to **preserve the lives** of people who are **dependent** on us. - This is *partially true* - but this is a different *type* of obligation - doesn't conflict with your **security of person** - it's possible to end this commitment - Some arguments ask us to imagine a **deserted island**, or a **cabin in a snowstorm**, i.e. a situation where we're isolated from society - but these questions all remove bodily integrity from the equation - **Doesn't the mother have a special relationship with the fetus that justifies a moral duty?** - It's her fault the fetus is in a state of dependence, as she's the one who put it into that scenario to begin with - A potential shortcoming with the drowning man analogy: it begins with him already in the river - How did he get there? - Insofar as pregnancy is like saving a drowning man, **the mother is like the one who pushed him into the river** - This justifies imposing a legal duty onto the mother to save him - But if you did push someone into a river and they drowned... - We wouldn't say the crime was you *not* saving him. **The crime was pushing him in to begin with**. - Are we saying... - if a woman gets pregnant and then has an abortion, - the crime is not the expulsion and death of the fetus, but getting pregnant? - To get pregnant is to impose a condition of risk and dependence onto the fetus - This arguments causes us to consider that **pregnancy is immoral** - To keep using the drowning man analogy... - We would have to say it's your fault the man is in the river, but it's only by falling into the river he could exist to begin with - There is nothing else in life that mirrors abortion perfectly - The drowning man analogy is not meant to be identical to abortion - It just provides a simple way to understand killing vs. not saving - it does make this point effectively - it also does articulate my attitude about abortion - **Is the mother not giving implied consent by choosing to get pregnant?** - made as a retort to using language like *voluntary* or *consensual* - A mother has consented by *choosing* to get pregnant - once given, this consent cannot be reasonably withdrawn - Analogy: - Imagine somebody commits to **flying a plane** for passengers - This effectively means you **consent** to a **commitment** to fly the plane until landing it safely - You couldn't just "withdraw consent" midway through the flight - We could use force to make them continue flying the plane - The problem is... - You have removed your right to bodily integrity from the equation - Analogies: - **Driving a car** entails some risk of a car accident - does this mean you consented to a car accident? - **Going out at night** involves some risk of getting mugged - Does this mean if somebody mugs you, that you consented to it? --- --- Let's say somebody agrees abortion is not "killing the fetus". They can still argue: **why don't you have an obligation to save the fetus?** # When does virtue become (legal) obligation? - Imagine a person is trapped in a big machine. - It's going to crush them to death unless you save them - you just need to press the emergency stop button on the side of the machine !image-span media/compactor.png media/stop-button.png - Not only is pressing this button **good**, but to **not** press the button seems very **bad**. - It is **immoral** to **not** press the button - Maybe it should even be a legal obligation - This could be analogous with pregnancy - there's a human who is going to die unless you save them - it seems evil to not do so This leads to us thinking about two different types of good action: - things that are good, but not necessary - **supererogation** - things that are good, and expected of you---if you don't do these things, you will be criticized or condemned - **moral duty** ### I'll add these to the chart from earlier: !table this { margin: auto; } this td { border: 1px solid var(--grey-8) } this .col-1 { max-width: 400px; } this .col-2 { max-width: 200px; } this .row-1 td { background-color: var(--c-bg-cream); } this .row-2 .col-1, this .row-3 .col-1 { background-color: var(--c-bg-lightgreen); } this .row-2 .col-2, this .row-3 .col-2 { background-color: var(--c-bg-lightred); } this .row-3 td { filter: brightness(0.9) saturate(200%) } **neutral** behaviour that isn't meaningfully good or bad .e.g. drinking a glass of water **virtue** (morally good) good, but not legally required .e.g. donating to charity
