!images src:media/carney-2025.png In the 2025 general election, the Liberal Party [won 169 House seats](https://newsinteractives.cbc.ca/elections/federal/2025/results/) of the 172 needed for a majority. For being the largest party in the House, the Liberal Party carried on as the governing party, as they have been since 2015, and Mark Carney stayed on as Prime Minister. Fast-forward to earlier this week, 13 April 2026, and you may have seen headlines announcing the Liberals [have now secured a majority](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/livestory/byelections-terrebonne-university-rosedale-scarborough-southwest-9.7162168), reaching 174 seats, two over the majority threshold, after winning three byelections. In University---Rosedale, a new MP had to be elected after Chrystia Freeland, its Liberal MP, [resigned in January](https://globalnews.ca/news/11606120/chrystia-freeland-resignation-liberal-mp/), and the result was a victory for the new Liberal candidate, [Danielle Martin](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/federal-byelection-danielle-martin-toronto-9.7162840). In Scarborough Southwest, a new MP had to be elected after Bill Blair, its Liberal MP, [resigned in February](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/bill-blair-appointment-high-commissioner-mp-9.7071166), and the result was a victory for the new Liberal candidate, [Doly Begum](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/federal-byelection-toronto-9.7162785). In Terrebonne, the Liberal candidate Tatiana Auguste was elected last year, but her very close and disputed victory [was annulled by courts](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c9q57y4wly7o) in February and ordered to be redone, resulting, this time around, in [a more firm, uncontested win](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/montreal/terrebonne-byelection-results-9.7158119) and her restoration as MP. You may notice these byelections did not increase the Liberal Party's total members from their 2025 general results. They didn't have a majority in late 2025, before Freeland or Blair stepped down and before the Terrebonne annulment, when Auguste was a serving member. The news of the week was byelections, but the real game-changer that moved the Liberals from a minority to majority government has been **floor crossing**, absorbing four members from the Conservative Party ([Chris d'Entremont](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/conservative-mp-out-of-caucus-chris-dentremont-9.6966836), [Michael Ma](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cy5ggqkl44lo), [Matt Jeneroux](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/jeneroux-joins-liberals-9.7095322), and [Marilyn Gladu](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c98k9wd254xo)) and one from the New Democrats ([Lori Idlout](https://globalnews.ca/news/11725093/ndp-mp-lori-idlout-crosses-floor-liberals/)). The results only weren't immediate because they dropped these three seats during the same period, which, as of these byelection victories, they've now picked back up. Floor crossing, or changing your party affiliation while in office, whether to sit as an independent or to join any other party that accepts you, is not a new or untested phenomenon. It's well established, and not news to anybody, that MPs are free to do this at their own discretion, and the history of our democracy [has many examples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Canadian_politicians_who_have_crossed_the_floor) of floor crossings at the federal and provincial level. Indeed, it's something you can observe in most democracies around the world. However, five floor crossings in a seven month period is more than usual for federal politics, and it's the first time a government has moved from minority to majority through floor crossings, which has brought more focus and scrutiny on the practice lately than usual. It seems agreeable to say, at the very least, that for the Liberal Party to reach a majority by Conservative members changing party affiliation to Liberal after the jump doesn't feel quite as clean as if they were to seat a majority on election night simply by having their candidates win in a majority of ridings. I'm also sympathetic to anybody who supported a Conservative member with the hopes they would oppose and obstruct Carney's agenda to the best of their abilities, only for the member to decide they wanted to support and enable Carney's agenda instead. However, we all understand there is currently no guarantee a candidate you vote for doesn't go on to do anything you don't approve of. It's possible they will vote for legislation tabled by the opposing party, for instance, if they find the contents of the bill reasonable. And indeed we depend on this being the case, at least some of the time, when we seat a minority government. The essential or underlying question that's been posed by many people about floor crossings is, when we vote in an election, **are we voting for the person, or for the party?** And is that how it ought to be? Traditionally, and by how elections are currently organized and presented to voters, the question has a correct answer: the person. It would be controversial to suggest that because the voters of University---Rosedale chose Chrystia Freeland in 2025 that the Liberal Party owns those votes and so, when Freeland steps down, they don't need to hold a byelection to appoint somebody new. The Liberal Party could put anybody they want in that seat, and they would represent the voters of that riding all the same because anybody from the Liberal Party will suffice. This is how it works in some countries. In Spain, for example, voters explicitly only vote for a party, with no say in which specific politicians the party then chooses to put in each seat. This is called a closed list system. Is this what anybody wants for Canada? It wouldn't be unthinkable, but one would have to argue for changing our system to achieve that. As it stands, members of Parliament, voted in by the constituents of their riding, are given great latitude to exercise their own discretion. They might do things you dislike, and you might criticize them for that, and if enough people agree with your criticism then it may hurt their ability to be reëlected. If joining the Liberals is indeed so unpopular in their riding, then perhaps the floor crossers won't be reëlected in the future. It's up to the MP to decide whether they want to take this risk, based on what they believe is right and what the people they're meant to be representing truly value. That isn't to say we couldn't choose to have a rule carving out a specific restriction against joining the caucus of another party, if we agree that seems like a step too far, an area where perhaps MPs are given *too much* latitude. If somebody were to make this argument, however, it should perhaps not be our current Conservative Party, whose case is complicated by the fact they benefited from floor crossings in the past, and, when there was a bill which sought to create a rule against it ([C-306 of the 41st Parliament](https://www.parl.ca/legisinfo/en/bill/41-1/c-306)), prescribing the remedy of forcing a member who joins another party to be automatically vacated and triggering a byelection, the Conservative majority voted against it (including Poilievre). Setting aside all optics and questions surrounding past behaviour, I'm skeptical of the proposal to restrict floor crossing. If I vote to elect a Liberal member and there's a Conservative minority government, it's possible my Liberal representative would choose to vote in favour of legislation proposed by the Conservatives. Would this be so much less of a betrayal than if they were to also change their party affiliation to Conservative? For MPs, the major difference is access to party caucus meetings. Each party organizes regular, confidential gatherings with their own members to discuss their ongoing agenda. It's here that members can have influence over decisions and the content of legislation, as well as insight into the strategy of leadership. A Liberal who commits to vote for a Conservative bill is a vote in the government's favour, but they don't get any increased voice or access while they remain a registered Liberal. This is primarily what draws people to change party status: they're expressing it would be a better use of their time to sit at the other party's table and sit in the other party's meetings. In terms of the function of government, this seems like a step up rather than a step down, a way for this member to have a seat at the table and be part of the process of what the government does. There have been instances in the past of controversial floor crossings, like David Emerson in 2006, but there have also been instances that appear to be reasonable, well intentioned, and not a betrayal or deceit of voters, such as the case of Chris d'Entremont earlier this year. Marilyn Gladu was recently criticized heavily online for joining the Liberals, including by people on the left who have been characterizing her as a staunch social conservative who doesn't belong. !images src:media/carney-and-gladu.png | caption:Carney meeting with Gladu after she joined the Liberals But Gladu's incompatibility with the values of the Liberal Party has being overstated. In 2020, as candidate for the Conservative leadership race, she [claimed to be](https://x.com/ctvqp/status/1216399825215860737) for the legality of abortion, supported the rights of the LGBT community, and, though she did [vote against Bill C-6](https://www.justice.gc.ca/eng/csj-sjc/pl/charter-charte/c6b.html) in 2020, which outlawed conversion therapy, she was clear in saying she was against conversion therapy but opposed the wording of the bill for not providing a sufficiently narrow definition of conversion therapy (she was the one who [presented a petition to the House](https://www.ourcommons.ca/petitions/en/Petition/Details?Petition=432-00378) asking for the government to amend the bill to narrow the definition, which it did not do). On the first day of the Liberal National Convention, Gladu [told reporters](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/liberal-convention-montreal-9.7156845) "I will vote with the government. I will protect the rights and freedoms of women to choose, for people to be who they are and love who they love," and reminded us that she has "been consistent when I said that women need to have the right to choose." Some people still complain about Gladu downplaying the pandemic and promoting some bad science in 2020 (in [a radio interview](https://sarnianewstoday.ca/news/2020/04/15/sarnia-lambton-mp-says-need-get-back-work), she said "more people are dying from cancer and smoking and we don’t shut down the economy for that" and described how "in the United States, they've been successful with the treatment of hydroxychloroquine, azithromycin and zinc sulphate"). However, it deserves to be noted any time this is brought up that Gladu [apologized about this in 2021](https://globalnews.ca/news/8360423/covid-vaccine-conservative-party-otoole/), saying that on reflection it was inappropriate and dangerous to share "misinformation about the severity of COVID-19 and the safety and efficacy of vaccines," and encouraging people in her riding to get vaccinated. Carney, speaking about some of the recent floor crossings, [explained](https://globalnews.ca/news/11771261/marilyn-gladu-liberals-abortion-carney/) "there's a general sense of many in this country, how crucial this time is and how important it is to work together to build this country." Gladu, likewise, explained that Canadians "need a serious leader who can address the uncertainty that has arrived due to the unjustified American tariffs. We need a global leader with a plan to make a more resilient Canada, a stronger Canada, a more self-reliant Canada, for this critical moment. And that man is our Prime Minister, Mark Carney." Indeed, the tone of the recent floor crossings has been a genuine interest in bipartisan cooperation, at a time when many people are both dissatisfied with the leadership direction of the Conservative Party and of the perception that Mark Carney has been more moderate than his predecessor Justin Trudeau, moving the Liberals closer to the centre and pursuing a broad national agenda that many Conservative MPs are willing to get behind. Over the last year of Liberal governance, Carney has been focused on diplomacy, infrastructure, government reform, and economic policy. He's increased Canadian military spending, reduced interprovincial trade barriers, and wants to expedite some national construction projects. He slashed unpopular carbon and gas taxes, as the Conservative Party had been campaigning for. Even if a Conservative MP were personally opposed to abortion, let's say, could this not be overlooked if they wanted to support Carney's broader agenda, if both parties agree that issue doesn't need to be dug up and addressed at a time like this?
