--- title: Allende and Pinochet date: 2026-08-09 mirrors: substack:210515116,medium:1b55782f90e9 --- !images float src: media/salvador-allende.png | caption: Salvador Allende **Salvador Allende** (*ay-unn-day*) was born in 1908 in Santiago, the capital city of Chile. After going to medical school, by his 20s he became involved in politics. In 1933, when he was 25, Allende was a founding member of the Socialist Party of Chile. In 1937, at age 29, he was elected to the Chamber of Deputies, the lower house of the Chilean congress. In 1939, he served as health minister for Chilean president Pedro Aguirre Cerda, resigning in 1942. In 1945 he was elected back into the Chilean congress as a senator, and he remained a senator until 1970, age 62, representing various constituencies along the way. He also participated as a presidential candidate in numerous elections (in 1952, 1958, and 1964) to represent his socialist party, returning to the senate after each unsuccessful attempt. By that 1970 election, Chile had become a battleground for the great powers in the Cold War. The 1959 Cuban revolution gave the USSR a foothold in the Western hemisphere in Fidel Castro, who now ruled Cuba as a Soviet-friendly dictatorship, and they were hungry for more. The U.S. got involved and before long both sides had agents in the country funnelling resources toward their favoured parties. The CIA paid to run anti-Allende ads; the KGB directly wired Allende money, which he knowingly accepted, maintaining covert channels with them. It's unclear whose efforts, American or Russian, had the greater impact, or what the results would've been if no meddling had occurred. There were three major candidates in the election: Allende as the firmly left-wing candidate, a firmly right-wing candidate (Jorge Alessandri), and a moderate Christian democrat (Radomiro Tomic). The result: 36 percent of votes for Allende, 35 percent for Alessandri, and 28 percent for Tomic. Under Chilean law, if one candidate didn't get more than 50 percent of the vote, the winner would be selected and ratified by congress. This usually meant choosing the largest plurality, but there was no guarantee --- and a controversial candidate winning by a narrow margin could be just the time to do otherwise. However, Tomic, as leader of a party that controlled a significant portion of the congress, urged support for Allende. Combined with support for Allende's own party, this was enough for him to win the congressional vote with 153 of 200 seats, albeit not with full-throated confidence. He was asked to explicitly sign a statute of constitutional guarantee, affirming above and beyond the usual oath of office that he would not undermine the Chilean constitution. Allende agreed and his presidency was ratified. For decade Allende had been vying for power as a habitual outsider. The only time he'd been part of the executive branch, as health minister, was in his early 30s, and he was given that position because the president was trying to maintain a political coalition that included Allende's socialist party. That coalition didn't last, and in all the years since then Allende had never part of the governing party or coalition. To now be elected president himself, even if only by the skin of his teeth, was a dream come true. This was his chance to implement everything he wanted. He couldn't let that chance go to waste. The day Allende was sworn in, there were early warning signs. He announced that by his election victory, in which he was chosen by only slightly more than a third of the voting public, "the people" had "rejected capitalism." He declared his intent on using the presidency to massively transform the country in accordance with his vision of a socialist future. His government began seizing control of the copper industry, which, at the time, was a popular move. Copper was a key export market for Chile, but the largest mines were owned by U.S. companies that acquired them generations ago. The largest mine in the country was owned by Anaconda Copper, one of the largest mining companies in the world, headquartered in the U.S., which bought the rights to that land in 1922. For many, it was a symbolic act of national self-determination to retake control of this natural resource. But it didn't stop there. Allende took control of other mining businesses, like coal. He took control of steel production and oil refineries. He took over much of the financial sector, nationalizing most of Chile's largest private banks and all their assets. He took over the telecom industry, including the country's television broadcast service. His government started seizing and redistributing land from private holdings. He implemented widespread price controls on basic goods. As the situation worsened, Allende's government announced its intent to simply default on the country's international debts. Within a couple years, the economy had collapsed, there were widespread shortages, falling wages and roaring poverty. Support for Allende had evaporated, but Chile had sex-year presidential terms, meaning the people had to survive until 1976 if they wanted a chance to vote him out. In the Chilean congress, an opposition bloc formed. It included the right-wing opposition as well as most of Tomic's Christian Democrats who had supported Allende's presidency in 1970. The congress was so alarmed by Allende overstepping his prerogative as president that it went so far as to pass constitutional amendments that would put a stop to Allende's plans to continue nationalizing the economy. Allende simply ignored them, triggering a constitutional crisis. The courts sided against Allende. He didn't budge. In 1973, a majority of the Chilean congress voted in support of a scathing resolution laying out the misconduct of Allende's administration as an open accusation. Among other things, they claim Allende was trying to destroy freedom of speech by taking over the media, pressuring opposing voices, and jailing critical journalists; that he was disregarding the supreme court, choosing whether to act on its decisions based only on whether he agreed with them; that Allende was abusing his power by using special decrees and legal loopholes to push through measures of great importance to the country that "should unquestionably be matter of legislation," thus usurping the role of congress and pursuing "absolute executive authority" with no legislative oversight; that Allende was facilitating the creation of "government-protected armed groups" that were "headed towards a confrontation" with the military; and that, on the whole, Allende had committed not "isolated violations" of the law but rather "made such violations a permanent system of conduct." They did not explicitly ask for the police and military to intervene, but used language that could be interpreted as hinting at it, calling on "the president, ministers of state, and members of the police and armed forces," claiming it's "their duty to put an immediate end" to the situation, to redirect Chile toward "the path of law and ensuring the constitutional order." Allende read their resolution. He saw the economy was in ruin, he saw everything falling down around him, he saw that people were angry with him, that he didn't have the people's support, that his actions had caused economic and political crises. And he chose to ignore all of that and commit to not changing anything at all. Instead, he implied the congress was acting seditiously by using language that sounds like it could incite a civil conflict of some sort. In true Trumpian fashion, he gave a speech in which he doubled down on everything, congratulated himself for what he considered to be his own vague accomplishments, and declared that his government was actually the *most* democratic in the country's history. !table td { border: 1px solid light-dark(#888, #434343); background-color: var(--bg-light-blue); } Spanish | English translation Con tranquilidad de conciencia y midiendo mi responsabilidad ante las generaciones presentes y futuras, sostengo que nunca antes ha habido en Chile un Gobierno más democrático que el que me honro en presidir, que haya hecho más por defender la independencia económica y política del país, por la liberación social de los trabajadores. The government has been respectful of the law and has worked to bring about transformations in our economic and social structures. ... Parliament has become a bastion against reform and has done everything in its power to disrupt the functioning of finances and institutions, stifling any creative initiative. Allende had somehow figured out that repeatedly *claiming* to believe in democracy was good for his image. Those who want to defend his legacy would carry on that gimmick for long after his death. In every left-wing resource one can find, terms like "democratically elected" and "committed to democracy" are sprinkled in wherever possible, even in the many instances where the myth of Allende's career is merely being weaponized by Communists who don't care about democracy in the first place and are happy to support undemocratic governments in every other instance. A man who truly cared about democracy would not insist that such a narrow election victory, and ratification by a tenuous congressional coalition, implied a broad popular mandate to radically transform the country in a way that was never expressed by most voters. In a country so divided, if Allende were an honourable person who seriously cared about the spirit of democratic rule, he should have been seeking compromise and consensus, executing laws neutrally in coöperation with congress, and figuring out how to pursue as much of his agenda as possible while maintaining support from the public and from his political allies. If I, or any other decent person, were in his position, I should want to be confident the people really are on my side before I attempt something as audacious as what Allende was trying to push through. Allende did not become so considerate until the September of 1973, after that congressional resolution, when he reportedly suggested in private that perhaps the country should hold a referendum on some question or another. It may have been too little, and it was definitely too late. In 1973, two weeks after the Chilean congress declared Allende's government illegal, the Chilean military mobilized. The air force bombed the government's propaganda infrastructure, the army moved to take the presidential palace. As they grew year, Allende gave one last speech over radio, mostly of pleasantries, thanking people for their service, and then committed suicide by gunshot. He was offered safe passage if he surrendered, but declined. He died believing everybody who didn't support him were traitors and that history would vindicate him. With Allende removed and those who remained loyal to him defeated, the Chilean Armed Forces didn't return power to the civilian government. Instead they assembled a military council, led by the heads of the different branches of the armed forces. These were Adm. Merino of the Navy, Gen. Leigh of the Air Force, Gen. Mendoza of the national police (Carabineros), and Gen. Pinochet of the army. Together they would control and administer all functions of government. This was presented this as a temporary measure, necessary to restore order. However, they soon dissolved the Chilean congress, suspended the constitution, banned all political parties, and began rounding up and killing thousands of people whom they identified as dissidents. They specifically targeted people who had been supporters of Allende's political faction, Popular Unity (Spanish: *Unidad Popular*). Instead of power being shared between them as initially proposed, Pinochet monopolized all power for himself. He was declared Supreme Chief of the Nation, then legitimized as president in 1974. !images float src: media/augusto-pinochet.png | caption: Augusto Pinochet, years after the coup The timing was lucky for Pinochet. He'd only risen to command-in-chief of the army earlier that year, appointed by Allende himself in the August of 1973, less than a month before participating in the coup. Pinochet later claimed he spent months plotting the coup because he wanted to save the country from falling into chaos, but other evidence, including testimony from other military leaders like Leigh and Merino, suggests he was the very last person to join the plot, which he may not have been aware of until days before launch. It was tragic to read that Carlos Briones, minister of the interior in Allende's government, claims in his memoirs that Allende put great trust in Pinochet, staying in communications with him until the very last day before the coup, believing that Pinochet would remain on his side even if other elements of the armed forces turned against him. When he learned Pinochet had joined the others, Allende realized all was lost. Pinochet spent years reshaping the laws and institutions of the country to keep himself in power. In the 1970s most of Latin America was ruled by a military dictatorship of some sort, and the death counts that Pinochet was causing weren't enough to make Chile stand out in a crowd. In 1980, Pinochet organized a referendum to approve a new Chilean constitution, and two thirds of the population supported it, despite it legally sanctioning Pinochet to stay in power for another eight years while the country figured out how to transition back to civilian rule. In 1988, there was another referendum, simply asking the public to confirm if they wanted Chile to remain in power. The result was 44 percent of people who supported him staying in power, 55 percent opposed. Pinochet didn't initially accept this result: he met with leaders of the national policy and military to discuss ways to orchestrate seizing power for himself again, but they refused. Only then, with no other options remaining, did Pinochet publicly concede and begin the transition process. The next year, in 1989, Chile held elections again, and in the March of 1990 Pinochet formally stepped down. The country swore in Patricio Aylwin as their first president elected by a majority since 1964. Pinochet's regime was brutal while it landed. It's interesting that he was able to step down and peacefully retire, a rarity for military dictators, who often hang onto power until death. He travelled to the UK, where he was unexpectedly arrested in 1998, at age 83, while the British government considered charging him with crimes against humanity; but he was released in 2000 on medical grounds and retired to Chile. He remained a controversial figure and some tried to bring charges against him, but he was never imprisoned nor convicted and died in his home in Santiago with his wife in 2006, age 91. The story of Allende and Pinochet is interesting because it's the tale of two terrible people that spawned two different hero myths. By the right Pinochet is cast as a competent military man who stepped up and saved his country from a Communist tyrant, rescuing the economy, and sure, he did some repression, but it went less far than you'd have thought, and then he gave up power peacefully. What a man! For the left, Allende was a tragic hero bordering on sainthood, an example of somebody who tried to pursue socialism the right way, but somehow he still got punished for it. How unfair, and how it shows our true colours that America didn't support him! The reality leaves us with little reason to idolize anybody involved. Allende wasn't ordering people slaughtered in the streets, but he was a megalomaniacal authoritarian ideologue who unleashed incredible suffering onto his people with reckless disregard, and he was in the process of taking steps to further entrench his power domestically while meeting with Soviet officials. It's entirely possible that if he weren't removed the country could have further degraded and become a Communist dictatorship, whether led by Allende or whoever succeeded him. Pinochet, meanwhile, was more a treacherous opportunist than anything principled or heroic, most of the destruction he unleashed was not necessary to save the country, and instead of returning the country to civilian rule after removing Allende, he allowed himself to become a tyrant, torturing thousands of innocents and suppressing Chilean democracy for years to come. He stepped down peacefully not by choice but because he knew that maintaining power by force was no longer possible.
