**Salvador Allende** (*ay-unn-day*) was born in 1908 in Santiago, the capital city of Chile. After going to medical school, by his 20s he became involved in politics. In 1933, when he was 25, Allende was a founding member of the Socialist Party of Chile. In 1937, at age 29, he was elected to the Chamber of Deputies, the lower chamber of the Chilean congress; then in 1939 served as health minister for Chilean president Pedro Aguirre Cerda, resigning in 1942. In 1945 he was elected back into the Chilean congress as a senator, as he remained until 1970, age 62, representing various constituencies along the way. He also participated as a presidential candidate numerous times along the way (in 1952, 1958, and 1964), returning to the senate after each unsuccessful attempt. By that 1970 election, Chile had become a battleground for the great powers in the Cold War. The 1959 Cuban revolution gave the USSR a foothold in the Western hemisphere in Fidel Castro, and they were interested in more. The U.S. got involved and soon both sides had agents in the country funnelling resources toward their favoured parties. The CIA paid to run anti-Allende ads; the KGB directly wired Allende money. It's unclear whose efforts had the greatest impact or what the results would've been if no meddling had occurred. The election was a close three-way race between Allende as the firmly left-wing candidate, a firmly right-wing candidate (Jorge Alessandri), and a moderate Christian democrat (Radomiro Tomic). The result: 36 percent of votes for Allende, 35 percent for Alessandri, and 28 percent for Tomic. In Chile the law was that if one candidate didn't get more than 50 percent of the vote, the winner would be selected and ratified by congress. This usually meant choosing the largest plurality, but there was no guarantee, and a controversial candidate winning by a narrow margin could have been the time to do otherwise. However, Tomic urged support for Allende, which combined with support for Allende's own party was enough for him to win the congressional vote with 153 of 200 seats, albeit not with full-throated confidence. He was asked to explicitly sign a statute of constitutional guarantee, affirming above and beyond the usual oath of office that he would not undermine the Chilean constitution. Allende agreed. Allende had been vying for power for decades as a habitual outsider. The one time he had been part of the executive branch, he was in his early 30s, and he was appointed to the position because the candidate who won the presidency was trying to maintain a political coalition that included Allende's socialist party. That coalition didn't last, and in all the years since then Allende was never part of the governing party or coalition. To now be elected president himself, even if only by the skin of his teeth, was a dream come true. This was his chance to implement everything he had been dreaming of for so long. He couldn't let that chance go to waste. The day he was sworn in, there were early warning signs. Allende announced that by his election victory, in which he was chosen by only slightly more than a third of voters, "the people" had "rejected capitalism," and declared his intent on using the presidency to massively transform the country in accordance with his vision of a socialist future. His government began seizing control of the copper industry, which, at the time, was a popular move. Copper was a key export market for Chile, but the largest mines were owned by U.S. companies that acquired them generations ago. The largest mine in the country was owned by Anaconda Copper, one of the largest mining companies in the world, which bought the rights to that land in 1922. For many, it was a symbolic act of national self-determination to retake control of this natural resource. But it didn't stop there. Allende also took control of other mining businesses, like coal. He took control of steel production, oil refineries. They took over much of the financial sector, nationalizing most of their largest private banks and all their assets. They took over the telecom industry, including the country's television broadcast service. They started seizing and redistributing land from private holdings. They implemented widespread price controls on basic goods. As the situation worsened, they announced their intent to simply default on the country's international debts. Within a couple years, the economy had collapsed, there were widespread shortages, falling wages and roaring poverty, and everybody had turned on Allende. But at the time, Chile had six-year presidential terms, meaning the people had to survive until 1976 if they wanted a chance to vote him out. In the Chilean congress, an opposition bloc formed, which came to include most of Tomic's Christian Democratic Party, despite helping Allende become president in 1970. The congress was so alarmed by Allende overstepping his prerogative as president, they went so far as to pass constitutional amendments to put a stop to Allende's plans to continue nationalizing the economy. Allende refused and ignored them, triggering a constitutional crisis. The courts sided against Allende; he didn't budge. In 1973, a majority of the Chilean congress voted in support of a scathing resolution that lays out the misconduct of Allende's administration as an open accusation. Among other things, they claim Allende was trying to destroy freedom of speech by taking over the media, pressuring opposing voices, and jailing critical journalists; that he was disregarding the supreme court, choosing whether to act on its decisions based on whether he agreed with them; that Allende was abusing his power by using special decrees and legal loopholes to push through measures of great importance to the country that "should unquestionably be matter of legislation," thus usurping the role of congress and pursuing "absolute executive authority" with no legislative oversight; that Allende was facilitating the creation of "government-protected armed groups" that were "headed towards a confrontation" with the military; and that, on the whole, Allende had committed not "isolated violations" of the law but rather "made such violations a permanent system of conduct." They all but explicitly ask for the police and military to intervene, calling on "the president, ministers of state, and members of the police and armed forces," claiming it's "their duty to put an immediate end" to the situation they have described, and to redirect Chile toward "the path of law and ensuring the constitutional order." Allende read their resolution. Allende had figured out that repeatedly *claiming* to believe in democracy was good for his image, and those who wanted to defend his legacy would carry on that gimmick long after his death. In every left-wing resource one can find, terms like "democratically elected" are inserted repeatedly even if redundant, even in the many instances where the myth of Allende's rise and fall is merely being weaponized by Marxists, Communists, and other left-wing extremists who don't care about democracy in the first place and who are happy to support undemocratic governments in every other context. Allende did not seem so considerate --- until the September of 1973, when he reportedly considered that perhaps the country should hold a referendum. It may not have been too little, but it was definitely too late. In 1973, two weeks after the Chilean congress declared Allende's government illegal, the Chilean military decided to act. The air force bombed the government's propaganda infrastructure and the army moved in to take the presidential palace. As they grew near, Allende gave one last speech over the radio, mostly of pleasantries like thanking people for their service, and committed suicide by gunshot. He was offered safe passage if he surrendered, but he declined. From what I can tell, Allende died believing everybody who didn't support him were simply traitors, and that history would vindicate him. With Allende removed and those who stayed loyal to him defeated, the Chilean Armed Forces set up a military council led by the heads of the different branches of the armed forces: Adm. Merino of the Navy, Gen. Leigh of the Air Force, Gen. Mendoza of the Carabineros (national police), and Gen. Pinochet of the Army. They would control and administer all functions of government indefinitely. Within the first few months, they dissolved the Chilean congress, suspended the constitution, banned all political parties, and started rounding up and killing thousands of people whom they identified as dissidents, targeting those who had been supporters of Allende's political faction, *Popular Unity* (Spanish: *Unidad Popular*). Pinochet was declared Supreme Chief of the Nation, then declared president in 1974, centralizing power away from the others. In a sense, the timing was lucky for Pinochet: he had only risen to become commander-in-chief of the Army earlier that year, appointed by Allende himself in the August of 1973, less than a month before participating in the coup. Pinochet later claimed he spent months plotting the coup because he wanted to save the country from falling into chaos, but other historical evidence, including testimony from other military leaders including Leigh and Merino, suggests he was the last person to join the plot and paints him as more of an opportunist. Carlos Briones, who was Minister of the Interior in Allende's administration, claimed in his memoirs that, tragically, Allende had great trust in Pinochet, staying in communications with him until the very last day before the coup, believing that Pinochet would remain on his side if other elements of the armed forces turned on him. Pinochet spent years reshaping the laws and institutions of the country to keep himself in power. In the 1970s, most of Latin America was ruled by a military dictatorship of some sort, and the death counts Pinochet's government caused weren't enough to make Chile stand out in a crowd. In 1980, Pinochet organized a referendum to approve a new Chilean constitution, and two thirds of the population supported it, despite it legally sanctioning Pinochet to stay in power for another eight years while the country transitioned back into being ruled by a civil government. In 1988, there was another referendum, simply asking the people of Chile to confirm whether they wanted Pinochet to remain in power. The result: 44 percent supported Pinochet, 55 percent opposed. Pinochet didn't initially accept the result. He met with leaders of the national police and military to discuss ways to orchestrate seizing power for himself once again, but they refused. Only then, with no other options remaining, Pinochet publicly conceded and began the transition process. The next year, 1989, Chile held elections again, and in the March of 1990 Pinochet formally stepped down as the country swore in Patricio Aylwin as the first president elected by a majority since 1964. Though his regime was brutal while it lasted, it's interesting that Pinochet was able to eventually step down peacefully and retire, a rarity for military dictators. He travelled to the UK, where he was arrested in 1998, age 83, as the government considered charging him with crimes against humanity, but he was released in 2000 on medical grounds and returned to Chile. He remained a controversial figure and some tried to bring charges against him, but he was never convicted nor imprisoned and died in his home in Santiago with his wife in 2006, age 91. As far as dictators go, Pinochet got a rare good ending, maintaining his freedom and dying in old age.
