In the French Revolutionary period, supporters of the monarchy sat on the right side of the National Assembly while supporters of the revolution sat on the left. This arbitrary seating arrangement is the origin of why we talk about politics in terms of left versus right, and the fact a "left--right spectrum" has only one dimension means it appears to divide politics in two: we're all either on one side of things or the other. It would therefore be reasonable to conclude that if your country is fighting an extreme right-wing threat, anybody on the left side of things would be an ally in that fight and vice versa. Sadly that is not often the case. Not all important divisions in politics are captured by left and right. And as it turns out, extremists make for unreliable allies. This was true in past eras and, unfortunately, it rings true today. A wave of revolutions along the way to democracy happened [in 1848](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1848); another came [around the end of the First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1917%E2%80%931923). These periods of upheaval are an opportunity for positive change, but are dangerous because they also represent opportunity for well-organized extremist groups to swoop in and seize the day before the dust settles. This happened in Italy, where years of conflict against the monarchy failed to dislodge the Italian king, but left the country fractured and made way for **Benito Mussolini**, founder of Fascist Party, to organize his followers into a paramilitary force known as the blackshirts (officially the MVSN) and launch the [March on Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_on_Rome) in 1922. This was led by people who believed in democratic reform, and who considered their positions to be temporary. They scheduled for elections to be held later that year, for the country to elect members to the Russian Constituent Assembly, after which the largest parties in the legislature would form a new, democratic government. This sounded like great news for Russia. This would be the first fully democratic elections in Russian history, and represented the country taking its first steps toward liberal democracy. But unfortunately, it wouldn't last. Later that year, before elections took place, the most extreme faction in the country, the **Bolshevik Party**, led an attack the new government in what became known as the [October Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/October_Revolution), taking over the executive branch in the Winter Palace. The elections were held later that month as planned, but when the Bolsheviks didn't win, they ignored the results, dissolved the assembly, outlawed opposition parties, and never held elections again. Instead they organized their new government into one-party state with Bolshevik leader **Vladimir Lenin** ruling as its first dictator until his death in 1924 (succeeded by Joseph Stalin). In response to the Bolshevik coup d'état, [a civil war broke out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Civil_War) across the country, as the Bolshevik siege on the government in Saint Petersburg was not popularly recognized. But after a few years, in 1922, the new Bolshevik government emerged victorious. With their domestic enemies now defeated, they formalized a new entity called the **Soviet Union**.
