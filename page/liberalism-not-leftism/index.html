In the French Revolution, supporters of the monarchy sat on the right side of the National Assembly while supporters of the revolution sat on the left. This arbitrary seating arrangement is what gave us the language of *left and right*, a lens with enduring appeal still used when talking about politics today. The fact there's only one dimension makes it seem like politics is divided in two: everything falls onto either one side or the other. And so you may think if we're fighting an extreme right-wing threat, anybody on the left side of things would be an ally, and vice versa. But not all divisions in politics are captured by left and right. And as it turns out, extremists unreliable allies. Left or right, they're more likely to turn on you in effort to take power for theirselves than to be good-faith participants in a united front against a common enemy. A wave of (mostly unsuccessful) revolutions along the way to modern democracy happened [in 1848](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1848), and then another wave happened [around the end of World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1917%E2%80%931923). These periods of upheaval can be an opportunity for great change, but also present opportunity for well-organized extremist groups to swoop in and seize the day while things are unstable during the transition. In Italy, there were years of conflict against the monarchy, but the people failed to dislodge the Italian king and he still had loyal followers --- until Benito Mussolini, founder of the Italian National Fascist Party, whose followers were organized into a paramilitary force now known as the blackshirts, took advantage of the situation by organizing the [March on Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_on_Rome) in 1922, declaring their intent to take control of the government by force if necessary. The Italian king, seeing the day was lost, chose to avoid unnecessary bloodshed and surrender power to Mussolini, naming him Prime Minister. This sequence of events --- an unstable political situation caused by popular dissatisfaction with the current government, which creates an opening for an extremist group to pull off a coup d'état and install theirselves as the new dictators --- is a simple pattern we've seen play out in many other countries. # Russia In early 1917, widespread opposition to the government of **Tsar Nicholas II** culminated in the [February Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/February_Revolution) (March 12), leading to the Tsar's abdication (March 15) and the formation of the Russian Provisional Government, with the first elections in Russian history to be held later that year. For a brief time, it sounded like great news for Russia, that it would be following in the footsteps of Western European countries toward liberal democracy. But it wouldn't last. Instead the most extreme faction in the country, the **Bolshevik Party**, flipped the table. Their supporters, organized and militant, attacked the new government (Nov 5: [October Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/October_Revolution)\). The elections were held later that month, but when the Bolsheviks didn't win they ignored the results, dissolved the assembly, outlawed opposition parties, and established a one-party state with Bolshevik leader **Vladimir Lenin** ruling as its first dictator until his death in 1924 (to be succeeded by **Joseph Stalin**). In response to the Bolshevik rise to power, a civil war broke out ([Russian Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Civil_War)\), and after a few years the new Bolshevik government emerged victorious (1922). Their domestic entities now crushed, they reformed a new entity called the **Soviet Union** --- and as WWII didn't cause its early demise as it did for the Nazis and Fascists, this entity would rule over Russia, and the other countries it managed to subvert and dominate, from then until it finally ran out of steam in 1991. .The names of some events in Russia are misaligned from the actual dates (things that occurred in early March were called February) because Russia was still using the Julian calendar until 1918. In those years after Lenin took power (1917), he oversaw a period of widespread political repression now known as the **Red Terror**, in which Bolshevik militants purged dissent across the countryside on Lenin's order. They publicly tortured and executed anybody they considered rebels to discourage standing up to their newfound authority. The regime Lenin started, and which Joseph Stalin inherited, was a repressive, militant, imperialist, authoritarian society.
