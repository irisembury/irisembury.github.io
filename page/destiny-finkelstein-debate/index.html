--- title: Destiny--Finkelstein Debate: Retrospective date: 2026-10-14 mirrors: substack:220450387 --- Debate: **Steven Bonnell, Benny Morris, Norman Finklestein, Mouin Rabbani**, hosted by Lex Fridman March 14, 2024 | 4 hours, 53 minutes https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1X_KdkoGxSs #### Preface In 2024, a number of people I knew online were talking about a debate that just happened. Everybody seemed to have the same impression: the internet streamer known as Destiny (Steven Bonnell) finally sat down with a real, serious person like Finkelstein to discuss the topic of Israel--Palestine, and he was thoroughly schooled, trounced, dismantled, he really met his match. I didn't really care, so I didn't watch the video, but I remember it being the talk of the town. The only part everybody was linking to were clips from the debate in which Finkelstein appears to forget Bonnell's name, calling him Borrell, Borreli, and Morelli, the furthest he got from his actual name. He later indicated he did this intentionally to belittle him, to make it sound as though he was so unimportant compared with the other participants that his name was not even worth remembering. It was mostly called a debate between Bonnell and Finkelstein, which is therefore how I've titled this post, but there were four participants at the time. Mouin Rabbani and Norm Finkelstein, two pro-Palestine activists, sat across from Bonnell and Benny Morris, a famous Israeli historian whose work is well regarded even by his opponents, who disagree with his politics but praise his accuracy as an historian. Rabbani, born in the Netherlands, is a child of Palestinian refugees and now works as a media analyst in Jordan. Finkelstein is an American Jew. Benny Morris is an Israeli-born Jew. The three people who aren't Bonnell have published books on the topic of Israel--Palestine broadly, making Bonnell the odd man out among them. He was likely used as the draw for the video, to bring in a younger audience who would never have watched a debate between two old men like Morris and Finkelstein otherwise. Bonnell doesn't speak until over a half-hour into the debate and speaks the least of the four people at the table. It may have felt more like a confrontation between Bonnell and Finkelstein if you only saw the popular clips that were shared of Finkelstein and Bonnell insulting each other. Those aside, I'd have likely forgotten this debate ever took place if not for the fact it's still referenced occasionally. Not many days ago, for example, on October 9th, I saw [Wajahat Ali referencing it on Twitter](https://x.com/WajahatAli/status/2108411395402502350), saying that Bonnell's debate with Finkelstein didn't go well. Did it not? That's what everybody says, but I don't know if I've ever seen a specific comment or analysis about what was said during the debate. We only get these ambiguous, high-level claims that Bonnell came out looking bad. For its lingering folk importance, I decided to watch the whole thing. Then I watched it again, and on the second watch I took notes about what parts of the debate were actually interesting or substantive, because clearly a lot of it could've been condensed. In a long, informal, open-ended conversation, there's naturally a lot of space between claims or back-and-forth waffling that doesn't end up going anywhere. Have you ever been slightly curious what really happened back then, but not enough to sit through the whole video? If anything, the video gave me a positive impression of Morris and a negative one of Finkelstein, whose belligerent attitude most stood in the way of several points being unpacked enough to get anywhere interesting. For a debate involving such well known figures, it doesn't go very far into anything. For this, I mostly blame the host, Fridman, who seems to prefer to ask rather vague, open-ended questions about big topics, leaving the participants to engage in their own exposition, and then instead of directing the conversation to an exploration of their differences, we move onto the next topic, as though the purpose of the meeting was not to debate but more to compare answers. In the final hours, he also repeats some variation of the same prompt, essentially asking the participants to please end on something optimistic, which is the point in the video where we most could have asked interesting, pointed questions now that the participants had laid out so many of their thoughts over the preceding hours. This in combination with Finkelstein's obstruction resulted in a lot of wasted potential. The most surprising element of the talk was Rabbani, whom I think was the least talked about participant in the aftermath, not the headliner nor perceived as Bonnell's main opposition, a price he paid for his calmer and more productive composure. Behind said composure, some of the things he said were surprisingly radical and inflammatory, though Morris and Bonnell mostly let it slide. In conclusion, I've probably thoroughly wasted my time. I also now have a strong suspicion that many people who claim Bonnell was trounced did not actually bother watching the debate and are simply counting on the assumption that you won't either.
