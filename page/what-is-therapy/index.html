!meta title: What is therapy? date: 2025-01-09 There was a point in my life where everybody around me started recommending I go to therapy. Not as an insult, but as they could see I was going through a difficult time, and they weren't sure what to do theirselves to alleviate it, so they wanted to advise that I seek professional help instead of suffering alone with no plan on how to make things better. I wasn't allergic to the idea. But where does one get therapy? I didn't know where to go, who to talk to, or what to say. Then I was especially struck by the realization I didn't know what I'd be asking for. What is therapy? What does it consist of? Talking to a person while laying on a sofa, like in the movies? For a physical ailment, let's say my leg hurts, I go to a doctor and they look at my leg, perhaps they scan me, and they're able to see something I don't. They diagnose the problem. "Oh, you have boneitis." They give you a solution of some kind, whether drugs or surgery. But for therapy? There's nothing to see. There's nothing to look at. We've not reached the point where I can say "I'm sad" and a doctor says "well then let's scan your brain to find out why". The only information they have access to is stuff I already know, because I'm the one who's going to tell them. And then once they've got to a point where I told them what the problem is, what solution do they offer? Not drugs, not any kind of medical procedure. Just more words. They talk to you. That's all they ever do. So it's like asking somebody for advice? Is it like hiring a consultant, a life coach? If the solution to my problems were a series of words, could somebody just tell me those words? Could I get them in an email? Or are the words this person is going to tell me so specific and individualized that we really can't be sure which sequence they're going to say to me, for my problems, until after weeks of narrowing it down? The whole process felt obscure to me, and when I expressed my concern and doubt, I wasn't met with reassuring answers. It was also very difficult to reach the point where I was actually sat down with a therapist in order to find out. There were months-long periods of jumping through hoops, waiting for appointments for phone conversations with people who would decide whether I was right for the therapy program they could recommend me for. A lot of process. Now, looking back, let me share with you what I learned. It turns out, for some reason, talking about stuff is good for your brain. But sometimes if you talk about the stuff you're feeling, the people around you react negatively and make you feel like you're gay or something because you're gay or something for having emotions and now you feel like a burden and it sucks. A therapist is somebody you can talk to about anything you're feeling, anything that's bothering you, and they're never going to be a dick about any of it, because then it would be pointless and you wouldn't pay them. So there's that. And for some people, just having a person to talk to is enough, because maybe that patient, sympathetic ear is the big thing that's missing in your life. You never get to express yourself, you feel like nobody ever listens to you. It can be a really nice, comforting experience. It wasn't for me, but for some people that's really all therapy has to be. But once you go in and start talking to them, then what? Where does the conversation go? If just getting it all out and being able to express yourself wasn't all you were looking for, then what's the part where the therapist actually does something to address or help with your problems? The content of therapy, the problem-solving part, can be reduced to two or three steps, depending on how you want to categorize it. First, **identify the problem**, and then maybe also identify **the cause of the problem**. Let's say you're unhappy. Why is that? You might not know. Not everyone does. You're talking with your wife one day, and you feel unhappy. She notices it in you. You're not the man you used to be. You're getting grumpier, less expressive, more rock-like than you used to be. What's going on with you? A therapist might help you figure out how to describe what's even happening. Is the problem your home life? Is it your friends? How are your parents doing? Maybe the problem ends up being your job. You're pissed off at work. Maybe you feel unsatisfied there. It's not much, but that's a starting point, and not everybody can identify the locale of a problem like that. Is it because you're annoyed at all coworkers? Do you have problems communicating? Do you just lack patience? Is it because of your boss? Maybe it's because you feel inferior when he tells you what to do, because you didn't achieve enough and you're not satisfied with where you are in your career, and that little prick is 3 years younger than you but he's higher up the corporate ladder because he went to business school even though you've been at this company longer than he has? Oh yeah, shit, that resonates with me. Huh. I never thought about it like that. Oh god, I think that's it. I'm disappointed with myself. That's kind of the second step: wandering around the different possibilities, until we can make your description of the problem more specific. We go from problem, or symptom, to the cause of that problem or symptom. Then it's like: okay, why is that what you're feeling? Are you *actually* not far enough into your career? Did something go wrong somewhere along the way? Or is this just an irrational feeling you're having, that's not really true if you stop and think about it? If it's the latter, then we get to have a whole conversation about **cognitive distortions**, which are just descriptions of certain ways that you might be thinking about stuff wrong. The therapist might give you a list of like 15 of them. Maybe you're catastrophizing. Maybe you're mind reading. Maybe you're -- whatever, doing one of those things. By the way, do you want any medication? Because a therapist might also be able to hook you up with stuff that helps certain physical symptoms related to mental health stuff. And they might not. It depends on the person. That shit can help, though. You might also ask where that feeling of inferiority or dissatisfaction comes from. Was it because you struggled at school and got bullied for having bad grades? Is it because your father wasn't very successful and you're scared of being like him? I don't know---but everybody expects their problems to have some kind of villain origin story. A therapist likes it if you can identify one of those, and most people find figuring out the origin of their problems to be satisfying and helpful to feeling like they can get over things, because you're "solving the puzzle" of your own mind. Next, you have to figure out **what to do about this problem**. In some cases, you're never going to find a "fix" and you just have to live with it. So you might feel like you're kind of fucked, but that's okay, because so are lots of people. That's normal. I suck at some stuff too. Don't be so hard on yourself? You're still a cool person who other people can value and love, even if you have certain things that you struggle with. It's like if you broke a knee a century ago, you had to limp. But you can still get up and leave the house and do stuff, even if stairs are a bit harder for you than other people. But maybe it *is* something you can fix and get over. Not all problems are unfixable. So how do you do that? What's the cure? It's not going to be like a doctor where they give you a pill or ointment. Take this for 30 days and you'll be fine. No, no. This side of the aisle is more talk-y. And there are a lot of different answers, depending on where you ended up, but on some level they're all variations of the same thing: you just have to do the stuff you don't want to do. That thing you're avoiding? Do it. I don't know what it is, but you know what I'm talking about. And that's it, really. That's the final boss of therapy. Maybe you do it once in a blue moon, maybe you do it an inch at a time, maybe you just bite the bullet and start at full-speed and hope it gets better. Whatever your specific approach ends up being, though, the only way things improve is if you figure out how to stop avoiding that thing you're avoiding. You know the thing. Let's say you're stressed out because you're scared of heights, because that creates a really tidy metaphor. Here's a ladder, climb up a step. Then try climbing up two steps. Was that too much? Are your overwhelmed? Then chill. Two was enough. Don't worry about it. Take a break. But come back eventually, and the goal is to do three. Then at some point, you have to do four. And so on. By the way, working out is like this, if you want to get into weight training or doing pull-ups or anything like that. Then go up a flight of stairs. Go to the second story of a building, look out the window. Then try two stories. And so on. This is really the only way you'll ever get over a fear of heights, or any kind of psychological aversion to anything. You conquer by doing. If you have crippling social anxiety? You have to socialize. You have to talk to people. Strangers. Talk to them. Really, for almost every mental health issues, there's an easily identifiable *thing* that you're obviously avoiding because of your condition, and you can either make peace with that -- by building your life around never having to do that thing (maybe you can go the rest of your life without ever going up a tall building? that's probably possible, just live in a really flat place), or you can get over it by making yourself do the thing. That's it. Those are the two outcomes. That's what therapy leads to. And then you also get a person to sit there and talk you through it. If you already have people to talk to in your life, so you don't feel that pressing need to *get it all out*---because seriously, talking about stuff makes everything feel better---and you already basically get what you're struggling with, why, and what to do about it, then you probably don't really need to go to therapy, quite frankly. You might benefit from it still, I guess. Like, you could. Maybe your therapist has some great insight that you never thought of. They just become advice-givers. But it's also possible that you're just going to go through the motions explaining your situation to them so, at the end, they can tell that you feel bad because of that stuff your parents did or whatever, and that if you want to get better, you have to go do that thing you're avoiding. If you can figure out that stuff, and you can identify the avoidant thing on your own, then yeah, you might save yourself the hassle. And the money, if you're not in circumstances where you can get it all paid for. !image-span media/01.png
