--- title: Thoughts on airport privatization date: 2026-09-20 mirrors: substack:216649667 --- Though it's been a big topic of conversation over the last year or so (ever since [Mr. Carney first mentioned it](https://globalnews.ca/news/11822472/canada-in-early-stages-of-talks-on-whether-to-privatize-airports-minister/)), I still don't have a strong stance on airport privatization in Canada simply because I don't seriously know if it'll have good or bad results. And what I don't like about the discourse about the proposal that we privatize airports is that, looking around at everybody commenting on it, I can hardly trust anybody else knows what the results will be either. It seems more like if you're oriented against the current government, then you're against the privatization, whereas if you're supportive of the government, you might defend it. The criticism and defense are both boilerplate, things that one could say of any instance of privatization ever. The downside is that we're selling ourselves off to spooky private equity and maybe the result will be higher prices, but the upside is that maybe actually the market will handle this better and it's really government that's the problem. Does anyone know anything about airports in particular, or about the air travel industry in Canada particularly? I don't. On the one hand, I do have some vague intuition that privatization feels bad, because it often comes about for short-term gain, doesn't seem to work out well in the long term, and is only something governments resort to because the thing in question suffered from neglect, and we're desperately looking for some way to dump it as an asset so the blame can fall on some company instead of on the government. Moreover, it feels like a one-way corridor: once you decide to fully privatize a thing, there's practically no going back. It's much easier to sell things off than to commit all the money it would take to buy them back. There's a conservative instinct to be against sweeping change that you know will be difficult to reverse if it doesn't work out well. And indeed, we can find instances of privatization not working out well (though we do also have some examples of it leading to good outcomes, like with CN Rail). That all said, I certainly wouldn't like the proposal of selling off roads, or schools, or parks. But airports? I have no idea. For one, I don't actually use airports much. It's been over a decade since I was on a plane. I'm therefore mostly relying on what I can read about to make any kind of judgment about quality. But considering the government will still, I assume, be able to regulate air travel, there's not something about an airport that strikes me as a service that can't reasonably be operated by private companies. Looking around the world, I see that public ownership is currently the norm in Canada as well as in the U.S., whereas private ownership is actually the norm in Europe, as well as in much of Latin America. What's the verdict? Which model works better? I don't know. It seems like they both "work," because I've never heard anybody complain that either grouping of countries have much better or much worse service than the other. Instead, complaints seem to be focused more on specific airlines or specific airports, which makes me think the problems with air travel are more granular than public versus private ownership. And the discourse surrounding the privatization plan never seems to get into anything granular. Mr. Carney has been perceived as a particularly European man (holding British and EU citizenship and speaking for many years at the World Economic Forum in Switzerland), so a thought that crosses my mind is perhaps he's experienced privatized airports in Europe and thought they seemed fine, ergo it seems like a perfectly safe and reasonable thing for him to pursue in Canada. However, his actual proposal, though it's often been shortened simply to privatization, has been for the government to retain de jure ownership of the underlying terminal but to issue long-term leases whereby private companies would operate them. Not the same as what they're doing in Europe. What's more, it sounds like the initial plan that's materialized is [to target Canada's four largest airports](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/carney-airport-private-investment-9.7344327) (YYZ, YVR, YUL, and YYC), not come for all of our airports all at once. Then depending on how that goes, we'll decide how we want to go further. The more details of the proposal I hear about, the less I'm against it. It's been interesting to see the response because we typically associate privatization with the right, but as it's being proposed by a Liberal minister, right-wingers online have naturally been skeptical, and in some cases are resorting to making the same generally anti-privatization arguments that you may have previously expected from the left. Our opposition leader, Pierre Poilievre, has not been explicitly and flat-out against the notion of privatizing airports, but [has been expressing his concern about the plan](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/mark-carney-privatizing-airports-explained-toronto-montreal-calgary-vancouver-9.7345485) possibly resulting in "sweetheart deals for Liberal insiders." This response is Poilievre's comfort zone. He's always warning us that everything the government does might be a secret conspiracy for these "insiders," never with any evidence and never speaking in specifics. But he's not categorically saying that privatization is bad, at least. All in all, I'm just waiting to see what happens, because I'm still not feeling principally nor immovably one way or the other.
