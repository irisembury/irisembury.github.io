In a 2002 episode of Futurama called *Godfellas*, Bender is fired into space at high speed and ends up lost, floating around so far from home he could realistically never be found nor navigate his way back. He eventually encounters a mysterious entity represented only by lights. It twice shares a unique sentiment that viewers found rather memorable: "When you do things right, people won't be sure you've done anything at all." The entity was actually describing the fine balance of being god without either making people dependent on you or letting them lose hope entirely, but the line can be interpreted in other ways. I've most often seen it related to your job. There are numerous careers that are naturally under-appreciated because you specialize in things people don't often focus on, such as if you maintain the infrastructure we all take for granted. A narrower example can be found in film production, where audiences notice and appreciate actors' performances, but things like sound mixing or editing are taken for granted, except when they're done poorly. When things like this are done poorly, they stand out and get our attention. When done right, they fade into the background and allow us to focus on other things. A way this relates to my life is a lesson I learned later than I wish I had: if nobody else is giving you credit or appreciating you, then you may have to do it yourself. Be your own advocate. If you do something good or selfless or virtuous and people don't notice, tell them. Point it out. Bring it to their attention. If you never do this, if you *refuse* to ever do it, then over time you may find that people take you for granted, don't appreciate you as much as they should, or even have an overall negative perception of you---because they notice when things go badly, and overlook you when you do things right. Not everyone is like this, of course---hopefully you have *someone* in your life who notices and appreciates you---but those on the periphery are more at risk of developing a certain lean over time. Now if you're like me, this feels bad to do. It feels you're doing something wrong, like you're committing a faux pas, giving into some sort of vice. Demanding attention, demanding thanks? No, we should be humble, gracious, don't brag, don't be conceited, don't be full of ourselves. That's how I feel too. In fact, to do a good thing and *except* gratitude or acknowledgement may feel like it taints or nullifies your doing a good thing in the first place, as now you're not doing good for its own sake. "A truly virtuous person," we say to ourselves, "does what's right just for the sake of doing so." But perhaps that mentality supposes a just world in which the virtuous person lives, one in which their virtue is sustainable and not self-destructive. Do we truly mean to say a virtuous person is content to do a thankless job forever, to never be appreciated, to be taken for granted by everyone around them? To expect nothing, to be content with nothing, but carry on doing everything? Is that what a life of virtue deserves? We might romanticize a person like that, but it's not a realistic or fair standard, for others *or* yourself. To do something thankless from time to time isn't bad. I've done thankless things just because it was right. I hope you have as well. But we can go too far in this direction and have it become a problem, the same way any other virtue can become a vice in excess. There's such a thing as being too nice, too forgiving, too generous, and so on. In the past I found I was too modest, and it's a quality I notice often in others. I think many of us are too modest, too afraid of being the nail that stands out, too afraid of being made fun of for being arrogant or full of ourselves, thinking we're better or more important than we really are. To be safe, we pull hard in the other direction. If that sounds like you, please believe me that it's a mental barrier you should make yourself cross. What are you good at? It's a question many people don't answer well. I've known so many people who are afraid to give theirselves credit for anything, even if it's how they feel, even if asked directly. They can't answer, say, "I'm a good painter", even if they are and it's something they've poured countless hours into. They might filter it through other people: "my friends say I'm a good painter". They might say it but only after layers of qualifiers: "well, I'm probably better at painting than most people, as it's something I've practiced a lot, and many people do seem to like what I paint". We're afraid of getting it wrong, of the person we're making these claims to disagreeing and telling us that actually, your paintings are terrible. And then any insult of your bad paintings is amplified by your sin of *thinking* you were good. Then you can be attacked for being full of yourself, thinking too highly of yourself, thinking you're better than other people. It's something our culture can be very punishing of. When I was younger I answered that I was good at typing. To me this felt like a safe answer because it was simple and measurable, not open to interpretation. It felt like if you asked what my good qualities are and I answered "being tall" or "having brown hair". It may evade the spirit of the question depending on how it was phrased, because it's not a hobby or something I'm passionate about, though it is a quality that's positive and which may come in use. I've since become less averse to answering, and would today say probably that I'm very analytical, organized, and a good communicator. I still feel a bit weird describing myself positively, but you *should* be able to do this when asked. A related insight: if somebody is speaking poorly about you, then you probably shouldn't simply look the other way and tolerate it. Sometimes, we should speak up and contradict them, point out inaccuracies, and provide a counter-narrative. If you try to be above-it-all and not reply, people who hate you will slowly chip away at your reputation. If a disagreement or altercation happens and somebody walks away unhappy with you and with a story to tell, then taking the road and avoiding the topic just means the rest of the world hears their side of the story but not yours. I've fallen for this high-road tendency a lot. I'm serially unpetty and don't dramatize small things. When I overhear what other people are worrying or bickering about my takeaway is often that we don't need to do anything, that your gossip may be *interesting* but that no action is required. To address and respond to small things feels unnecessary and may sound like you're caring too much or even legitimizing an accusation or story by taking it seriously. That's how I feel, anyway. But I have to remind myself that doing nothing isn't a winning strategy against people who are persistent. Pettier people will kill you slowly, one paper-cut at a time if they have to. It feels like you're doing the right thing by not getting dragged down into playing their game, but you don't always win by not playing. Sometimes not playing means you lose. (Legal matters also work this way: you can't make lawsuits go away by pretending they don't exist.) And if you're ever accused of wrongdoing, you might want to bring up good things you've done in the past, how you've helped others or made sacrifices, to balance out one's perception of you. But if you wait too long to garner this approval it doesn't work well and may backfire. It may come off as your being defensive, saving face, only doing good now because you want to buy back our approval. Your good actions aren't cheques that you can save up and cash whenever you like. They're more like seeds that you need to be sowing along the way, to build up a positive reputation *before* people start fomenting hatred against you. It takes time. And if they're not growing, because people aren't recognizing you or giving you credit, then you may, uncomfortably or not, need to give yourself credit. Because sometimes when you do things right, people won't be sure you've done anything at all.
