// !article-top "We are now two weeks into the siege of the city of Ottawa." --- Doug Ford, Premier of Ontario, February 2022 In January 2022, a bunch of people drove into Ottawa in big trucks, parked them in the road, and started honking. Its participants and proponents called it the **Freedom Convoy**, though it's commonly referred to simply as "the convoy protest." At its height, Doug Ford called it [the Siege of Ottawa](https://www.cbc.ca/player/play/video/1.6348216) (as did others, like [Yves-François Blanchet](https://www.ourcommons.ca/documentviewer/en/44-1/house/sitting-33/hansard#T1025) or [Gord Johns](https://www.ourcommons.ca/DocumentViewer/en/44-1/house/sitting-21/hansard#T1335)), but even that name undersells it. What was the Freedom Convoy? Why did Poilievre support it, and why was his support for it so controversial? The truckers were supposedly upset about being required to get a vaccine for the COVID-19 pandemic if they wanted to keep their jobs, and they were showing up to protest as a way of letting their grievances be known. This could've been a cause I were sympathetic to, if it were exactly that and no more. Unfortunately, things were not as they seemed. This is my full report. # Preliminary ## Timeline *The pandemic years blur together in many people's minds, and not everybody was keeping up with it at the time. Now, before I move on, let's get something straight: ## Were you forced to get vaccinated? It became required for some jobs. But for most people, no, it was optional. So whether you were forced to get it depends on who you were, and what you mean by "forced." I personally knew multiple people who simply never bothered going to a vaccination site, and there was no penalty, nothing happened to them, nobody tried to compel them to get it. In the West, the countries that went furthest in trying to achieve high vaccination rates were [Austria](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/covid-19-vaccine-austria-parliament-mandate-adults/) and [Ecuador](https://www.reuters.com/world/americas/ecuador-makes-covid-19-vaccination-obligatory-2021-12-23/), where it was directly mandated that you had to get vaccinated or face legal penalty. If you refused and wouldn't budge, I imagine you could *eventually* get out of it --- it was only a civil charge; nobody was ever coming to your house to hold you down and force the needle in you --- but on paper, they did basically say "it's illegal to not be vaccinated," unless you could prove you had a legitimate medical reason to not get one. In the U.S., the federal government [tried to implement a soft mandate through labour regulations](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/biden-tasks-osha-with-vaccine-mandate) by making large employers require their workers to get vaccinated to keep them as employees, though this didn't survive legal challenge. This indirect approach was far more common, technically not making it illegal to be unvaccinated, but making life more difficult, expensive, or inconvenient for people who refused by restricting access to anything they didn't have a legal right to. Tying vaccination status to employment was appealing because it allowed governments to outsource the actual enforcement of vaccines rules to private companies. What portion of labourers were affected varied by country. In Italy it was [nearly all companies](https://www.ctvnews.ca/lifestyle/article/italy-makes-covid-19-health-pass-mandatory-for-all-workers/), while in some other countries, like France, it was [only people who worked in specific fields](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-europe-58581682) --- often government employees, travel, healthcare, and long-term care facilities. I won't go over every country. In many countries it was a mix targetting industries considered high risk. The U.S., UK, and Sweden stood out for not having workplace vaccine mandates at all (in Sweden's case, because [it would conflict with their country's unique constitutional protections](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=da1ed979-cf94-4c33-81bf-4bd738d632f3) against compelled "bodily intrusion"). In Canada, vaccination was [required for workers in the public service](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/federal-vaccine-mandate-1.6201528) and other highly regulated industries, like telecomm and air travel. If you worked at a department store, a software company, a restaurant, a law firm, or any other business outside the scope of where the government focused its efforts, you were probably unaffected. This is why I say Canada didn't have a *general* vaccine mandate, but did have rules to compel people in certain jobs if they wanted to keep those jobs. However, it wasn't impossible for someone in one of those fields to get hired somewhere else and never get vaccinated, if it was truly all-important to them (as it may have been if you thought the vaccine was unsafe and going to seriously harm you). Another area where you could face restrictions if you were unvaccinated was while travelling. The [standard at the time was](https://tc.canada.ca/en/binder/border-measures-general) that if you came from away, either from out of country or moving between provinces, you had to do a COVID-19 test and then were kept away from the general population for 14 days, the standard incubation period for the disease. They didn't weld your door shut, but they would send you to a hotel, have food delivered to your door, and ask you to stay away from large gatherings, including going to see relatives at their home. If you *were* travelling just to see family, you were advised to delay travel altogether, since they realized a two-week buffer on arrival before getting to see anybody was a lot to ask. If you were only coming across the border to deliver freight and go home, that's a different story, so we issued exemptions for essential workers until January 2022, when the Canadian government [eliminated that exemption](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/news/2022/01/requirements-for-truckers-entering-canada-in-effect-as-of-january-15-2022.html), and the U.S. [did the same a week later](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/01/24/2022-01403/notification-of-temporary-travel-restrictions-applicable-to-land-ports-of-entry-and-ferries-service). It was no longer possible to travel back and forth if you weren't vaccinated, unless you were a citizen of both countries. We knew these rules wouldn't be in place forever, but there was no specific timeline given for its expiration when first implemented, so waiting it out wasn't an option. At a glance, you may have thought truckers protesting the border rule had a fair point. Maybe truck drivers who were citizens *should* be exempt from normal procedure after returning from the U.S. as part of their job. It's not a crazy thing to ask. But after the U.S. policy went into effect --- before their protest even began --- the situation was already no longer one the Canadian government could remedy unilaterally. It wouldn't be impossible for the Canadian government to both change its policy and persuade the U.S. to change theirs, but this would take some time, and the purpose of the protest quickly evolved: instead of just complaining about the international border, anybody who looked around at the convoy could tell you attendees were clearly upset about *all* pandemic-related rules. But that wasn't going to work, either. The pandemic rules were broadly popular, and there was no sign of everybody else in the country changing their mind based on the truckers' protest. Some people at the time suggested the government should give in and drop all pandemic rules to satisfy the truckers, but that's not how anything works: the government shouldn't side against the majority just to please a group that decided to put on a protest. So from the onset, this whole thing seemed like a non-starter. It was an oddly expensive and time-consuming way to vent frustration, not a plan to realistically going to get what they wanted --- or at least, that's how it seemed to the rest of us. Nonetheless, they all showed up. ## The right to protest The truckers arrived in Ottawa. Problems began immediately. When we think of protest, we tend to think of people coming together in a public place to deliver a message. They chant, they hold up signs, maybe they ask people to sign petitions. They make their views and grievances heard. And if a law is affecting you unreasonably, but in a way that the majority of the population would be unaware of, that seems like a perfect time for protest to let the rest of us know about what's happening to you. A right to protest is not explicitly mentioned in the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, but it's understood as a natural consequence of the right to free expression plus the right to peaceful assembly, and is considered one of the cornerstones of a democratic society. Though some people online would later falsely claim otherwise, nobody in Canada was ever denied the right to protest throughout this whole ordeal, nor was that right ever generally in question. But the Freedom Convoy was not *merely* engaging in protest, and engaging in protest doesn't make you immune to laws that don't prevent you from protesting. If I started killing people as a form of protest and got arrested for murder, that doesn't mean I've been deprived of my right to protest. It just means protesting doesn't override murder laws, or that murder isn't a legitimate form of protest. If the government passed a law against speaking in public, on the other hand, that law would be challenged because it interferes with the ability to exercise a right to protest, among other things. So while protesting is a universally cherished right that nobody questions, it's not a license to do *anything* you want, and has to be balanced with other concerns. # Occupation days #### The siege of Ottawa !image-float media/streets-blocked.png | Streets still blocked on day 11 of the occupation The main demonstration began on the 31st. Problems began immediately. The longer it went on, the clearer those problems became. The truckers in the Freedom Convoy [had completely blocked off the streets in downtown Ottawa](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-us-canada-60190452). That could be fine for a weekend, at the city's discretion, but not for weeks on end. Most forms of protest don't block off streets at all. They also started [laying on their horns for hours at the time](https://globalnews.ca/video/8580501/trucker-convoy-blaring-horns-cheers-as-hundreds-descend-on-parliament) despite their proxitimy to residential property. The area reportedly [smelled like diesel fumes](https://globalnews.ca/news/10029656/freedom-convoy-trial-ottawa-resident/) because of how many trucks were sitting there idling for so long. The protesters were [committing vandalism](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-us-canada-60190452), intimidating locals, and there was concern about some protesters displaying extremist or hate symbols, including the [Confederate flag](https://www.npr.org/2022/02/10/1079842220/ottawa-trucker-convoy-protest), probably for its loose association with rebellion. Ottawa residents began feeling their own downtown core was rendered unusuable, nearby businesses were all practically forced closed, and as we came into the next week, everyone started realizing this was lasting far longer than anticipated. If these truckers were all out of work, one may have wondered how they afford to drive across the country and then sit around doing this for days on end. But the answer is they had millions of dollars being given to them by people anonymously online, where they were using their social media presence to fundraise. They were having food and jerry cans of fuel brought in to keep it going. They eventually [built makeshift kitchens](https://www.ibtimes.com/canada-protesters-dig-military-style-proficiency-3397485) in the street as it evolved from a protest into an indefinite occupation. It was clear they were not intent on leaving any time soon. !image-float media/coutts-truckers.png | Some of the truckers in Coutts media/coutts-map.png | Location of Coutts, AB **Meanwhile**, the same day truckers convened in Ottawa, another group of truckers showed up and [completely blocked off Alberta Highway 4](https://globalnews.ca/news/9264433/emergencies-act-inquiry-coutts-border-blockades/), which connected to the U.S. state of Montana just south of a border village called **Coutts, Alberta**. The Alberta Premier immediately [called the protest illegal](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-us-canada-60218354). Blocking critical infrastructure in the middle of nowhere lacks the same veneer of legitimacy as protesting in downtown Ottawa in plain view of government buildings. There were over 100 people involved. Back in Ottawa, the public had turned against the protesters. Had I lived in Ottawa then, I'd have been outraged if they weren't at least being given tickets and court dates. Do you know what would happen if I walked downtown right now and sat in traffic to protest something? A police officer would show up and drag me off the street. Unless you get a permit, we don't let people randomly block traffic. So why weren't the police doing anything about the truckers, who were doing that and more? It was a huge nuisance, far beyond what a protest normally permits, and city residents were frustrated that the protest was being given so much lenience. Many people also suggested the police were significantly more lenient with the convoy than they would've been with other protest movements, and this reflects a systemic bias with the police force. On February 1st, day 2 of the occupation, Gord Jones, an MP from B.C., had already [remarked in the House](https://www.ourcommons.ca/DocumentViewer/en/44-1/house/sitting-21/hansard#T1335) that "right now we are hearing from land defenders, from indigenous communities, who see a double standard happening in Canada." And many others would eventually make the same observation, like Daniel Blaikie, a Manitoba MP, who [would later say](https://www.ourcommons.ca/DocumentViewer/en/44-1/house/sitting-33/hansard#T1645) he's "seen police clear out demonstrations of people protesting against free trade agreements and racism and in defence of indigenous rights far more quickly and far more brutally, despite those demonstrations being truly peaceful." It's not inconceivable the Ottawa police were simply biased toward the truckers because the truckers were mostly white men and police naturally love white men, but there are so many other differences between the trucker convoy and the other sorts of demonstrations that Blaikie is contrasting it with that even he couldn't say for sure. #### What went wrong? There were several points of failure in the response. We know more now than we did at the time because there were public inquiries into what went wrong, including the [Public Order Emergency Commission](https://publicorderemergencycommission.ca/final-report/), an inquiry led by Paul Rouleau, a justice for the Court of Appeals for Ontario, who [eventually concluded](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/poec-report-released-friday-1.6750919) "the very high threshold for the invocation" of the Emergency Powers Act "was met." (For international readers, this is not exactly a "provincial court," as Canadian courts are not demarcated in that way. It's located in Ontario, but hears cases pertaining to both provincial and federal law. Rouleau is considered a federal justice.) For one, it was an extraordinary situation, "unprecedented in size and complexity." By Saturday, [thousands of vehicles](https://web.archive.org/web/20220202214635/https://www.ottawapolice.ca//Modules/News/index.aspx?newsId=4e505c6b-0e2e-4a69-a9f0-cfccdcb3e542) had arrived in Ottawa. (A week later, the protest still ongoing, police would say there were still [over 500 heavy vehicles in the area](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/ottawa/ottawa-protests-police-chief-no-end-in-sight-1.6341192).) The police initially guided them into position thinking it would be a normal protest that would naturally conclude after a few days, not realizing what they were in for. An immediate concern after realizing the situation was getting out of control was officer safety, as these protesters outnumbered them, seemed agitated, and were being led by ideologues who were looking for a fight. Police are willing to give some leeway if it reduces the chance of putting theirselves into a situation where they have to respond with force. There was internal "distrust and dysfunction" within [the Ottawa Police Service](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/ottawa/emergencies-act-ottawa-police-intelligence-fail-1.6752239). Intelligence existed but wasn't properly disseminated and acted upon. "Much of the disarray in Ottawa was a result" of their "incorrect belief regarding how long the protests would last," Rouleau's report [said of the OPS command](https://www.ctvnews.ca/ottawa/article/series-of-policing-failures-contributed-to-freedom-convoy-spinning-out-of-control-inquiry-finds/), despite having access to reports indicating "a strong possibility that the Ottawa protests would extend past the first weekend." In at least one case, a police inspector was given reports but didn't believe the contents and thus didn't act on it. Much of blame was put on Peter Sloly, the city's police chief (who [resigned amid backlash](https://globalnews.ca/news/8621091/ottawa-police-peter-sloly-resigns/) on February 15th). Another point of difficulty was removing the trucks. If the protesters saw police were starting to arrest everybody, they could shut off their engines, lock their doors, and hide the keys. These were big trucks with air brakes, meaning the wheels would lock and [it'd be nearly impossible to move them](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/ottawa/ottawa-protest-truck-tow-remove-1.6339652) without a heavy towtruck called a wrecker to inflate the wheels or cage the brakes.
