// !article-top "We are now two weeks into the siege of the city of Ottawa." --- Doug Ford, Premier of Ontario, February 2022 In January 2022, a bunch of people drove into Ottawa in big trucks, parked them in the road, and started honking. Its participants and proponents called it the **Freedom Convoy**, though it's commonly referred to simply as the convoy protest, the trucker protest, the trucker convoy, or some combination of words that doesn't award them their chosen title. At its height, Doug Ford called it [the Siege of Ottawa](https://www.cbc.ca/player/play/video/1.6348216) (others used "siege" to describe it, like [Yves-François Blanchet](https://www.ourcommons.ca/documentviewer/en/44-1/house/sitting-33/hansard#T1025) or [Gord Johns](https://www.ourcommons.ca/DocumentViewer/en/44-1/house/sitting-21/hansard#T1335)), but even that name, if it captures how things felt in Ottawa, undersells the big picture. I will mostly be referring to it as the Freedom Convoy because that was the name given to it by its creators. The questions I'll be answering on this page are: What *was* the Freedom Convoy? Why did it form, what happened, who was behind it, and what did it mean for Canada? Also: why did Poilievre support it, and why was his support for it controversial? The truckers were supposedly upset about being required to get a vaccine for the COVID-19 pandemic if they wanted to keep their jobs, and they were showing up to protest as a way of letting their grievances be known. This could've been a cause I were sympathetic to, if it were exactly that and no more. Unfortunately, things were not as they seemed. In many people's recollection, even those who lived in Ontario and heard about it every day on the news as it happened, the Freedom Convoy has become a blur in our collective memory. There's a lot of lingering misinformation or misunderstanding about what happened, who did what, and why. The story is often reduced to something along the lines of, "truckers came to Ottawa to protest for their jobs, maybe it went on for too long, but the government went too hard on them." # Preliminary ## Timeline First, let's try to understand *when* the convoy emerged in a timeline of events of the pandemic more broadly. *There's additional info about the pandemic on this [WHO timeline](https://www.who.int/news/item/27-04-2020-who-timeline---covid-19). Now, before I move on, let's get something straight: ## Were you forced to get vaccinated? I remember at the time saying Canada didn't have a vaccine mandate, because I knew plenty of people who never got vaccinated and nothing went wrong for them. There were no penalties, nobody tried to make them get it. Vaccination was for the most part optional. However, it became required for some jobs, if you wanted to keep that job. So you could say we mandated it for some jobs, and if that's a vaccine mandate, then you could say we mandated it for some people, or that we had a partial mandate. In the West, the countries that went furthest in trying to achieve high vaccination rates were [Austria](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/covid-19-vaccine-austria-parliament-mandate-adults/) and [Ecuador](https://www.reuters.com/world/americas/ecuador-makes-covid-19-vaccination-obligatory-2021-12-23/), where it was directly mandated that you had to get vaccinated or face legal penalty. If you refused and wouldn't budge, I imagine you could *eventually* get out of it --- it was only a civil charge; nobody was ever coming to your house to hold you down and force the needle in you --- but on paper, they did basically say "it's illegal to not be vaccinated," unless you could prove you had a legitimate medical reason to not get one. In the U.S., the federal government [tried to implement a soft mandate through labour regulations](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/biden-tasks-osha-with-vaccine-mandate) by making large employers require their workers to get vaccinated to keep them as employees, though this didn't survive legal challenge. This indirect approach was far more common, technically not making it illegal to be unvaccinated, but making life more difficult, expensive, or inconvenient for people who refused by restricting access to anything they didn't have a legal right to. Tying vaccination status to employment was appealing because it allowed governments to outsource the actual enforcement of vaccines rules to private companies. Instead of the police coming after you, it was your manager at work you had to deal with. What portion of labourers were affected varied by country. In Italy it was [nearly all companies](https://www.ctvnews.ca/lifestyle/article/italy-makes-covid-19-health-pass-mandatory-for-all-workers/), while in some other countries, like France, it was [only people who worked in specific fields](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-europe-58581682) --- often government employees, travel, healthcare, and long-term care facilities. Typically industries that were already heavily government-regulated, and which we all agreed that a person should have a reasonable expectation of being able to interact with and not face a risk of dealing with unvaccinated employees. I won't go over the details of every country. The U.S., UK, and Sweden stood out for having basically no workplace-related vaccine requirements at all (in Sweden's case, because [it would conflict with their country's unique constitutional protections](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=da1ed979-cf94-4c33-81bf-4bd738d632f3) against compelled "bodily intrusion"). In Canada, vaccination was [required for workers in the public service](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/federal-vaccine-mandate-1.6201528) ("public service" is the Canadian term for the civil service, meaning people who work for the government) and other highly regulated industries, like telecomm and air travel. If you worked at a department store, a software company, a restaurant, a law firm, or any other business outside the scope of where the government focused its efforts, you were probably unaffected. This is why I say Canada didn't have a *general* vaccine mandate, but did have rules to compel people in certain jobs --- if they wanted to keep those jobs. However, it wasn't impossible for someone in one of those fields to get hired somewhere else and never get vaccinated, if avoiding the vaccine was truly all-important to them (as it may have been if you thought the vaccine was dangerous and untested and being pushed by a conspiracy between the government and big pharma, or whatever it was they believed). Another area where you could face restrictions was if you were unvaccinated while travelling. The [standard at the time was](https://tc.canada.ca/en/binder/border-measures-general) that if you came from away, either from out of country or moving between provinces, you had to do a COVID-19 test and then were kept away from the general population for 14 days, the standard incubation period for the disease. They would direct you to a hotel, have food delivered to your door, and ask you to stay away from large gatherings, including going to see relatives at their home. If you *were* travelling just to see family, you were advised to delay travel altogether, since they realized a two-week buffer on arrival before getting to see anybody was a lot to ask. If you were only coming across the border to deliver freight and then go home, that's a different story, so we issued exemptions for essential workers, including licensed truck-drivers --- until January 2022, when the Canadian government [eliminated that exemption](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/news/2022/01/requirements-for-truckers-entering-canada-in-effect-as-of-january-15-2022.html), and the U.S. [did the same a week later](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/01/24/2022-01403/notification-of-temporary-travel-restrictions-applicable-to-land-ports-of-entry-and-ferries-service). It was no longer possible to travel back and forth *at all* if you weren't vaccinated, unless you were a citizen of both countries. We knew these rules wouldn't be in place forever, but there was no specific timeline given for their expiration when first implemented, so waiting it out wasn't an option. ## What was the point? At a glance, you may have thought truckers protesting the border rule had a fair point. Maybe truck drivers who were citizens *should* be exempt from normal procedure after returning from the U.S. as part of their job. It's not a crazy thing to ask. But after the U.S. policy went into effect --- which was before their protest even began --- the situation was already no longer one the Canadian government could remedy unilaterally. It wouldn't be impossible for the Canadian government to both change its policy and persuade the U.S. to change theirs, but this would take some time, and the purpose of the protest quickly evolved. They were not just complaining about the border rule. That was, apparently, only a catalyst to get their attention. By the time they assembled in Ottawa, people were clearly upset about *all* pandemic-related public health rules. But that doesn't make for a good protest, either. The pandemic rules were broadly popular. We just had an election, and the governments that implemented them, both federally and provincially, all won again. There was no sign of everybody else in the country changing their mind based on the truckers' demonstration. There were some people at the time who suggested the government should give in and drop all pandemic rules to satisfy the truckers and make the issue go away, but that's not how anything works: the government shouldn't side against the majority (again, the rules were popular) to please a louder minority opinion just because the latter put on a protest. The Canadian Trucking Alliance, an industry advocate group representing thousands of trucking companies --- which [asked governments to reconsider](https://cantruck.ca/cta-update-on-border-domestic-vaccine-mandates/) their border policies in December --- issued [a statement](https://cantruck.ca/canadian-trucking-alliance-statement-on-road-border-protests/) on January 19, as word about a big trucker protest was first reaching them, saying they don't support "any protests on public roadways, highways and bridges" and encouraged anybody who wanted to express their disagreement with the government's policy to hold a lawful event that would "not disrupt the travelling public." On January 29, as the meet-up in Ottawa grew near, they [issued another statement](https://cantruck.ca/statement-by-canadian-trucking-alliance-president-on-ottawa-protests/) claiming many of the protesters "have no connection to the trucking industry and have a separate agenda beyond a disagreement over cross border vaccine requirements." They also pointed out on the 22nd that [the vast majority of truckers are vaccinated](https://cantruck.ca/canadian-trucking-alliance-statement-to-those-engaged-in-road-border-protests/). Moreover, most of the trucking industry is domestic trucking, not international trucking, and most big companies deal in both, meaning if a small percentage of your fleet couldn't get vaccinated, it would likely be easy to move those drivers to domestic routes. The convoy's narrative didn't align with the facts. From the onset, in multiple ways, this whole thing seemed like a non-starter with no realistic plan for how to get the things they wanted. In the best case scenario, it seemed like it was just going to be an oddly expensive and time-consuming way to vent frustration and show the world how fed up you were about everything, and that's it. Nonetheless, they all showed up. ## The right to protest The truckers arrived in Ottawa. Problems began immediately. When we think of protest, we tend to think of people coming together in a public place to deliver a message. They chant, they hold up signs, maybe they ask people to sign petitions. They make their views and grievances heard. And if a law is affecting you unreasonably, but in a way that the majority of the population would be unaware of, that seems like a perfect time for protest: to inform everybody else about what's happening to you. A right to protest is not explicitly mentioned in [the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/const-index.html), but it's understood as a natural consequence of the right to peaceful assembly plus the right to free expression, and is, as such, a foundational right of any democratic society. Though some people online would later falsely claim otherwise, nobody in Canada was ever denied the right to protest throughout this whole ordeal, nor was that right ever generally in question. But the Freedom Convoy was not *merely* engaging in protest, and engaging in protest doesn't make you immune to laws that don't prevent you from protesting. If I started killing people as a form of protest and got arrested for murder, that doesn't mean I've been deprived of my right to protest. It just means protesting doesn't override murder laws, or that murder isn't a legitimate form of protest. If the government passed a law against speaking in public, on the other hand, that law would be challenged because it interferes with the ability to exercise a right to protest, among other things. So while protesting is a universally cherished right that nobody questions, it's not a license to do *anything* you want, and has to be balanced with other concerns. # Occupation days ## The main protest begins !images float src:media/streets-blocked.png | caption:Streets still blocked on day 9 of the occupation The main demonstration began on the 31st. Problems began immediately. The longer it went on, the clearer those problems became. The truckers in the Freedom Convoy [had completely blocked off the streets in downtown Ottawa](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-us-canada-60190452). That could be fine for a weekend, at the city's discretion, but not for weeks on end. Most forms of protest don't block off streets at all. They also started [laying on their horns for hours at the time](https://globalnews.ca/video/8580501/trucker-convoy-blaring-horns-cheers-as-hundreds-descend-on-parliament) despite their proximity to residential property. Ottawa residents found their own downtown core was rendered unusable, nearby businesses were all practically forced closed, and as we came into the next week, everyone started realizing this was lasting far longer than anticipated. If these truckers were all out of work, one may have wondered how they afford to drive across the country and then sit around doing this for days on end. But the answer is they had millions of dollars being given to them by people anonymously online, where they were using their social media presence to fundraise. They were having food and jerry cans of fuel brought in to keep it going. They eventually [built makeshift kitchens](https://www.ibtimes.com/canada-protesters-dig-military-style-proficiency-3397485) in the street as it evolved from a protest into an indefinite occupation. Blocking critical infrastructure in the middle of nowhere lacks the same veneer of legitimacy as protesting in downtown Ottawa in plain view of government buildings. There were over 100 people involved. Back in Ottawa, the public had turned against the protesters. Had I lived in Ottawa then, I'd have been outraged if they weren't at least being given tickets and court dates. Do you know what would happen if I walked downtown right now and sat in traffic to protest something? A police officer would show up and drag me off the street. Unless you get a permit, we don't let people randomly block traffic. So why weren't the police doing anything about the truckers, who were doing that and more? In particular, for reasons that I discuss in detail in the report, I have concluded that when the decision was made to invoke the Act on February 14, 2022, Cabinet had reasonable grounds to believe that there existed a national emergency arising from threats to the security of Canada that necessitated the taking of special, temporary measures. "I do not come to this conclusion easily, as I do not consider the factual basis for it to be overwhelming. Reasonable and informed people could reach a different conclusion than the one I have arrived at." --- Paul Rouleau, [Feb 17, 2023](https://www.cbc.ca/player/play/video/1.6752402) Rouleau mostly approved of the measures the government enacted after the emergency was declared, though he commented about how the implementation could be better in the future. The way the government froze funds was simply by telling Canadian banks to do so, and the rules of how that was supposed to work should have been clearer to financial institutions. Rouleau also said different levels of government (federal and provincial) did not adequately work together, criticizing the Ontario government in particular. Ontario Premier Doug Ford neglected the situation and didn't do enough for the city of Ottawa, erroneously acting as though it was a federal problem from the start simply because Ottawa is the location of federal government buildings. Though he delivered memorable public remarks, Ford would not become meaningfully involved in what was going on until the blocking of Ambassador Bridge on February 7th, over a week into the occupation. "Minister Blair suggested that federal, provincial, and Ottawa officials come together for regular meetings to discuss and resolve issues at the leadership level. ... "Though Premier Doug Ford and Solicitor General Sylvia Jones were invited to participate, they declined to do so. This was a source of frustration for the other participants, as well as for the Prime Minister. "Prime Minister Trudeau said that 'Doug Ford has been hiding from his responsibility on it for political reasons.' In his testimony before me, the Prime Minister said that he believed the OPP was engaged behind the scenes with the OPS, but that at the political level, there was probably a decision to 'stay back a little bit and let us wear it.'" --- Paul Rouleau, POEC Report, Vol. 3, p. 50 Note that while Rouleau's analysis was interesting and valuable, the inquiry he led was not a court case, and as such it will not bear as much weight in creating precedent for future interpretations as Mosley, below. ### Richard Mosley !files src:https://www.fct-cf.ca/Content/assets/pdf/base/2024.01.23-306-22-T-316-22-T-347-22-T-382-22.pdf |name: Mosley-2024-FC-42.pdf After the POEC final report was issued, two groups, the *Canadian Constitution Foundation* and the *Canadian Civil Liberties Association*, helped [bring a case about this to federal court](https://www.ctvnews.ca/politics/article/federal-court-rules-emergencies-act-invocation-not-justified/), resulting in a federal case called *Canadian Frontline Nurses v. Canada (Attorney General)*, or some longer name. In the resulting decision, [2024 FC 42](https://www.fct-cf.ca/Content/assets/pdf/base/2024.01.23-306-22-T-316-22-T-347-22-T-382-22.pdf), the federal justice Richard Mosley comes to a different conclusion from Rouleau, writing that "the legal constraints on the discretion of the GIC to declare a public order emergency were not satisfied." Mosley agrees the situation was dire: he says "it was reasonable for the GIC to be alarmed" and agrees the protests represented "an unacceptable breakdown of public order." However, he finds there were other things the federal government could have done before resorting to the path they went down, which makes it questionable whether it met the meaning of "emergency" intended by the legislation they invoked. Select quotes: "The [*Emergencies Act*] definition of 'national emergency' requires that emergencies 'transcend' provincial authorities before justifying resort to the Constitution's 'Peace Order and Good Government' emergency power. ... Thus, [the Applicants] contend, emergency powers are only available in times of genuine provincial incapacity and not simply provincial *inaction*. ... "It may be considered unrealistic to expect the Federal Government to wait when the country is 'threatened by serious and dangerous situations'... while the Provinces or Territories determine whether they have the capacity or authority to deal with the threat or, if not, could enact what is lacking in their respective legislative or regulatory tool boxes. However, that is what the *Emergencies Act* appears to require. ... "[T]he Act does not require that there be unanimous agreement from the Provinces before the Federal Government can declare that an emergency exists. But most Premiers informed the Prime Minister that invocation of the Act was not required in their provinces as their legislation and law enforcement authorities could deal with the situation, as they had for example, in Quebec. ... "Due to its nature and to the broad powers it grants the Federal Executive, the *Emergencies Act* is a tool of last resort. The GIC cannot invoke the *Emergencies Act* because it is convenient, or because it may work better than other tools at their disposal or available to the provinces. ... "While I agree that the evidence supports the conclusion that the situation was critical and required an urgent resolution by governments the evidence, in my view, does not support the conclusion that it could not have been effectively dealt with under other laws of Canada, as it was in Alberta, or that it exceeded the capacity or authority of a province to deal with it. That was demonstrated not to be the case in Quebec and other provinces and territories including Ontario, except in Ottawa. ... "For these reasons, I conclude that there was no national emergency justifying the invocation of the Emergencies Act and the decision to do so was therefore unreasonable and *ultra vires*." --- Mosley, 2024 FC 42, pp. 80--87 Thus, even if there were a significant breakdown of the public order, that doesn't make the resulting situation an emergency. It must be that the provincial government is overwhelmed and incapable of doing more. If instead the provincial government chooses to do nothing, that is its prerogative, and the *Emergencies Act* does not empower the federal government to declare an emergency to override the province's decision not to act. And in this case, it appears the provinces were not incapable. Mosley also takes issue with the scope of the emergency: "The Prime Minister's letter to all premiers on February 15, 2022 to outline the reasons why the GIC decided to declare a public order emergency responded to the question of whether the declaration should apply nationally. The letter emphasized that the measures would be applied to targeted areas and would supplement, rather than replace, provincial and municipal authorities. "Section 17(2)(c) of the Act requires that if the effects of the emergency did not extend to the whole of Canada, the area of Canada to which it did extend shall be specified. While the word 'area' in the legislative text is singular, per [section 33(2) of the *Interpretation Act*](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/i-21/page-2.html#279434) that includes the plural. Thus, it was open to the GIC to specify several or many areas that were affected by the emergency excluding others where the situation had not arisen or was under control. "However, the Proclamation stated that it 'exists throughout Canada'. This was, in my view, an overstatement of the situation known to the Government at that time. Moreover, in the first reason provided for the proclamation, which referenced the risk of threats or use of serious violence, language taken from [section 2 of the *CSIS Act*](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/c-23/page-1.html#h-76161), the emergency was vaguely described as happening at 'various locations throughout Canada'." "By applying throughout Canada, the Applicants submit, the Regulations exposed everyone in the country to their reach: the fact that they were not enforced in particular areas is inconsequential because they still applied everywhere." --- Mosley, 2024 FC 42, p. 84, pp. 116--117 Mosley refers to [Section 17(2)(c) of the *Emergencies Act*](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/E-4.5/page-1.html#h-213927), which says: "A declaration of a public order emergency shall specify: if the effects of the emergency do not extend to the whole of Canada, the area of Canada to which the effects of the emergency extend." This means the government was supposed to specify where the order would apply to. In [the official proclamation letter](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/SOR-2022-20/page-1.html), this was not specified. The only reference to a location was in saying the emergency was constituted of "blockades... at various locations throughout Canada," among other concerns. Given these two problems, Mosley concludes: "The Regulations and Economic Order fail the minimal impairment test for two reasons: 1) **they were applied throughout Canada**; and 2) **there were less impairing alternatives available**. The scope of the Declaration and the measures could have been limited to Ontario which faced the most intransigent situation. And possibly Alberta, although the Coutts situation had been resolved when the Act was invoked. ... "The Respondent's position is that it was necessary to apply the measures across Canada because the participants in the several blockades came from across the country, as did their financial support. That may have been a compelling reason if there was evidence that the measures would not have achieved their objective if they did not have effect throughout the country. But that evidence was not part of the Respondent's record." --- Mosley, 2024 FC 42, p. 119 Mosley also addressed whether actions taken by the government during the emergency violated anyone's Charter rights. In some cases he found that it did. He notes the *Emergencies Act* does not invoke the notwithstanding clause, which means that despite the Act bearing "emergency" in its name, it doesn't grant any legal authority to override regular Charter protections. To pick out his conclusions: * **Charter, Section 2: Fundamental freedoms** **Freedom of thought, belief, opinion, expression** "I agree with the Applicants that the scope of the Regulations was overbroad in so far as it captured people who simply wanted to join in the protest by standing on Parliament Hill carrying a placard. ... To the extent that peaceful protestors did not participate in the actions of those disrupting the peace, their freedom of expression was infringed." (p. 105) **Freedom of peaceful assembly** "The legislation clearly permits special measures to prevent public assemblies that will likely lead to a breach of the peace. ... I agree with the Respondent that 'gatherings that employ physical force, in the form of enduring or intractable occupations of public space that block local residents' ability to carry out the functions of their daily lives, in order to compel agreement [with the protestors' objective] are not constitutionally protected.' ... I therefore find no breach of the Charter right of peaceful assembly." (p. 106) **Freedom of association** "In my view, the special measures adopted to deal with the occupation of Ottawa and blockades at other locations did not infringe upon the participants' freedom of association" (p. 107) * **Charter, Section 7: Life, liberty and security of person** "[A] party asserting a violation of section 7 must not only show that the impugned law interfered with or deprived them of their life, liberty or security of the person, which laws do all the time, but also that the deprivation in question is not in accordance with the principles of fundamental justice. In this instance, the deprivation was temporary in nature and subject to judicial review as these proceedings have demonstrated. In the result, I am not prepared to find a breach of section 7." (p. 109) * **Charter, Section 8: Search or seizure** "I find that the failure to require that some objective standard be satisfied before the accounts were frozen breached s. 8. Whether that could be justified in the circumstances depends on a section 1 analysis." (p. 115) What is a section 1 analysis? Section 1 of [the Charter](https://www.canada.ca/content/dam/pch/documents/services/download-order-charter-bill/canadian-charter-rights-freedoms-eng.pdf) says: The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms guarantees the rights and freedoms set out in it subject only to such reasonable limits prescribed by law as can be demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society. This means it's not impossible to justify overriding a Charter right, but the burden to make that argument rests with the party seeking to uphold such a limitation, and it's a high bar that should be enforced strictly. In Canadian constitutional law, the standard is what's now known as [the Oakes test](https://www.constitutionalstudies.ca/2019/07/oakes-test/), originating from the Supreme Court decision [*R. v. Oakes*](https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/117/index.do) (1986). In summary, the infringing law or government action must: 1. be serving an important and urgent purpose; * be rationally connected to the outcome it's trying to achieve; * be minimally impairing; and * be proportional, i.e. it must achieve a social good that is more beneficial than the harm of infringing on protected rights. There is substantial legal discourse about the correct way to interpret each of these points. Select quotes from Mosley's ruling: "Political speech is granted the highest level of protection because of its essential role in democratic life. Since the Regulations directly target a political demonstration and the right to free expression of the protestors, the Applicants submit that the highest level of protection is warranted in this case. "While parked trucks obstructing the roads and blaring horns are not 'high value' speech, the Regulations did not simply prohibit this conduct, which was already illegal under provincial and municipal law, but criminalized the attendance of every single person at those protests regardless of their actions. ... "The Regulations impaired the right to free expression more than was necessary. They captured bystanders who did not agree with the blockades, did not create them and protested in a non-disruptive way. The Regulations also criminalized travelling to a protest where there might have been a blockade, no matter the person's purpose for being there and whether an actual breach of the peace had occurred or not. ... "Having found that the infringements of Charter sections 2(b) and 8 were not minimally impairing, I find that they were not justified under section 1." --- Richard Mosley, 2024 FC 42, pp. 116--120 So the reality of the situation is mixed, but Mosley finds the government did overstep in some places. That being so, some of the actions taken by the government during the emergency period were "unconstitutional" insofar as they restricted Canadians' Charter rights without necessary justification. This doesn't mean everything about [the government response](https://www.publicsafety.gc.ca/cnt/trnsprnc/brfng-mtrls/prlmntry-bndrs/20220625/10-en.aspx) was unjustified. There could also be aspects of the response that are unjustified, but which also don't infringe on Charter rights. As noted earlier, for example, Mosley finds the situation in February did not satisfy the meaning of "emergency" as would be required by the *Emergencies Act* particularly, which means the government was not justified to declare a public order emergency. However, this does not infringe on Charter rights. ### Freezing accounts After the pandemic was over, the most controversial part of the government's response was how the RCMP ordered Canadian banks to freeze the accounts of people they named, which they believed were involved in, or involved in funding, the occupation, or any of the blockades. I suspect this was given so much emphasis because every other aspect of the government's response was reasonably defensible. In the two years before Mosley's conclusion was released, it would have been difficult to persuade the average person through common sense alone to think the government went too far by arresting people who blockaded an area with heavy vehicles and refused to leave for weeks after being told they were breaking the law. Whether the government went too far by also going after those people's bank accounts was an easier case to make. Rouleau said it seemed appropriate and effective: "The prohibition on providing material support to individuals involved in illegal protests was appropriate. The Ottawa protests were only able to become as entrenched as they did through extensive contributions of support from others. Cash donations allowed for the purchase of hotel rooms and fuel. Sophisticated operations like Adopt-A-Trucker provided food, showers, and shelter. All of these contributed to sustaining the protests. It was appropriate for the Government to attempt to cut off this support to encourage protesters to depart before the police resorted to the use of force. ... "I also have no difficulty in concluding that this measure was effective. Although I have found that few of the online donations ever reached the protesters, there were many other ways that third parties provided material support that sustained the protests. When this provision was taken together with other measures, such as the asset freezing regime under the EEMO, I conclude that it played a role in reducing the level of support received by the protesters, which in turn reduced their capacity to continue with the protests. This likely had at least some impact on the footprint of the protests prior to police enforcement action." --- Paul Rouleau, POEC final report, Vol. 1, p. 237 Mosley, however, came to a different conclusion, albeit because he was asking different questions. On February 15, 2022, a day after the public order emergency was declared, the government [issued an order to the governor general](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/SOR-2022-22/page-1.html) called the **Emergency Economic Measures Order**, SOR/2022-22. In his judgment, Mosley notes [the RCMP passed along info](https://www.fct-cf.ca/Content/assets/pdf/base/2024.01.23-306-22-T-316-22-T-347-22-T-382-22.pdf#page=20) about "57 named entities," resulting in **257 accounts** being temporarily frozen under the order. Mosley writes: "Section 8 provides that everyone has the right to be secure against unreasonable search or seizure. "The issue here, the Applicants submit, is whether the law that authorized the search, the Economic Order, was reasonable. They submit a law will be reasonable when it reasonably balances the importance of the state objective with the degree of impact on the individual's privacy interest ... "The Applicants argue that two of the provisions of the Economic Order contravene *Charter* section 8. First, section 2(1) of the Economic Order empowered financial institutions to freeze the assets of any designated person, which constitutes a seizure within the meaning of *Charter* s. 8. Second, section 5 of the Economic Order required financial institutions to disclose private information, such as what money people have and how they spent it, regarding designated persons, to the RCMP or CSIS. That is a search, the Applicants contend. "The Respondent contends that the Economic Order did not authorize activity that constitutes a 'seizure' within the meaning of Charter s. 8." --- Richard Mosley, 2024 FC 42, pp. 109--111 .Here "applicant" means the party suing, while "Respondent" means the party being sued. In this context, the Respondent is the government, as represented by various civil servants representing council for the Attorney General. It seems like the government did not argue about whether its order constituted a search, but it did contend whether the order to freeze accounts was a seizure. This leads to a dispute about the very meaning of seizure for the purpose of interpreting [section 8 of the Charter](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/const/page-12.html) (often written as "s. 8"), which simply says: **Legal Rights** ...
**8.** Everyone has the right to be secure against unreasonable search or seizure. Mosley looks at two cases brought to the Supreme Court of Canada where "seizure" had to be interpreted and the court explicitly included a definition in their ruling: 1. In [*R. v. Dyment*](https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/375/index.do) (1988), a man got into a traffic collision and was being treated at a hospital. A doctor took a blood sample while the man was unconscious. Later, the man told the doctor they had a beer prior to drinking. Later still, the doctor gave the blood sample to police, which was used to convict him for driving over the legal alcohol limit. The man did not consent to the blood sample being taken nor, to the blood sample being given to police. As part of its ruling, the court said "the essence of a seizure under s. 8 is the taking of a thing from a person by a public authority without that person's consent" (also adding that "even if he had given his consent, I do not think that would have mattered if the consent was restricted to the use of the sample for medical purposes"). 2. In [*Thomson Newspapers Ltd. v. Canada*](https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/583/index.do) (1990), the government ordered officers of Thomson Newspapers (a predecessor to the modern Thomson Reuters, which owns the Reuters news agency) to appear before a commission and answer questions about their employer under oath as part of an investigation into the company, pursuant to the now-defunct *Combines Investigation Act* of 1923. As part of its ruling, the court referenced the *R. v. Dyment* definition: "The essence of a seizure under s. 8 is the taking of a thing from a person by a public authority without that person's consent. An order to produce documents under [s. 17 of the Act], therefore, constitutes a seizure within the meaning of s. 8. But [such a] seizure is not unreasonable." It also penned a similar definition, worded anew: "A seizure under s. 8 is the taking by a public authority of a thing belonging to a person against that person's will. Applying a purposive interpretation of s. 8, the compulsory production of documents in a criminal or quasi‑criminal law context falls within that definition. "Whether the public authority 'takes' the documents or compels the person to hand them over, the impact on the person's right to privacy in the documents is the same." There is ambiguity here. It could be reasonably argued that by ordering bank accounts frozen, the RCMP did not *take* anyone's property, i.e. the money was not transferred to the government's possession. It was still in the bank accounts of the people targeted, or, perhaps we could say, it was still being held by their banks. Is this the same as the above cases? One could argue it is not. Moreover, in the *Thomson* case, the principal concern was a right to *privacy*, not a right to property. In both cases, the infringement was a matter of information being made available to the government that the subject did not want to disclose. This, too, is unlike the case concerning the invocation of the *Emergencies Act*. But where there is ambiguity, the text does not explicitly cut in the other direction, either; and it's Mosley, not I, who is in a position of authority to settle that ambiguity. And his opinion was to say "governmental action that results in the content of a bank account being unavailable to the owner of the said account would be understood by most members of the public to be a 'seizure' of that account" (2024 FC 42, p. 112). Mosley also ruled that by requiring banks to provide financial information about designated persons to the RCMP, this, too, is an unreasonable search or "seizure of information" noting that financial info "can reveal personal details about someone such as their financial status and lifestyle choices," and those affected should have had the expectation of privacy under section 8 of the Charter. Referencing another SCC case, [*R. v. MacKenzie*](https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/13265/index.do) (2013), reasonable suspicion must be "grounded in 'objectively discernible facts, which can then be subjected to independent judicial scrutiny.'" Mosley found the RCMP simply gave names to financial institutions, which were then required to disclose information; no objective criteria was articulated; instead, the RCMP operated as though the requirement was only "bare belief" (2024 FC 42, p. 114). This will be the law going forward, unless Parliament chooses to amend the constitution to clarify otherwise, or unless the Supreme Court of Canada chooses to disagree with Mosley. Neither has happened at time of writing. ## The government response to Mosley Some people quickly [argued Mosley's conclusion is flawed](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/emergencies-act-appeal-1.7450394) because Mosley was revisiting these events two years later with perfect hindsight, but this misunderstands the nature of the ruling. Mosley is not condemning the government nor recommending anybody in Cabinet be prosecuted. And Mosley acknowledges, of course, that he's revisiting events with a more informed perspective than the government had at the time. As Mosley writes: "I had and continue to have considerable sympathy for those in government who were confronted with this situation. Had I been at their tables at that time, I may have agreed that it was necessary to invoke the Act. And I acknowledge that in conducting judicial review of that decision, I am revisiting that time with the benefit of hindsight and a more extensive record of the facts and law than that which was before the GIC." --- Richard Mosley, [2024 FC 42, p. 123](https://www.fct-cf.ca/Content/assets/pdf/base/2024.01.23-306-22-T-316-22-T-347-22-T-382-22.pdf#page=123) Mosley's role is to provide analysis about whether the law was followed correctly, given his reading of the law and his interpretation of the facts that were made available to him; and to rule on the specific case that was brought to him by the applicant. In this case, he awarded financial relief to some of the people who had been unjustly affected by the emergency orders. This ruling will now exist to serve as precedent to guide future governments about how to apply the *Emergencies Act*. Chrystia Freeland, then Deputy Prime Minister, also [responded at a press conference](https://www.ctvnews.ca/politics/article/federal-court-rules-emergencies-act-invocation-not-justified/) to the initial reporting of Mosley's conclusion, saying, "The public safety of Canadians was under threat. Our national security, which includes our national economic security, was under threat. I was convinced at the time it was the right thing to do, it was the necessary thing to do. I remain and we remain convinced of that." These comments are clearly oriented toward defending the public reputation of Trudeau's Cabinet, not toward contradicting the opinion of the justice. It is possible to believe at once that Freeland's comments here are accurate but that the government, though well-meaning, acted in error and overstepped its strict legal boundaries. The government said they plan to appeal the decision to the Supreme Court to seek further perspective and clarification. # My opinion *Was the government's response justified?* There were some aspects of Mosley's ruling I found questionable --- such as how Mosley reasons the orders against gathering didn't unreasonably restrict freedom of peaceful assembly, but *did* unreasonably restrict freedom of expression because it stopped people from gathering to express their views; and the logic in saying the federal government cannot reasonably declare a public order emergency only because of provincial *inaction* as opposed to incapacity when, [according to Rouleau's inquiry](https://publicorderemergencycommission.ca/files/documents/Final-Report/Vol-3-Report-of-the-Public-Inquiry-into-the-2022-Public-Order-Emergency.pdf#page=101), the Ontario premier supported the invocation and communicated to other relevant authorities that dealing with the Ambassador bridge blockade had consumed all of their resources (was that not true? should the federal government not have believed him?); and how Mosley applied the meaning of "seizure." If somebody had asked me about these aspects of the government's response prior to Mosley's ruling, I would not have answered in the way Mosley eventually did. That is not to say I'm right and Mosley is wrong. Mosley was an utmost reasonable and educated man, and served on the Federal Court of Canada for over 20 years. There may well be things he knows or understands better than I do, which leads to him having a different intuitions about some things. I would have no business claiming that my intuition about a question of law ought to override his. In all ways, I respect his ruling. Disagreement about those specific questions notwithstanding, I don't know if I'd have reached a different conclusion about the big-picture question of whether the government was justified in declaring a public order emergency, if I had been given to understand the *Emergencies Act* in the way Mosley presents. The impression I get, after reading through Rouleau's final report, and still after Mosley's ruling, is simply that nobody in government knew the law in great detail. This is not surprising, as the *Emergencies Act* had never been invoked before, and some of the legal questions Mosley explores would not be knowledge government officials have offhand. If they had more time, we might have expected them to consult with lawyers, but instead each person was acting primarily on political instinct while trying to work with other parts of government on an official response. Rouleau [mentions a CSIS director advising the Prime Minister](https://publicorderemergencycommission.ca/files/documents/Final-Report/Vol-1-Report-of-the-Public-Inquiry-into-the-2022-Public-Order-Emergency.pdf#page=207) *incorrectly* about how to interpret some of the language in the text of the *Emergencies Act*, for example. At the end of the day, I believe Trudeau and his cabinet made a good faith attempt to follow the law, and I've seen nothing to indicate the inverse, that they were flippant or not taking the law seriously. If we *don't* ask the question of whether the government's response followed the letter of the law, almost everything that remains appears reasonable: the government was restrained and tried many avenues to address the situation, not being too hasty to react; they [organized meetings with the premiers of every province](https://publicorderemergencycommission.ca/files/documents/Final-Report/Vol-3-Report-of-the-Public-Inquiry-into-the-2022-Public-Order-Emergency.pdf#page=101) to keep everybody informed and coördinate their efforts; they resorted to declaring an emergency only after weeks of inaction, after it seemed like the problem was not able to be solved without it (and that's what people were telling them); even after invoking emergency powers, they were restrained and measured in trying to bring the situation to a close, not charging in with reckless, overwhelming force; the emergency was confirmed democratically by Parliament; the emergency powers were narrowly enforced, taking feedback from provincial premiers about where it was needed; the emergency was ended quickly, as soon as the government felt it was no longer needed; nothing was delayed or unnecessarily stretched out; they didn't do anything to take advantage of the emergency unfairly other than using it to end the crisis. If Richard Mosley says what they ended up doing was not as the law required, I believe him, but that is not exactly the same question as how I feel about the people in government for making the choices they did. I think nearly anybody, if they were to sit down and analyze the choices Cabinet made, would get the impression these were reasonable people trying to do what was right. They were not acting tyrannically, they were trying to be responsible, they were not excited about the ability to use emergency powers. Overall, my opinion of our government did not decline meaningfully as a result of how they handled the emergency. The legal problem with freezing accounts ended up being the RCMP didn't apply a standard of reasonable suspicion when choosing whose accounts to freeze. This means the court is *not* conceding that your bank account is a special, sacred thing the government can never lock away from you, if you're known to be engaging in criminal activity, as some of the government's critics imply. Indeed, it would have been legal to order holds on *some* of the accounts they did; the problem was only there was also collateral damage, that is, people who were innocent being accidentally affected as well due to their targeting selection not being sufficiently specific. But even if it were all legally justified and they did a better job narrowing their focus to only people they were more certain were engaging in criminal activity, freezing accounts temporarily as a way of applying pressure on those involved in the occupation might not be an effective tactic. I have mixed feelings about it in that respect. In the future, if this type of thing were to be attempted again, perhaps protesters will have started using crypto-currency technology that the RCMP cannot simply order a hold on. It was probably reasonable to open a broad investigation to follow the money and figure out who is financing groups knowingly engaging in illegal activity, at least. I lived in Ontario when the Freedom Convoy arrived in Ottawa. I followed the events closely on the news as things unfolded. It did not occur to me to think that handling this protest would be a federal responsibility instead of the city or, failing that, the province. I was thus dissatisfied with the response by city and provincial police. In the Ontario legislature, the opposition, then led by Andrea Horwath, strongly criticized our premier Doug Ford for his inaction. In 2022, I recall being pleased when Doug Ford started issuing unambiguous public condemnation of the occupation. But reviewing the whole situation years later with more information and perspective than the public had access to at the time, I consider Ford to be one of the worst actors in all this. I have come to believe the following: * Ford didn't want to be too critical of the truckers, likely because he viewed them as a right-wing social force that was more likely to support him than not. * Ford didn't care about Ottawa at all. He viewed the Ottawa occupation as an anti-Trudeau protest, and thus was happy to neglect his responsibilities as premier and let Trudeau suffer. * He only issued those strong statements after people blocked off the Ambassador Bridge, because that was suddenly not about Trudeau, and was more harmful to the Ontario economy, thus he viewed it as his problem. * His strongest statements, threatening people with fines and jail time, were made only about the people blocking the Ambassador Bridge. He still didn't care about Ottawa. * The federal government's emergency declaration probably completely distracted the public from giving Doug Ford the blame that he deserved for how he acted throughout all this.
