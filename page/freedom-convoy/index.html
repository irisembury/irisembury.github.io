// "We are now two weeks into the siege of the city of Ottawa." --- Doug Ford, Premier of Ontario, February 2022 In January 2022, a bunch of people drove into Ottawa in big trucks, parked them in the road, and started honking. Its participants and proponents called it the **Freedom Convoy**, though it's commonly referred to simply as "the convoy protest." I like to call it the Siege of Ottawa, because [that's what Doug Ford called it](https://www.cbc.ca/player/play/video/1.6348216) at its height. What was the Freedom Convoy? Why did Poilievre support it, and why was his support for it so controversial? The truckers were supposedly upset about being required to get a vaccine for the COVID-19 pandemic if they wanted to keep their jobs, and they were showing up to protest as a way of letting their grievances be known. This could've been a cause I were sympathetic to, if it were exactly what it sounds like --- but unfortunately, things were not as they seemed. # Timeline: the Pandemic and the Convoy *The pandemic years blur together in many people's minds, and not everybody was keeping up with it at the time. Let's ensure we're all on the same page with a timeline of relevant events. There's more info on this [WHO timeline](https://www.who.int/news/item/27-04-2020-who-timeline---covid-19).* #### 2020 * **January**: We first began seeing [reporting about](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-china-50984025) a "viral pneumonia" detected in Wuhan, China the previous year spreading to our countries. By the end of the month, the WHO [convened an emergency committee](https://www.who.int/news/item/30-01-2020-statement-on-the-second-meeting-of-the-international-health-regulations-(2005\)-emergency-committee-regarding-the-outbreak-of-novel-coronavirus-(2019-ncov\)) and declared the matter an emergency of international concern. * **February**: The WHO [officially named](https://www.who.int/emergencies/diseases/novel-coronavirus-2019/technical-guidance/naming-the-coronavirus-disease-(covid-2019\)-and-the-virus-that-causes-it) the virus SARS-CoV-2 (severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2, a sister virus to the [2003 outbreak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SARS) simply called SARS) and the disease COVID-19 (coronavirus disease of 2019). * **March**: The WHO announced the virus [had become a pandemic](https://www.nytimes.com/2020/03/11/health/coronavirus-pandemic-who.html). As cases emerged across the country, every province and territory in Canada **declared a state of emergency**, as did many towns and cities. At this point, provincial governments [began ordering the closure](https://globalnews.ca/news/6688074/ontario-doug-ford-coronavirus-covid-19-march-17/) of many non-essential places of gathering. * **December**: Pfizer and Moderna both finished phase 3 clinical trials for their vaccines, which were authorized for use by Health Canada. The very first doses that were available that month were [given to care workers](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/first-doses-covid-19-vaccine-1.5839614). #### 2021 * **January, February**: Vaccines started being distributed to the public, starting with seniors. In Ontario, booking eligibility [was expanded to all people 18 or older](https://news.ontario.ca/en/release/1000143/covid-19-vaccine-booking-expanding-to-ontarians-18-ahead-of-schedule) in **May 2021**, marking the start of the mass vaccine rollout and push toward herd immunity. * As the year progressed, some rules began changing, but the details varied by province, mostly for political reasons. * In **Ontario**, for example, there had been a general ban on indoor assembly, affecting things like gyms and restaurants, but in **September** [that was partially lifted](https://news.ontario.ca/en/backgrounder/1000806/using-your-vaccination-receipt-frequently-asked-questions) for people with proof of vaccination, who were temporarily allowed access to such things early. * The most restrictive policies were probably in **Quebec**, where [they twice implemented nightly curfews](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/montreal/quebec-pandemic-curfew-1.7470532) during particularly bad waves of infections, and instead of only requiring proof of vaccination for indoor seating at restaurants, it was also required for high-density foot traffic areas like Walmart. * In **the prairies**, pandemic rules were less popular, and the response by provincial governments reflected that to some extent (see [Alberta's "Open for Summer" plan](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/edmonton/jason-kenney-deena-hinshaw-covid-reopening-update-1.6070925) for example). They tried to avoid using the term "vaccine passport," and they let people be seated at indoor gatherings without being vaccinated if they provided proof of a negative COVID-19 test (see Saskatchewan's policy [here](https://www.saskatchewan.ca/government/news-and-media/2021/september/28/proof-of-vaccination-or-negative-tests--required-broadly-october-1-2021)). In Alberta, the government [offered to pay people $100](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/calgary/poll-angus-reid-alberta-prairies-vaccine-passport-1.6168075) to get vaccinated, trying to use reward instead of punishment. * By the fall, most provincial governments had [laid out a plan](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/ontario-covid-exit-roadmap-reopen-capacity-limits-lifted-1.6220653) for how bring pandemic policies to a close, though many had to change course after [the Omicron variant of the virus hit Canada unexpected hard](https://www.cbc.ca/news/health/omicron-canada-world-subvariants-covid-vaccination-hybrid-immunity-1.6680620) in December 2021 and January 2022. (The disease was milder but the virus was far more contagious, and we realized the vaccine didn't protect from it, forcing health organizations to rethink their approach.) * **October 29**: The U.S. government said [it planned to end the exemption](https://www.dhs.gov/archive/news/2021/10/29/dhs-releases-details-fully-vaccinated-non-citizen-travelers-enter-us-land-and-ferry) for "essential travel" to vaccine requirements for non-citizens entering their country starting in January 2022. * **November**: The Canadian government [announced they would implement the same border policy as the U.S.](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/news/2021/11/adjustments-to-canadas-border-and-travel-measures.html) come January, indicating that "essential service providers including truck drivers" will no longer be exempt at that time. * **December 2021**: The group behind the convoy, which called itself **Canada Unity**, [published a document](https://web.archive.org/web/20220123051114/https://canada-unity.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Combined-MOU-Dec03.pdf) calling for the Canadian Senate and Governor General to overrule the House of Commons and the Ministry, to end all vaccine rules in the country, and to rehire anybody whose employment was terminated for reasons related to vaccine rules. They also demanded the creation of a new, parallel authority, the "Citizens of Canada Committee," and said if the Senate and Governor General don't do this, they demand all involved "resign their lawful positions of authority immediately." #### 2022 * **January 15**: The government announced a change to how international travel rules worked, [eliminating the exemption for essential workers](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/news/2022/01/requirements-for-truckers-entering-canada-in-effect-as-of-january-15-2022.html). If you had proof of vaccination when entering the country, you could enter normally. If you didn't, then non-citizens were denied entry, and citizens were allowed to return home, were subject to requirements like testing or quarantine. * **January 22**: The United States begins enforcing [the same policy](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/01/24/2022-01403/notification-of-temporary-travel-restrictions-applicable-to-land-ports-of-entry-and-ferries-service), not allowing unvaccinated non-citizens across their border. At this point, it's effectively impossible to work as an international truck-driver if you're not vaccinated because one country or the other won't let you in. * **January 23--30**: Truckers across Canada start driving to Ottawa. They pass through [numerous Canadian cities](https://www.ctvnews.ca/winnipeg/article/convoy-opposing-trucker-vaccine-mandate-draws-large-crowd-concerns-over-comparison-to-holocaust/) fundraising and advertising their cause. There ended up being [a few different convoys](https://www.ctvnews.ca/ottawa/article/truck-convoy-rolls-into-kingston-ont/) driving around before they converged in Ottawa. * **January 31:** The Freedom Convoy trucker convoy protest arrive in Ottawa near Parliament Hill for the final weekend of the month. It was estimated [to be about 3,000 people](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/ottawa/ottawa-protest-convoy-length-ticket-tow-mayor-1.6333497) present. Some people would remain until mid-February, occupying the area for about three weeks. * **February 2**: Erin O'Toole was [voted out as Conservative Party leader](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-us-canada-60236191), only a few days into the protest. * **March**: Nearly all *domestic* pandemic rules were phased out in or around March, varying by province. The trucker protest had no apparent effect on the timeline, which had been laid out months in advance. The earliest was Saskatchewan, which [ended all pandemic-related public health orders](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/saskatchewan/public-health-orders-saskatchewan-february-28-1.6366407) on February 28. In mid-February, the Ontario government [announced](https://news.ontario.ca/en/release/1001600/ontario-moving-to-next-phase-of-reopening-on-february-17) it would meet its initial timeline, and [ended almost all pandemic-related public health orders](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/covid19-ontario-march-9-mask-mandates-1.6378148) on March 21. Some minor restrictions lingered around into April. * **May 5**: The Conservative Party held a [leadership debate](https://www.cpac.ca/public-record/episode/conservative-leadership-debate-in-ottawa--may-5-2022?id=0f0d5ab9-fba2-4162-b24b-59d1c16c3b14), at which Jean Charest criticized Poilievre for supporting the convoy protest, and Poilievre hit back defending them. * **October**: Federal border policies related to the pandemic [phased out](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/news/2022/09/government-of-canada-to-remove-covid-19-border-and-travel-measures-effective-october-1.html). * **September 10**: Pierre Poilievre [declared the winner](https://www.ctvnews.ca/politics/article/pierre-poilievre-wins-conservative-leadership-on-first-ballot/) of the Conservative Party's leadership race. // https://cantruck.ca/statement-by-canadian-trucking-alliance-president-on-ottawa-protests/ # Were you forced to get vaccinated? For some jobs, yes, if you wanted to keep that job. For most people, no, it was optional. I knew multiple people who simply never bothered going to a vaccination site, and there was no penalty, nothing happened to them, nobody tried to compel them to get it. In the West, the countries that went furthest in trying to achieve high vaccination rates were [Austria](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/covid-19-vaccine-austria-parliament-mandate-adults/) and [Ecuador](https://www.reuters.com/world/americas/ecuador-makes-covid-19-vaccination-obligatory-2021-12-23/), where it was directly mandated that you had to get vaccinated or face legal penalty. If you refused and wouldn't budge, I imagine you could *eventually* get out of it --- it was only a civil charge; nobody was ever coming to your house to hold you down and force the needle in you --- but on paper, they did basically say "it's illegal to not be vaccinated," unless you could prove you had a legitimate medical reason to not get one. In the U.S., the federal government [tried to implement a soft mandate through labour regulations](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/biden-tasks-osha-with-vaccine-mandate) by making large employers require their workers to get vaccinated to keep them as employees, though this didn't survive legal challenge. This indirect approach was far more common, technically not making it illegal to be unvaccinated, but making life more difficult, expensive, or inconvenient for people who refused by restricting access to anything they didn't have a legal right to. Tying vaccination status to employment was appealing because it allowed governments to outsource the actual enforcement of vaccines rules to private companies. What portion of labourers were affected varied by country. In Italy it was [nearly all companies](https://www.ctvnews.ca/lifestyle/article/italy-makes-covid-19-health-pass-mandatory-for-all-workers/), while in some other countries, like France, it was [only people who worked in specific fields](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-europe-58581682) --- often government employees, travel, healthcare, and long-term care facilities. In many countries it was a mix targetting industries considered high risk. The U.S., UK, and Sweden stood out for not having workplace vaccine mandates at all (in Sweden's case, because [it would conflict with their country's unique constitutional protections](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=da1ed979-cf94-4c33-81bf-4bd738d632f3) against compelled "bodily intrusion"). In Canada, we [required vaccination of workers in the public service](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/federal-vaccine-mandate-1.6201528) and in other highly regulated industries, like telecomm and air travel. If you worked at a department store, a software company, a restaurant, a law firm, or any other business outside the scope of where the government focused its efforts, you were probably unaffected. This is why I say Canada didn't have a *general* vaccine mandate, but did have rules to compel people in certain jobs if they wanted to keep those jobs. However, it wasn't impossible for someone in one of those fields to get hired somewhere else and never get vaccinated, if it was truly all-important to them (as it may have been if you thought the vaccine was unsafe and going to seriously harm you). Another area where you could face restrictions if you were unvaccinated was while travelling. The [standard at the time was](https://tc.canada.ca/en/binder/border-measures-general) that if you came from away, either from out of country or moving between provinces, you had to do a COVID-19 test and then were kept away from the general population for 14 days, the standard incubation period for the disease. They didn't weld your door shut, but they would send you to a hotel, have food delivered to your door, and ask you to stay away from large gatherings, including going to see relatives at their home. If you *were* travelling just to see family, you were advised to delay travel altogether, since they realized a two-week buffer on arrival before getting to see anybody was a lot to ask. If you were only coming across the border to deliver freight and go home, that's a different story, so we issued exemptions for essential workers until January 2022, when the Canadian government [eliminated that exemption](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/news/2022/01/requirements-for-truckers-entering-canada-in-effect-as-of-january-15-2022.html), and the U.S. [did the same a week later](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/01/24/2022-01403/notification-of-temporary-travel-restrictions-applicable-to-land-ports-of-entry-and-ferries-service). It was no longer possible to travel back and forth if you weren't vaccinated, unless you were a citizen of both countries. We knew these rules wouldn't be in place forever, but there was no specific timeline given for its expiration when first implemented, so waiting it out wasn't an option. At a glance, you may have thought they had a point. Maybe truck drivers who were citizens *should* be exempt from normal procedure after returning from the U.S. as part of their job. But after the U.S. policy went into effect, which was *before* their protest began, the situation was already no longer one the Canadian government could remedy unilaterally. It wouldn't be *impossible* for the Canadian government to both change its policy *and* persuade the U.S. to change theirs, but this would take some time, and the purpose of the protest quickly evolved: instead of just complaining about the border, attendees were clearly upset about *all* pandemic-related laws. But that wasn't going to work either, because pandemic rules were broadly popular, and there was no sign of everybody else in the country changing their mind based on the truckers' protest. So from the onset, this whole thing seemed like a non-starter --- an oddly expensive and time-consuming way to vent frustration. # The truckers arrive in Ottawa Problems began immediately. When we think of protest, we tend to think of people coming together in a public place to deliver a message. They chant, they hold up signs, maybe they ask people to sign petitions. They make their views and grievances heard. And if a law is affecting you unreasonably, but in a way that the majority of the population would be unaware of, that seems like a perfect time for protest, to let the rest of us know about what's happening to you. A right to protest is not explicitly mentioned in the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, but it's understood as a natural consequences of the right to free expression plus the right to peaceful assembly, and is considered one of the cornerstones of a democratic society. Though some people online would falsely claim otherwise, nobody in Canada was ever denied the right to protest throughout this whole ordeal, nor was that right ever generally in question. But the Freedom Convoy was not *merely* engaging in protest, and engaging in protest doesn't make you immune to laws that don't prevent you from protesting. If I started killing people as a form of protest and got arrested for murder, that doesn't mean I've been deprived of my right to protest. It just means protesting doesn't override murder laws, or that murder isn't a legitimate form of protest. If the government passed a law against speaking in public, on the other hand, that law would be challenged because it interferes with the ability to exercise a right to protest, among other things. So while protesting is a universally cherished right that nobody questions, it's not a license to do *anything* you want, and has to be balanced with other concerns. !image-float media/streets-blocked.png | Streets still blocked on day 11 of the protests To begin with, truckers in the Freedom Convoy [had completely blocked off the streets in downtown Ottawa](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-us-canada-60190452). That could be fine for a weekend, at the city's discretion, but not for weeks on end. Most forms of protest don't block off streets at all. They also started [laying on their horns for hours at the time](https://globalnews.ca/video/8580501/trucker-convoy-blaring-horns-cheers-as-hundreds-descend-on-parliament) despite their proxitimy to residential property. The area reportedly [smelled like diesel fumes](https://globalnews.ca/news/10029656/freedom-convoy-trial-ottawa-resident/) because of how many trucks were sitting there idling for so long. The protesters were [committing vandalism](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-us-canada-60190452), intimidating locals, and there was concern about some protesters displaying extremist or hate symbols, including the [Confederate flag](https://www.npr.org/2022/02/10/1079842220/ottawa-trucker-convoy-protest), probably for its loose association with rebellion. Ottawa residents began feeling their own downtown core was rendered unusuable, nearby businesses were all practically forced closed, and as we came into the next week, everyone started realizing this was lasting far longer than anticipated. If these truckers were all out of work, how could they afford to drive across the country and then sit around doing this for days on end? Did they come in with trucks full of supplies, or were they having food and jerry cans of fuel brought in to keep it going? They didn't seem intent on leaving any time soon. !image-float media/coutts-truckers.png | Some of the truckers in Coutts media/coutts-map.png | Coutts, AB location Meanwhile, the same day truckers convened in Ottawa, other groups of truckers associated with them arrived [arrived and completely blocked off Alberta Highway 4](https://globalnews.ca/news/9264433/emergencies-act-inquiry-coutts-border-blockades/), which connected to the U.S. state of Montana just south of a border village called Coutts, Alberta. The Alberta Premier immediately [called the protest illegal](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-us-canada-60218354), as blocking critical infrastructure in the middle of nowhere lacks the same veneer of legitimacy as protesting in downtown Ottawa in plain view of government buildings. There were over 100 people involved. Back in Ottawa, the public turned against the protest quickly. If I lived there at the time, I'd have been outraged if they weren't at least being given tickets and court dates. Do you know what would happen if I walked downtown right now and sat in traffic to protest something? A police officer would show up and drag me off the street. Unless you get a permit, we don't let people randomly block traffic. So why weren't the police doing anything about the truckers, who were doing that and more? It was a huge nuisance, far beyond what a protest normally permits, and city residents were frustrated that the protest was being given so much lenience. There were several points of failure in the response. We know more now than we knew at the time because there were public inquiries into what went wrong, including the [Public Order Emergency Commission](https://publicorderemergencycommission.ca/final-report/), an inquiry led by Paul Rouleau, a justice for the Court of Appeals for Ontario, who [eventually concluded](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/poec-report-released-friday-1.6750919) "the very high threshold for the invocation" of the Emergency Powers Act "was met." (For international readers: this is not a "provincial court," as Canadian courts are not demarcated in that way. It's located in Ontario, but hears cases pertaining to both provincial and federal law. Rouleau is considered a federal justice.) For one, it was an extraordinary situation, "unprecedented in size and complexity." There were **hundreds of vehicles** and **thousands of people** who came into Ottawa all at once to support them. A week later, the protest still ongoing, police said there were still [over 500 heavy vehicles in the area](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/ottawa/ottawa-protests-police-chief-no-end-in-sight-1.6341192). The police initially guided them into position thinking it would be a normal protest that would naturally conclude after a few days, not realizing what they were in for. An immediate concern after realizing the situation was getting out of control was officer safety, as these protesters outnumbered them, seemed agitated, and were being led by ideologues who were looking for a fight. The police would rather give some leeway if it meant reducing the chance of violent confrontation, or of putting theirselves into a situation where they have to respond with force. There was internal "distrust and dysfunction" within [the Ottawa Police Service](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/ottawa/emergencies-act-ottawa-police-intelligence-fail-1.6752239). Intelligence existed but wasn't properly disseminated and acted upon. "Much of the disarray in Ottawa was a result" of their "incorrect belief regarding how long the protests would last," Rouleau's report [said of the OPS command](https://www.ctvnews.ca/ottawa/article/series-of-policing-failures-contributed-to-freedom-convoy-spinning-out-of-control-inquiry-finds/), despite having access to reports indicating "a strong possibility that the Ottawa protests would extend past the first weekend." In at least one case, a police inspector was given reports but didn't believe the contents and thus didn't act on it. Much of blame was put on Peter Sloly, the city's police chief, who [resigned amid backlash](https://globalnews.ca/news/8621091/ottawa-police-peter-sloly-resigns/) on February 15th. Another point of difficulty was removing the trucks. If the protesters saw police were starting to arrest everybody, they could shut off their engines, lock their doors, and hide the keys. These were big trucks with air brakes, meaning the wheels would lock and [it'd be nearly impossible to move them without a heavy towtruck called a wrecker](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/ottawa/ottawa-protest-truck-tow-remove-1.6339652) to inflate the wheels or cage the brakes. The police tried contacting towing companies, who didn't want to get involved, some claiming the situation was too risky and [asking the government to indemnify them](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/carrique-tow-trucks-emergencies-act-1.6631222) against losses, which the city wasn't able to provide at that time. The Canadian Trucking Alliance (which [asked governments to reconsider](https://cantruck.ca/cta-update-on-border-domestic-vaccine-mandates/) their border policies in December) [issued a statement](https://cantruck.ca/statement-by-canadian-trucking-alliance-president-on-ottawa-protests/) on Janary 29 claiming many of the protesters "have no connection to the trucking industry and have a separate agenda beyond a disagreement over cross border vaccine requirements." They also claimed [the vast majority of truckers are vaccinated](https://cantruck.ca/canadian-trucking-alliance-statement-to-those-engaged-in-road-border-protests/). Back on January 28, as some truckers were first reaching Ottawa, **Pierre Poilievre**, not yet the national figure he is today (he wasn't even in the running for the Conservative Party leadership yet), [filmed a promotional video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FJqXJ2gm1KE) endorsing the truckers --- but now, after the protest came into full swing, he and many Conservatives had some hesitation about whether it was really something they wanted to be associated with. **Erin O'Toole**, still the Conservative Party leader at this time, [wrote on Twitter](https://x.com/erinotoole/status/1487589621047468036) that he supported peaceful protest, but condemned those who were showing disrespect and desecrating memorials. Poilievre hedged [the next day](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/poilievre-conservative-otoole-convoy-vaccine-mandate-1.6335286), "acknowledging there are people... who have different and unacceptable views." Michael Cooper, an Alberta MP, [gave an interview in front of protesters in Ottawa](https://www.cbc.ca/player/play/video/1.6334453), then [issued a statement](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/edmonton/edmonton-area-mp-under-fire-for-photo-of-him-near-flag-bearing-nazi-symbol-1.6333266) condemning the person who could be seen behind him carrying a flag with swastikas on it. (Ironically, just before the flag appeared, Cooper had been saying: "When you have tens of thousands of people, there's always going to be a small number of unsavoury characters.") !image-float media/swastika.png | The swastika seen behind Michael Cooper on CBC | Michael Cooper being interviewed on CBC But the unknown person in the background of that interview wasn't trying to convey support for Nazism. They were *accusing the government of Nazism* for implementing vaccine rules. This was a common theme throughout the protest: donning extremist symbols as an accusation. Some protesters were seen with signs featuring a syringe and a sickle, meant to look like a hammer and sickle, to implicitly compare vaccine rules to being ruled by Communist governments. Others wore yellow Stars of David, meant to compare their fate with those of Jews during Nazi Germany, who were forced to wear a Star of David to identify theirselves in public. There were complaints by Jewish groups in the media [calling the comparison insulting](https://www.ctvnews.ca/saskatoon/article/saskatoon-rabbi-calls-holocaust-imagery-used-by-ottawa-protestors-insulting/) and arguing it [reflects of a "growing ignorance"](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/ottawa/vaccine-protesters-holocaust-comparisons-1.6175321) about the Holocaust around the world that political activists continue invoking the Holocaust for comparatively minor grievances. !square-gallery { "src":"media/hammer-sickle.png", "caption":"Protesters with signs featuring the hammer and sickle" } { "src":"media/star-of-david-badge.png", "caption":"An anti-vaccine protester wearing a faux Star of David", "alt":"A photo of an anti-vaccine protester wearing a Star of David badge" } { "src":"media/anne-frank.png", "caption":"A protester with a sign of Anne Frank. An injunction [was ordered](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-us-canada-60293407) on the 7th, giving police a clear legal justification to act. !image-float media/ambassador-bridge.png | The Ambassador Bridge in Windsor (connecting to Detriot, U.S.) media/windsor-map.png | The location of Windsor in Ontario But a day before that, on the 6th, people connected to the convoy [started blocking provincial highways](https://www.theobserver.ca/news/police-close-hwy-402-as-anti-restrictions-convoy-arrives-in-sarnia-area) in Sarnia, Ontario, trying to expand their influence beyond Ottawa, and then news of the injunction on the 7th was overshadowed by reporting that truckers [were blocking the Ambassador Bridge](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/windsor/ambassador-bridge-protest-1.6342837) in Windsor, the point of a major border crossing that's of vital econcern for the province, as it's the only bridge connecting Windsor with Detroit, Michigan between Lake Eerie and Lake St. Claire. A substantial amount of international trade goes through that one bridge. At this point, the truckers had lost almost all sympathy. What were they going to do, go around the province wrecking things until they got their way? The Ontario Premier, Doug Ford, [declared a state of emergency](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/ford-announcement-ontario-protests-1.6347810) to grant extra powers to law enforcement, threatening protesters blocking the Ambassador bridge with [fines, loss of commercial licensing, or jail time](https://youtu.be/OK4lyKg3GN0?t=51) if they don't disperse. Of the convoy still ongoing in Ottawa, Ford [said, as part of his announcement](https://globalnews.ca/news/8612348/ontario-doug-ford-border-protests-announcement/), "I call it a siege because that is what it is. It's an illegal occupation." Despite issuing strong words, the government didn't rush and tried to minimize casualties. A court [issued an injunction ordering protesters to clear the way](https://news.ontario.ca/en/bulletin/1001597/notice-of-court-order-concerning-access-to-the-ambassador-bridge) on the 11th, and the police began an operation to arrest anybody still blocking the way and having all their vehicles towed. The bridge was [declared fully reopened](https://www.justice.gc.ca/eng/trans/bm-mb/other-autre/emergencies-urgence/58.html) on the 13th. Meanwhile, protests [were emerging across the country](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/pandemic-mandate-protests-feb12-2022-1.6349468) in support of the Ottawa truckers, most of them traditional protests (crowds of people with signs in Victoria, Toronto, Winnipeg, Regina, Calgary, Quebec City), but in a few places groups [blocked off highways](https://www.publicsafety.gc.ca/cnt/trnsprnc/brfng-mtrls/prlmntry-bndrs/20221013/03-en.aspx) (in Surrey, BC on Feb 12; Emerson, MB on Feb 13). These remained peaceful and were dispersed after a few days, none of them turning into a huge ordeal. The next day, February 14th, the federal government decided to stop waiting and [declared a Public Order Emergency](https://www.justice.gc.ca/eng/csj-sjc/section58.html), invoking the *Emergency Powers* act. This gave the RCMP jurisdiction [to enforce municipal law](https://www.ctvnews.ca/politics/article/trudeau-makes-history-invokes-emergencies-act-to-address-trucker-protests/), to order holds on bank accounts of people knowingly engaging in or funding criminal activity, and to declare temporary "red zones" where people weren't allowed to assemble. Trudeau [said at a press conference](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/trudeau-premiers-cabinet-1.6350734) "there are serious challenges to law enforcement's ability to effectively enforce the law," referring to the failures of city and provincial police. They spent another few days trying to clear people out before issuing a final warning on the 17th, then starting a full sweep of the area on the 18th, arresting any convoy truckers who remained --- which was still over 100 of them, clinging on til the bitter end, fully accepting that a confrontation with police was the only way they were ever going to leave. Also on the 14th, it was reported [the RCMP seized weapons and ammunition](https://globalnews.ca/news/8618494/alberta-coutts-border-protest-weapons-ammunition-seized/) at the blockade in Coutts. We later learned the RCMP had [sent undercover officers to the blockade](https://calgaryherald.com/news/crime/some-coutts-protesters-wanted-to-alter-canadas-political-system-court-documents-say) and learned about the shipments of weapons ahead of time, then stuck around to confirm the shipment was received. Four men were arrested and charged with conspiracy to commit murder. RCMP Const. Trevor Checkley wrote in an affidavit that one of them men told their undercover officer "if the RCMP came in with force they would be met with greater force," that "the RCMP were the enemy," that they were "willing to die for the cause," and they "believed the government wanted to destroy the middle class and the economy was going to collapse." One of the undercover officers [testified in court](https://calgary.citynews.ca/2024/06/11/undercover-officer-tells-coutts-murder-conspiracy-trial-protester-willing-to-die/) the same man "said this is war and why he is willing to give his life. This is ground zero."
