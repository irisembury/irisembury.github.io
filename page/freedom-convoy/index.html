// !article-top "We are now two weeks into the siege of the city of Ottawa." --- Doug Ford, Premier of Ontario, February 2022 In January 2022, a bunch of people drove into Ottawa in big trucks, parked them in the road, and started honking. Its participants and proponents called it the **Freedom Convoy**, though it's commonly referred to simply as "the convoy protest." At its height, Doug Ford called it [the Siege of Ottawa](https://www.cbc.ca/player/play/video/1.6348216) (as did others, like [Yves-François Blanchet](https://www.ourcommons.ca/documentviewer/en/44-1/house/sitting-33/hansard#T1025)), but even that name undersells it. What was the Freedom Convoy? Why did Poilievre support it, and why was his support for it so controversial? The truckers were supposedly upset about being required to get a vaccine for the COVID-19 pandemic if they wanted to keep their jobs, and they were showing up to protest as a way of letting their grievances be known. This could've been a cause I were sympathetic to, if it were exactly that and no more. Unfortunately, things were not as they seemed. This is my full report. # Preliminary ## Timeline *The pandemic years blur together in many people's minds, and not everybody was keeping up with it at the time. Let's ensure we're all on the same page with a timeline of relevant events.* *There's additional info about the pandemic on this [WHO timeline](https://www.who.int/news/item/27-04-2020-who-timeline---covid-19). Numerous other sources have created timelines about the Freedom Convoy (e.g. CTV News: ['Freedom Convoy' protest: How did we get here?](https://www.ctvnews.ca/canada/article/freedom-convoy-protest-how-did-we-get-here/) and [Public Safety Canada: Overview -- Freedom Convoy 2022](https://www.publicsafety.gc.ca/cnt/trnsprnc/brfng-mtrls/prlmntry-bndrs/20221013/03-en.aspx).)* #### 2020 * **January**: We first began seeing [reporting about](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-china-50984025) a "viral pneumonia" detected in Wuhan, China the previous year spreading to our countries. By the end of the month, the WHO [convened an emergency committee](https://www.who.int/news/item/30-01-2020-statement-on-the-second-meeting-of-the-international-health-regulations-(2005\)-emergency-committee-regarding-the-outbreak-of-novel-coronavirus-(2019-ncov\)) and declared the matter an emergency of international concern. * **February**: The WHO [officially named](https://www.who.int/emergencies/diseases/novel-coronavirus-2019/technical-guidance/naming-the-coronavirus-disease-(covid-2019\)-and-the-virus-that-causes-it) the virus SARS-CoV-2 (severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2, a sister virus to the [2003 outbreak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SARS) simply called SARS) and the disease COVID-19 (coronavirus disease of 2019). * **March**: The WHO announced the virus [had become a pandemic](https://www.nytimes.com/2020/03/11/health/coronavirus-pandemic-who.html). As cases emerged across the country, every province and territory in Canada **declared a state of emergency**, as did many towns and cities. At this point, provincial governments [began ordering the closure](https://globalnews.ca/news/6688074/ontario-doug-ford-coronavirus-covid-19-march-17/) of many non-essential places of gathering. * **December**: Pfizer and Moderna both finished phase 3 clinical trials for their vaccines, which were authorized for use by Health Canada. The very first doses that were available that month were [given to care workers](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/first-doses-covid-19-vaccine-1.5839614). #### 2021 * **January, February**: Vaccines started being distributed to the public, starting with seniors. In Ontario, booking eligibility [was expanded to all people 18 or older](https://news.ontario.ca/en/release/1000143/covid-19-vaccine-booking-expanding-to-ontarians-18-ahead-of-schedule) in **May 2021**, marking the start of the mass vaccine rollout and push toward herd immunity. * As the year progressed, some rules began changing, but the details varied by province, mostly for political reasons. * In **Ontario**, for example, there had been a general ban on indoor assembly, affecting things like gyms and restaurants, but in **September** [that was partially lifted](https://news.ontario.ca/en/backgrounder/1000806/using-your-vaccination-receipt-frequently-asked-questions) for people with proof of vaccination, who were temporarily allowed access to such things early. * The most restrictive policies were probably in **Quebec**, where [they twice implemented nightly curfews](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/montreal/quebec-pandemic-curfew-1.7470532) during particularly bad waves of infections, and instead of only requiring proof of vaccination for indoor seating at restaurants, it was also required for high-density foot traffic areas like Walmart. * In **the prairies**, pandemic rules were less popular, and the response by provincial governments reflected that to some extent (see [Alberta's "Open for Summer" plan](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/edmonton/jason-kenney-deena-hinshaw-covid-reopening-update-1.6070925) for example). They tried to avoid using the term "vaccine passport," and they let people be seated at indoor gatherings without being vaccinated if they provided proof of a negative COVID-19 test (see Saskatchewan's policy [here](https://www.saskatchewan.ca/government/news-and-media/2021/september/28/proof-of-vaccination-or-negative-tests--required-broadly-october-1-2021)). In Alberta, the government [offered to pay people $100](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/calgary/poll-angus-reid-alberta-prairies-vaccine-passport-1.6168075) to get vaccinated, trying to use reward instead of punishment. * By the fall, most provincial governments had [laid out a plan](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/ontario-covid-exit-roadmap-reopen-capacity-limits-lifted-1.6220653) for how bring pandemic policies to a close, though many had to change course after [the Omicron variant of the virus hit Canada unexpected hard](https://www.cbc.ca/news/health/omicron-canada-world-subvariants-covid-vaccination-hybrid-immunity-1.6680620) in December and January. (The disease was milder but the virus was far more contagious, and we realized the vaccine didn't protect from it, forcing health organizations to rethink their approach.) * **October 29**: The U.S. government let us know well in advance that [it planned to end the exemption](https://www.dhs.gov/archive/news/2021/10/29/dhs-releases-details-fully-vaccinated-non-citizen-travelers-enter-us-land-and-ferry) for "essential travel" to vaccine requirements for non-citizens entering their country starting in January 2022. * **November 19**: The Canadian government [announced it would implement the same border policy as the U.S.](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/news/2021/11/adjustments-to-canadas-border-and-travel-measures.html) come January, indicating that "essential service providers including truck drivers" will no longer be exempt at that time. * **December 2021**: The group behind the convoy, which called itself **Canada Unity**, [published a document](https://web.archive.org/web/20220123051114/https://canada-unity.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Combined-MOU-Dec03.pdf) calling for the Canadian Senate and Governor General to overrule the House of Commons and the Ministry, to end all vaccine rules in the country, and to rehire anybody whose employment was terminated for reasons related to vaccine rules. They also demanded the creation of a new, parallel authority, the "Citizens of Canada Committee," and said if the Senate and Governor General don't do this, they demand all involved "resign their lawful positions of authority immediately." #### 2022 * **January 15**: The government announced a change to how international travel rules worked, [eliminating the exemption for essential workers](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/news/2022/01/requirements-for-truckers-entering-canada-in-effect-as-of-january-15-2022.html). If you had proof of vaccination when entering the country, you could enter normally. If you didn't, then non-citizens were denied entry, and citizens were allowed to return home, were subject to requirements like testing or quarantine. * That same day, **Tamara Lich**, one of the convoy organizers, [started a GoFundMe page](https://web.archive.org/web/20220115003941/https://www.gofundme.com/f/taking-back-our-freedom-convoy-2022) to raise funds for the cause. This raised [millions of dollars](https://globalnews.ca/news/8542593/trucker-convoy-gofundme-one-million/) by the 27th. * **January 22**: The United States begins enforcing [the same policy](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/01/24/2022-01403/notification-of-temporary-travel-restrictions-applicable-to-land-ports-of-entry-and-ferries-service), not allowing unvaccinated non-citizens across their border. At this point, it's effectively impossible to work as an international truck-driver if you're not vaccinated because one country or the other won't let you in. * **January 23--29**: Truckers across Canada start driving to Ottawa. * Throughout this week, they pass through [numerous Canadian cities](https://www.ctvnews.ca/winnipeg/article/convoy-opposing-trucker-vaccine-mandate-draws-large-crowd-concerns-over-comparison-to-holocaust/) fundraising and advertising their cause. There ended up being [a few different convoys](https://www.ctvnews.ca/ottawa/article/truck-convoy-rolls-into-kingston-ont/) driving around Ontario before they converged in Ottawa. * The messaging about the purpose of the protest already begins shifting. One organizer, Jason LaFace, [was quoted in the media](https://www.baytoday.ca/local-news/truck-convoys-message-muddies-the-closer-it-gets-to-capital-4994947) saying "This is no longer about the mandate anymore, this is about Canada," complaining about mask rules and calling Trudeau a criminal. Another, James Bauder, said [it's about mandates, and masks, and lockdowns](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/edmonton/anti-vaccine-mandate-truck-convoy-alberta-covid-1.6324819), not giving any particular focus to the border policy. * In the days before the 29th, numerous outlets began reporting about how insane some of the convoy organizers were. * **Global News**: [Far-right groups hope trucker protest will be Canada's 'January 6th'](https://globalnews.ca/news/8537433/far-right-groups-trucker-protest-jan-6/) * **Global News**: ['Fringe minority' in truck convoy with 'unacceptable views' don't represent Canadians: Trudeau](https://globalnews.ca/news/8539610/trucker-convoy-covid-vaccine-mandates-ottawa/) * **VICE News**: [MPs Told to Hide From Anti-Vaxxer Convoy by Parliament Security Chief](https://www.vice.com/en/article/mps-told-to-hide-from-anti-vaxxer-trucker-convoy-by-parliament-security-chief/) * **January 28**: As truckers arrived in Ottawa, Poilievre [filmed a video watching and promoting it](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FJqXJ2gm1KE). * **January 29**: The Freedom Convoy trucker convoy protest arrive in Ottawa near Parliament Hill for the final weekend of the month. It was estimated [to be about 3,000 people](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/ottawa/ottawa-protest-convoy-length-ticket-tow-mayor-1.6333497) present. Some people would remain until mid-February, occupying the area for about three weeks. * **February 2**: Erin O'Toole was [voted out as Conservative Party leader](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-us-canada-60236191), only a few days into the protest. * **February 10**: The convoy protest [raised millions more](https://globalnews.ca/news/8610512/givesendgo-fundraiser-trucker-convoy-frozen/) on GiveSendGo and other platforms. * **February 14**: The government of Canada invoked the *Emergencies Act* and clears the convoy protesters out of Ottawa. * **March 2022**: Nearly all *domestic* pandemic rules were phased out in or around March, varying by province. The trucker protest had no apparent effect on the timeline, which had been laid out months in advance. * The earliest was Saskatchewan, which [ended all pandemic-related public health orders](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/saskatchewan/public-health-orders-saskatchewan-february-28-1.6366407) on February 28. * In mid-February, [the Ontario government announced](https://news.ontario.ca/en/release/1001600/ontario-moving-to-next-phase-of-reopening-on-february-17) it would meet its initial timeline, and [ended almost all pandemic-related public health orders](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/covid19-ontario-march-9-mask-mandates-1.6378148) on March 21. * Some minor restrictions lingered around into April. * **May 5**: The Conservative Party held a [leadership debate](https://www.cpac.ca/public-record/episode/conservative-leadership-debate-in-ottawa--may-5-2022?id=0f0d5ab9-fba2-4162-b24b-59d1c16c3b14), at which Jean Charest criticized Poilievre for supporting the convoy protest, and Poilievre hit back defending them. * **April**: Poilievre [criticizes Charest](https://x.com/PierrePoilievre/status/1512507527094755341) for *not* supporting the truckers. * **October**: Federal border policies related to the pandemic [phased out](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/news/2022/09/government-of-canada-to-remove-covid-19-border-and-travel-measures-effective-october-1.html). * **September 10**: Pierre Poilievre [declared the winner](https://www.ctvnews.ca/politics/article/pierre-poilievre-wins-conservative-leadership-on-first-ballot/) of the Conservative Party's leadership race. Now, before I move on, let's get something straight: ## Were you forced to get vaccinated? It became required for some jobs. But for most people, no, it was optional. So whether you were forced to get it depends on who you were, and what you mean by "forced." I personally knew multiple people who simply never bothered going to a vaccination site, and there was no penalty, nothing happened to them, nobody tried to compel them to get it. In the West, the countries that went furthest in trying to achieve high vaccination rates were [Austria](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/covid-19-vaccine-austria-parliament-mandate-adults/) and [Ecuador](https://www.reuters.com/world/americas/ecuador-makes-covid-19-vaccination-obligatory-2021-12-23/), where it was directly mandated that you had to get vaccinated or face legal penalty. If you refused and wouldn't budge, I imagine you could *eventually* get out of it --- it was only a civil charge; nobody was ever coming to your house to hold you down and force the needle in you --- but on paper, they did basically say "it's illegal to not be vaccinated," unless you could prove you had a legitimate medical reason to not get one. In the U.S., the federal government [tried to implement a soft mandate through labour regulations](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/biden-tasks-osha-with-vaccine-mandate) by making large employers require their workers to get vaccinated to keep them as employees, though this didn't survive legal challenge. This indirect approach was far more common, technically not making it illegal to be unvaccinated, but making life more difficult, expensive, or inconvenient for people who refused by restricting access to anything they didn't have a legal right to. Tying vaccination status to employment was appealing because it allowed governments to outsource the actual enforcement of vaccines rules to private companies. What portion of labourers were affected varied by country. In Italy it was [nearly all companies](https://www.ctvnews.ca/lifestyle/article/italy-makes-covid-19-health-pass-mandatory-for-all-workers/), while in some other countries, like France, it was [only people who worked in specific fields](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-europe-58581682) --- often government employees, travel, healthcare, and long-term care facilities. I won't go over every country. In many countries it was a mix targetting industries considered high risk. The U.S., UK, and Sweden stood out for not having workplace vaccine mandates at all (in Sweden's case, because [it would conflict with their country's unique constitutional protections](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=da1ed979-cf94-4c33-81bf-4bd738d632f3) against compelled "bodily intrusion"). In Canada, vaccination was [required for workers in the public service](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/federal-vaccine-mandate-1.6201528) and other highly regulated industries, like telecomm and air travel. If you worked at a department store, a software company, a restaurant, a law firm, or any other business outside the scope of where the government focused its efforts, you were probably unaffected. This is why I say Canada didn't have a *general* vaccine mandate, but did have rules to compel people in certain jobs if they wanted to keep those jobs. However, it wasn't impossible for someone in one of those fields to get hired somewhere else and never get vaccinated, if it was truly all-important to them (as it may have been if you thought the vaccine was unsafe and going to seriously harm you). Another area where you could face restrictions if you were unvaccinated was while travelling. The [standard at the time was](https://tc.canada.ca/en/binder/border-measures-general) that if you came from away, either from out of country or moving between provinces, you had to do a COVID-19 test and then were kept away from the general population for 14 days, the standard incubation period for the disease. They didn't weld your door shut, but they would send you to a hotel, have food delivered to your door, and ask you to stay away from large gatherings, including going to see relatives at their home. If you *were* travelling just to see family, you were advised to delay travel altogether, since they realized a two-week buffer on arrival before getting to see anybody was a lot to ask. If you were only coming across the border to deliver freight and go home, that's a different story, so we issued exemptions for essential workers until January 2022, when the Canadian government [eliminated that exemption](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/news/2022/01/requirements-for-truckers-entering-canada-in-effect-as-of-january-15-2022.html), and the U.S. [did the same a week later](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2022/01/24/2022-01403/notification-of-temporary-travel-restrictions-applicable-to-land-ports-of-entry-and-ferries-service). It was no longer possible to travel back and forth if you weren't vaccinated, unless you were a citizen of both countries. We knew these rules wouldn't be in place forever, but there was no specific timeline given for its expiration when first implemented, so waiting it out wasn't an option. At a glance, you may have thought truckers protesting the border rule had a fair point. Maybe truck drivers who were citizens *should* be exempt from normal procedure after returning from the U.S. as part of their job. It's not a crazy thing to ask. But after the U.S. policy went into effect --- before their protest even began --- the situation was already no longer one the Canadian government could remedy unilaterally. It wouldn't be impossible for the Canadian government to both change its policy and persuade the U.S. to change theirs, but this would take some time, and the purpose of the protest quickly evolved: instead of just complaining about the international border, anybody who looked around at the convoy could tell you attendees were clearly upset about *all* pandemic-related rules. But that wasn't going to work, either. The pandemic rules were broadly popular, and there was no sign of everybody else in the country changing their mind based on the truckers' protest. Some people at the time suggested the government should give in and drop all pandemic rules to satisfy the truckers, but that's not how anything works: the government shouldn't side against the majority just to please a group that decided to put on a protest. So from the onset, this whole thing seemed like a non-starter. It was an oddly expensive and time-consuming way to vent frustration, not a plan to realistically going to get what they wanted --- or at least, that's how it seemed to the rest of us. Nonetheless, they all showed up. # Occupation days: Jan 31 to Feb 23 The truckers arrived in Ottawa; problems began immediately. When we think of protest, we tend to think of people coming together in a public place to deliver a message. They chant, they hold up signs, maybe they ask people to sign petitions. They make their views and grievances heard. And if a law is affecting you unreasonably, but in a way that the majority of the population would be unaware of, that seems like a perfect time for protest to let the rest of us know about what's happening to you. A right to protest is not explicitly mentioned in the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, but it's understood as a natural consequence of the right to free expression plus the right to peaceful assembly, and is considered one of the cornerstones of a democratic society. Though some people online would later falsely claim otherwise, nobody in Canada was ever denied the right to protest throughout this whole ordeal, nor was that right ever generally in question. But the Freedom Convoy was not *merely* engaging in protest, and engaging in protest doesn't make you immune to laws that don't prevent you from protesting. If I started killing people as a form of protest and got arrested for murder, that doesn't mean I've been deprived of my right to protest. It just means protesting doesn't override murder laws, or that murder isn't a legitimate form of protest. If the government passed a law against speaking in public, on the other hand, that law would be challenged because it interferes with the ability to exercise a right to protest, among other things. So while protesting is a universally cherished right that nobody questions, it's not a license to do *anything* you want, and has to be balanced with other concerns. !image-float media/streets-blocked.png | Streets still blocked on day 11 of the protests To begin with, truckers in the Freedom Convoy [had completely blocked off the streets in downtown Ottawa](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-us-canada-60190452). That could be fine for a weekend, at the city's discretion, but not for weeks on end. Most forms of protest don't block off streets at all. They also started [laying on their horns for hours at the time](https://globalnews.ca/video/8580501/trucker-convoy-blaring-horns-cheers-as-hundreds-descend-on-parliament) despite their proxitimy to residential property. The area reportedly [smelled like diesel fumes](https://globalnews.ca/news/10029656/freedom-convoy-trial-ottawa-resident/) because of how many trucks were sitting there idling for so long. The protesters were [committing vandalism](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-us-canada-60190452), intimidating locals, and there was concern about some protesters displaying extremist or hate symbols, including the [Confederate flag](https://www.npr.org/2022/02/10/1079842220/ottawa-trucker-convoy-protest), probably for its loose association with rebellion. Ottawa residents began feeling their own downtown core was rendered unusuable, nearby businesses were all practically forced closed, and as we came into the next week, everyone started realizing this was lasting far longer than anticipated. If these truckers were all out of work, how could they afford to drive across the country and then sit around doing this for days on end? They had millions of dollars being given to them by people anonymously on the internet. They were having food and jerry cans of fuel brought in to keep it going. They [built makeshift kitchens](https://www.ibtimes.com/canada-protesters-dig-military-style-proficiency-3397485) as it evolved from a protest into an indefinite occupation. It was clear they were not intent on leaving any time soon. !image-float media/coutts-truckers.png | Some of the truckers in Coutts media/coutts-map.png | Location of Coutts, AB Meanwhile, the same day truckers convened in Ottawa, other groups of truckers showed up and [completely blocked off Alberta Highway 4](https://globalnews.ca/news/9264433/emergencies-act-inquiry-coutts-border-blockades/), which connected to the U.S. state of Montana just south of a border village called **Coutts, Alberta**. The Alberta Premier immediately [called the protest illegal](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-us-canada-60218354). Blocking critical infrastructure in the middle of nowhere lacks the same veneer of legitimacy as protesting in downtown Ottawa in plain view of government buildings. There were over 100 people involved. Back in Ottawa, the public had turned against the protesters. Had I lived in Ottawa then, I'd have been outraged if they weren't at least being given tickets and court dates. Do you know what would happen if I walked downtown right now and sat in traffic to protest something? A police officer would show up and drag me off the street. Unless you get a permit, we don't let people randomly block traffic. So why weren't the police doing anything about the truckers, who were doing that and more? It was a huge nuisance, far beyond what a protest normally permits, and city residents were frustrated that the protest was being given so much lenience. Some people suggested the police were being more lenient because the convoy members were mostly white. "In my day, I have been part of many different political demonstrations and supported many different causes. I have seen police clear out demonstrations of people protesting against free trade agreements and racism and in defence of indigenous rights far more quickly and far more brutally, despite those demonstrations being truly peaceful demonstrations." --- Daniel Blaikie There were several points of failure in the response. We know more now than we knew at the time because there were public inquiries into what went wrong, including the [Public Order Emergency Commission](https://publicorderemergencycommission.ca/final-report/), an inquiry led by Paul Rouleau, a justice for the Court of Appeals for Ontario, who [eventually concluded](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/poec-report-released-friday-1.6750919) "the very high threshold for the invocation" of the Emergency Powers Act "was met." (For international readers, this is not exactly a "provincial court," as Canadian courts are not demarcated in that way. It's located in Ontario, but hears cases pertaining to both provincial and federal law. Rouleau is considered a federal justice.) For one, it was an extraordinary situation, "unprecedented in size and complexity." By Saturday, [thousands of vehicles](https://web.archive.org/web/20220202214635/https://www.ottawapolice.ca//Modules/News/index.aspx?newsId=4e505c6b-0e2e-4a69-a9f0-cfccdcb3e542) had arrived in Ottawa. (A week later, the protest still ongoing, police would say there were still [over 500 heavy vehicles in the area](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/ottawa/ottawa-protests-police-chief-no-end-in-sight-1.6341192).) The police initially guided them into position thinking it would be a normal protest that would naturally conclude after a few days, not realizing what they were in for. An immediate concern after realizing the situation was getting out of control was officer safety, as these protesters outnumbered them, seemed agitated, and were being led by ideologues who were looking for a fight. Police are willing to give some leeway if it reduces the chance of putting theirselves into a situation where they have to respond with force. There was internal "distrust and dysfunction" within [the Ottawa Police Service](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/ottawa/emergencies-act-ottawa-police-intelligence-fail-1.6752239). Intelligence existed but wasn't properly disseminated and acted upon. "Much of the disarray in Ottawa was a result" of their "incorrect belief regarding how long the protests would last," Rouleau's report [said of the OPS command](https://www.ctvnews.ca/ottawa/article/series-of-policing-failures-contributed-to-freedom-convoy-spinning-out-of-control-inquiry-finds/), despite having access to reports indicating "a strong possibility that the Ottawa protests would extend past the first weekend." In at least one case, a police inspector was given reports but didn't believe the contents and thus didn't act on it. Much of blame was put on Peter Sloly, the city's police chief, who [resigned amid backlash](https://globalnews.ca/news/8621091/ottawa-police-peter-sloly-resigns/) on February 15th. Another point of difficulty was removing the trucks. If the protesters saw police were starting to arrest everybody, they could shut off their engines, lock their doors, and hide the keys. These were big trucks with air brakes, meaning the wheels would lock and [it'd be nearly impossible to move them without a heavy towtruck called a wrecker](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/ottawa/ottawa-protest-truck-tow-remove-1.6339652) to inflate the wheels or cage the brakes. The police tried contacting towing companies, who didn't want to get involved, some claiming the situation was too risky and [asking the government to indemnify them](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/carrique-tow-trucks-emergencies-act-1.6631222) against losses, which the city wasn't able to provide at that time. The Canadian Trucking Alliance (which [asked governments to reconsider](https://cantruck.ca/cta-update-on-border-domestic-vaccine-mandates/) their border policies in December) [issued a statement](https://cantruck.ca/statement-by-canadian-trucking-alliance-president-on-ottawa-protests/) on Janary 29 claiming many of the protesters "have no connection to the trucking industry and have a separate agenda beyond a disagreement over cross border vaccine requirements." They also claimed [the vast majority of truckers are vaccinated](https://cantruck.ca/canadian-trucking-alliance-statement-to-those-engaged-in-road-border-protests/). Before I go on: **do *you* know how Canada works?** Technically, the "Goverment of Canada" refers to two things: 1. The executive branch, called the **Canadian Ministry**, also called **Cabinet**. This is a collection of people sworn in as ministers, and they're led by a Prime Minister, who is the head of government. The ministry is the main actual thing that makes decisions about governing the country in practice. * The ministry is made up of members of the House of Commons who belong to the party that has the most seats in the House. * The **Public Service** (formerly the Civil Service), which refers to the various departments and agencies, like Health Canada or the Department of Finance, as directed by the associated ministers in Cabinet. The ministry governs Canada *through* the Public Service. * In colloquial speech some people may also say "government" to mean Parliament or the House of Commons, as it's the lower chamber of Parliament that form the ministry, but this is technically **incorrect**. If you followed points 1 and 2, you're up to speed. That's how Canada works. The funny nuance is that, on paper, it's actually the monarch of Canada that's governing the country. The head of state of our executive branch is Charles III. But as Charles doesn't have time to be everywhere, he's represented in the country by a governor general, whom he appoints to function on his behalf. And as the governor general can't do everything on their own, they require council. The governor general therefore appoints people to be member of such a council, which is called the **King's Privy Council for Canada**. Any time the government does anything, it's technically council *advising* Charles, and then Charles choosing to do as his council advised. But as is customary, he always and only does as recommended by the Prime Minister. Who's the Prime Minister? It's whomever Charles decides should be Prime Minister. But as is customary, Charles always appoints the person who commands the confidence of the House of Commons in the Canadian Parliament. And in practice, it's not directly Charles who does this, but the governor general who represents him does it on his behalf. Meaning after it's all set up, the system works without Charles having to physically be present at all. We hold an election, the governor general appoints the leader of the largest party in the House to the Privy Council and declares them Prime Minister, and then the Prime Minister advises which members of the House the governor general should appoint to be ministers of government departments, and they're also admitted to the Privy Council. Centuries ago, the monarch would often intervene and make decisions independently from council, or disagreeing with council. However, over the years, it's become the norm that everything works better if the monarch simply always does as council advises, and for a long time now the country has been running on that assumption. For example, in 2019, you may have heard that Justin Trudeau appointed Nicholas Kasirer to the Supreme Court of Canada. But the way Trudeau actually does this isn't by appointing Kasirer himself, but by recommending Kasirer to the governor general, Julie Payette, and then Payette is the one who appoints Kasirer. For this reason, we don't say Kasirer was appointed by Trudeau, but that he was appointed *on advice of* Trudeau. He was appointed by the Crown. But as is customary, Payette would always and only have appointed the person the Prime Minister advises. And when Payette resigned, Trudeau selected Mary Simon to succeed her as the next governor general; but again, he didn't appoint Simon himself; rather, he advised the monarch, then Elizabeth II, on whom to appoint, and it was the Queen who appointed Simon her next representative. For this reason, we don't say Simon was appointed by Trudeau, but that Simon was appointed *on advice of* Trudeau. But as is customary, the Queen would always and only have appointed the person the Prime Minister advised. This is why *de jure* power is held by the monarch and governor general, but *de facto* power is held by the Prime Minister and their cabinet. Every Canadian learns the basics of this in gradeschool. It's why the monarch of Canada is called a figurehead, while the role of governor general is considered mostly ceremonial. They play a vital role in the system, but neither exercise any serious decision-making power in practice. They respect the advice of the Prime Minister because the Prime Minister represents the House of Commons, which is the main organ of Canadian democracy. If a monarch or governor general ever decided to disregard the Prime Minister, it would represent a constitutional crisis for the Canadian system and may force us to completely upend the current order of things and develop a new framework. Unless the Prime Minister decides that we're actually cool with what the monarch is doing, in which case we could all choose to shrug and just carry on like that didn't just happen. Who knows. Why do we do things like this? At a glance, some people find it odd for a person who makes a decision to not have the authority to carry it out by theirself, but have to advise another person to do it for them; but that kind of indirectness or layering is common in government structures throughout the world for various reasons. I do have suggestions for how the Canadian system could be improved in some areas, but I see no problem with this aspect of how our government works. I'd argue it's a well balanced constitutional monarchy that helps to foster a political culture of respect, modesty, wisdom, and national and spiritual unity. But suffice to say, we do it because it's our tradition, and it is unimportant whether the rest of the world appreciates it as such. *Now let's get back to the document distributed by the Canada Unity group.*

The *Emergencies Act* was invoked on February 14 and revoked on February 23. The government then [started a Parliamentary committee](https://www.publicsafety.gc.ca/cnt/trnsprnc/brfng-mtrls/prlmntry-bndrs/20221013/10-en.aspx) and, later, an [independent judicial inquiry](https://www.pm.gc.ca/en/news/news-releases/2022/04/25/prime-minister-announces-public-order-emergency-commission-following) to investigate and organize reports on what happened, what went wrong, and to evaluate the government's invocation of the act. This was particularly important because it was the first time this act had been invoked since it was passed in 1988, meaning the government had no direct precedent for how to it should be used, and the text of Act contains numerous terms and conditions that are ambiguous or open to interpretation. Analysis and review of this situation would generate useful insights for future government actors. In 2023, Paul Rouleau, the justice tasked by the government to handle this inquiry, and who released a 2,000 page report on the matter, said: "When the decision was made to invoke the Act on February 14, 2022, Cabinet had reasonable grounds to believe that there existed a national emergency arising from threats to the security of Canada that necessitated the taking of special, temporary measures. I do not come to this conclusion easily, as I do not consider the factual basis for it to be overwhelming. Reasonable and informed people could reach a different conclusion than the one I have arrived at." --- Paul Rouleau ([February 17, 2023](https://www.cbc.ca/player/play/video/1.6752402)) The next year, two groups --- the Canadian Constitution Foundation and the Canadian Civil Liberties Association --- [brought a case about this to federal court](https://www.ctvnews.ca/politics/article/federal-court-rules-emergencies-act-invocation-not-justified/), and the federal justice Richard Mosley eventually [came to a different conclusion](https://www.fct-cf.ca/Content/assets/pdf/base/2024.01.23-306-22-T-316-22-T-347-22-T-382-22.pdf), writing that "the legal constraints on the discretion of the GIC [Government in Council] to declare a public order emergency were not satisfied" and it "was unreasonable for the GIC to conclude that the requisite thresholds in the Act had been met." In other words, he agrees the situation was dire --- he says the protests represented "an unacceptable breakdown of public order" and "it was reasonable for the GIC to be alarmed" --- but he believes there were other things the government had to do before resorting to the path they went down. This conclusion is not necessarily a condemnation of the government, but a clarification about the correct way to apply this law in the future. As Mosley wrote: "I had and continue to have considerable sympathy for those in government who were confronted with this situation. Had I been at their tables at that time, I may have agreed that it was necessary to invoke the Act. And I acknowledge that in conducting judicial review of that decision, I am revisiting that time with the benefit of hindsight and a more extensive record of the facts and law than that which was before the GIC." --- Richard Mosley He also notes how "the meaning of 'national security' is not defined in the statutes," and it's difficult to define because the term is "highly fact-based and political in a general sense." However, the government did not adequately communicate with the provinces and territories to "determine whether they had the capacity or authority to deal with the threat," and "most Premiers informed the Prime Minister that invocation of the Act was not required in their provinces as their legislation and law enforcement authorities could deal with the situation." "It may be considered unrealistic to expect the Federal Government to wait when the country is "threatened by serious and dangerous situations," as the Respondent characterizes the events of January and February 2022, while the Provinces or Territories determine whether they have the capacity or authority to deal with the threat or, if not, could enact what is lacking in their respective legislative or regulatory tool boxes. However, that is what the *Emergencies Act* appears to require." --- Richard Mosley A lawyer for the government [argued the conclusion is flawed](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/emergencies-act-appeal-1.7450394) because Mosley was revisiting these events two years later with (as Mosley himself says above) "a more extensive record of the facts" (and understanding of the law) than was available to the government at the time. Chrystia Freeland, then Deputy Prime Minister, [responded at a press conference](https://www.ctvnews.ca/politics/article/federal-court-rules-emergencies-act-invocation-not-justified/), saying, "The public safety of Canadians was under threat. Our national security, which includes our national economic security, was under threat. I was convinced at the time it was the right thing to do, it was the necessary thing to do. I remain and we remain convinced of that." However, again, the point of the ruling was not to hold the government in contempt or to condemn Cabinet for making the decision it did. This is legal review and analysis to provide clarity and guidance for future governments.
