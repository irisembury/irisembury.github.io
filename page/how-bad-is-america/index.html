I recently made an aside, while talking about something else, that America has been a net force for good in the world. The phrasing --- a *net* force for good --- doesn't mean it hasn't done bad, but that the good outweighs the bad in the big picture. The reason I made that note was to clarify where I'm coming from, as my goal was to offer broad analysis of the country. If you think America has always been terrible, then you may feel differently about its recent decline. In the view of some people, the rise of Trumpism has merely been America taking off its disguise, deciding it no longer cares about *pretending* to be the good guys; that the country has changed over superficially. This is a horribly ignorant view of things, not only uninformed, probably, about just how much has been changed under Trump (because most people aren't fully plugged in and paying attention, reading about the news in detail to understand the depths of the ongoing destruction), but also about how everything worked *before* Trump. There's widespread, honest-to-god lack of knowledge about law and government these days among people who nonetheless maintain very strong opinions about precisely that. "The establishment" is a black box for the average person: a big thing that does stuff, but they don't know what goes on inside of it. Their imagination fills the gaps. If we flatten everything into simple abstractions, a few stand-out data points, and an invitation to speculate, it's not hard to make it sound as though the world hasn't changed much. Sure, Trump is doing corrupt things, but you don't think past presidents were also corrupt? Sure, the U.S. overthrew Nicholás Maduro in 2025, but in the 2000s the U.S. overthrew Saddam Hussein, and in the 1970s they overthrew Salvador Allende. So you see? It's all the same, really. This kind of lazy thinking is the reason "nothing ever happens" has become a real current in young peoples' thinking: there's no event that you can't relate to or make sound similar to some past event, thus rhetorically minimizing its significance. There is also a lot of negative misinformation that floats around in the ether concerning America not as a country per se, but as an international actor. It feels almost like the sort of propaganda the USSR would produce in the Cold War, but instead it's homegrown cynicism: people who grew up in the U.S., being told by their teachers and parents that their country was good. Their rebellion is expressed as repentance. Likely corresponding with the internet age, it's become popular to throw one's hands up in the air and say everything is broken, there is no justice in the world, power corrupts and therefore everything that's powerful is correct. It's a dismal view of the world but it's also very appealing to somebody who's not informed because it doesn't invite further investigation or critical engagement, but dismissal. And when that attitude is applied to history, the result is people with only a cursory view of many different events, each one looked into one enough to conclude that America was the bad guy. Once that framing get into one's head, it becomes their default assumption, they start seeing "confirmation" of it everywhere, and it can be hard to shake them of it. For such people, I have three points to make: **1. We can only judge a thing by its alternatives.** What I mean is this: where "imperialism" means one country exerting its power over others in some way (through direct military action, through economic leverage, through cultural exports, and so on), you cannot have a world in which there is no imperialism. It simply does not exist, nor do we have the power to create it. If America were to retreat from everywhere, all that would happen is other countries would expand, filling the vacuum America leaves behind. How we must judge America's leadership, therefore, is by comparing it to the available alternatives. If not America, what power do you believe would make the best leading world military hegemon? I don't mean to say the U.S. is the best we could've hoped for; it's possible a united Europe would be preferable. I don't know and that's not the point. The U.S. only needs to be better than other countries poised to take the spotlight today and in the last century. I would claim: 1. In the mid-1900s, the U.S. was clearly better than the Axis powers. 2. In the latter part of the 1900s, the U.S. was clearly better than the USSR. 3. In the modern era, the U.S. is clearly better than its modern enemy nations (Iran, Russia, China, including their proxy states from Ansar Allah to North Korea). This alone is likely enough to conclude the U.S. is a net force for good in the world. It's continuously engaged against an enemy, and in each case, if you had to choose a side, the U.S. is the side of good. Perhaps if you compare the U.S. to the government of, say, Denmark, it might not look favourable, but that's a loss of perspective. A comparison that might be useful: suppose we're in a rough city where the police are fighting gangs and street crime, but also, every so often, engage in misconduct of their own. Say they arrest a person for bad reasons, they shoot a person who's innocent, they use excessive force and beat somebody who gave them a hard time. They're far from perfect. But would it be better to have no police at all? If they all quit and criminals could operate and attack people openly with no recourse? A police force that engages in misconduct and needs improvement is likely still a net force for good, in the sense we're better off having it than not having it, because it's better than the alternative. **2. Sometimes the alternative is hard to see.** Let's suppose you're a military general and there's a terrorist leader that we're looking for. We have intelligence to say he's in a building complex at the north end of town. We don't know with certainty this is the case, as our intel could be wrong, and it could even be a trap, but we're mostly confident that he's there because a few people who have been reliable in the past reported seeing him go there. There are some trucks coming in the evening from the south to meet him, and there might be civilians in the building complex, but he's planning to bomb an airport that has far more civilians than what might be in that building complex. What should we do? We could try to intercept the trucks. We could try to evac the airport. We could send ground forces in to approach the building. We could also just bomb the building. There are pros and cons to every approach, and each approach involves grappling with various unknowns and taking on a certain amount of risk. No matter what path we go down, success is not guaranteed. Things could go wrong. Some things are less likely to go wrong than others, and some failures would be more costly than others. And doing nothing would be a failure of its own, because then the terrorist group bombs the airport. Time is ticking. You have to make a call. What's your order? If you choose to bomb the building and it turns out our intel was wrong and he wasn't there, it's easy for a news report to simply say "the military bombed a building; innocents died." But what if it turned out he *was* there, and we *didn't* bomb the building? That could have turned out to be a far worse outcome than us accidentally killing innocents in the building. What if instead we intercept the trucks, have our people drive them into location, and it turns out the terrorist leader *was* in the building waiting to meet with them, and the result of us driving in is a huge ground battle that results in casualties on our side and the terrorist leader being wounded, but getting away. In that case, we might look back and wish that we had just bombed the building. When a military does something and you hear about the results on the news, you only see what happens. You don't see the other options that were available to them, you don't see the information they were working with, you don't see the risks they were managing. If you can only judge a thing by its alternatives, then it's very hard to judge military action because you simply don't know the alternatives, because that's all classified military intel they're not giving to the press. There are people who audit their decision-making to ensure they're making the right calls, but those people work on the inside as part of the system, so you as a civilian don't get to be privy to any of it. In the end, it's fairly difficult to judge military decisions from the outside. Even if it seems like something bad happens, we can't be sure the alternative wouldn't have been even worse. This is a perspective that many people lack because they see what happened, and they don't see the alternative that didn't. I am reminded, while writing this, of Frédéric Bastiat's 1850 essay Ce qu'on voit et ce qu'on ne voit pas ("What we see and what we don't see"), in which he deals with how difficult it is for some people to perceive opportunity cost, because we more easily see the things that we spend our time and money on than the alternate things that we *could've* spent that same time and money on but didn't. So too is this difficulty present when comparing the U.S. with its international enemies. What does a world in which the U.S. is the leading military hegemon look like? We know the flaws or problems of such a world because it's the one in which we live. It's less apparent how bad the world would be if the U.S. collapsed and today the USSR ruled the globe because you never got to see that timeline. It didn't happen. There is nothing to look at, nothing to examine, no practical case to point to. Therefore, we can only judge U.S. hegemony by its alternative, but the alternative is hard to "see." It's not a spectacle. The evening news can't run footage of all the stuff that isn't happening. **3. Many of the popular stories you think you know have probably been horribly oversimplified and one-sided.** When people in the West study history as kids in school, we focus only on the biggest themes and conflicts, because history is too vast to address everything. You likely remember learning about WWII, and hearing about but not focusing nearly as much on WWI. If you live in America, you probably don't spend any time on the Napoleonic Wars despite its historical importance because it's not as pertinent to America; instead you probably learn about the U.S. Civil War and maybe the Vietnam War, which students in other countries aren't given to focus on at all. The U.S. was a major participant in the Korean War, but from what I've heard American schools barely touch on that at all and most American adults don't know anything about it beyond that it happened in Korea in the 1950s. Then you're out of school and unless you choose to take history in post-secondary school, you're on your own to happen upon information about other things your country got up to. The case against American meddling in other countries is almost always factually skewed by leaving out balancing context, and exaggerated in scope by overstating America's influence on the outcomes. To that end, two things are typically downplayed. First, the divisions within the countries where the U.S. was involved, whose people are treated with diminished agency, or spoken of as though they had been united prior to the U.S. overthrowing their governments. In reality these countries were likely divided between multiple warring factions fighting for control, and all the U.S. had to do was provide intelligence and resources to one side to tip the scales and allow them to succeed. Second, the role of the USSR. It's common to overhear that the U.S. was involved in regime change in Latin America, for example, but without mentioning the Soviet Union was present in all the same countries, trying to foster communist revolutions to create Soviet-aligned dictatorships that would give them a foothold in the region (the success case for this is Cuba). A great example of this pattern often brought up online is Chile, where in 1973 the army general Augusto Pinochet deposed the socialist Chilean president Salvador Allende. Pinochet then dissolved the Chilean congress, suspended the constitution, violently purged thousands of leftists and other dissidents, famously throwing some out of helicopters (an execution method that became known as *death flights*), and ruled Chile as a military dictator for the next 17 years. In many retellings of these events, the coup is described as U.S.-backed, or things are so reduced that you might see people say things such as "the U.S. overthrow Allende." The conclusion is that the U.S. chose a repressive dictatorship over a democracy simply because they didn't like that the Chilean people chose a socialist. Omitted is that Allende had relatively little support to begin with, barely getting elected in 1970 but immediately trying to use his position to radically transform the Chilean economy. His government took over whole sectors of the economy including copper mining, steel production, and finance. Within a couple years the economy had crashed, everybody in the country was uniting against him, but he planned on carrying on in this way. The courts sided against him, the Chilean congress passed a constitutional amendment to explicitly limit his government's power and Allende simply ignored them, and finally [a majority of the congress passed a scathing resolution](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Agreement_of_the_Chamber_of_Deputies_of_Chile ) declaring his government illegal and asking the Chilean military to remove him from power. The only involvement the U.S. had was sharing intelligence with the Chilean military beforehand. The more you look into almost any of these stories of American imperial malfeasance, the weaker and less convincing these one-sided vilifications of America become.
