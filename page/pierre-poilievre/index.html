This is partly because the dynamics of institutional borrowing is very different from the dynamics of personal borrowing. Not only is it normal for big companies to have billions in debt (at time of writing, Apple has C$116 billion, Microsoft C$55 billion, Amazon C$180 billion), but it'd be foolish for them *not* to have debt because they can do more with the money they borrow than it costs them in interest to borrow it. In finance, this is called *leverage*. The result is that "having more debt" simply isn't an indicator of how well a company is doing financially. Things are even more complex when talking about national debt. Most countries have the ability to issue more of their own currency in a pinch, allowing them to ease their debts in exchange for inflation (this is why federal spending is fundamentally different from spending at other levels of government, like the city or province, which don't control the money supply as the federal government does). When examining the debt of a private company, you might compare this against their total assets or revenues --- Microsoft is holding C$55 billion in debt, but they reportedly own over C$950 billion in total assets, and they move over C$380 billion in annual revenue --- but how do you make comparisons like this when it comes to a whole country? To start, we could talk about our annual gross domestic product (GDP), which is all revenue of everything produced in the country per year; or the total value of all Canadian assets (which is tricky to calculate, but we do have *some* idea). Canada's federal national debt is about C$1.35 trillion, but provincial governments can also hold debts. Add those in and you get about C$2.4 trillion. Wait, no, that figure is substracting the value of government-owned assets. If you don't factor those out, total federal and provincial *gross debt* reaches about C$3.7 trillion. In 2025, our GDP was about C$3.3 trillion, meaning our debt-to-GDP ratio is as low as 39% or as high as 112% depending on how you look at it. Statistics Canada [estimates that our of our national net worth](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/260316/dq260316b-eng.htm), the sum of our national wealth and foreign asset position, was $19.3 trillion in 2025. This fluctuates significantly over time due to changes in the estimated value of our national resources. We should also realize these values are different from the government's revenue, meaning the money they pull in from the country. The *country* may have trillions in wealth across every person's house and bank account and business and in the ground, but only a portion of that is available to the government via taxation, and there's a political cost for increasing the tax burden, which further complicates this whole dynamic. So, are we in good standing as a country? I don't know. Our quality of life is good, so probably? But it's complicated, and I'm not the right person to ask. The takeaway here is that if you think you understand how government budgeting work because you've bought things for yourself on a credit card before: you really don't. It's not a good comparison. It doesn't help us understand what's really happening. The same intuition you have about one doesn't translate to the other. Moreover, the reason credit cards are intuitively frightening for the average person is because credit cards are a form of unsecured loan (meaning there's no collateral for the lender), which therefore charge very high interest rates. In that sense, it's nearly the opposite of national debt, which is very secure and comes to us at low interest rates. Running up a credit card is also something we associate with an emergency or something you turn to doing as a last resort, not as a viable way of financing investments. It would obviously sound unwise to make investments or start a business by taking out a cash advance on credit, but that's also nothing like what's happening when a government borrows money. If we wanted a *closer* analogy for what's happening, it might be to say national debt is like a student loan or mortgage --- which still neglects the differences between institutional and personal borrowing, but that at least gets closer in spirit to what's being discussed (and there *is* such a thing as taking out too much in loans for school or property, so it's not like that type of borrowing is unimpeachable). #### Gambling or betting To call it **gambling** (or **betting**) is also fairly misleading.
