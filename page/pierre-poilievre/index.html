QC Premier 2003--2012 # Passport changes I'll begin with a simple, not-too-consequential example to illustrate Poilievre's deceptive and small-minded nature. The Canadian passport is periodically updated to add security features and change its design. It features faded images in the background of the visa pages, which are part of its anti-counterfeiting measures. The [previous version](https://www.canada.ca/en/news/archive/2012/10/harper-government-unveils-new-epassport-new-look-celebrates-canadian-history-while-boosting-security.html), issued in 2013 under the Harper government, was specifically made to be extra patriotic, [representing "the historical story of Canada"](https://www.thecanadianpressnews.ca/politics/what-ideas-were-left-on-the-cutting-room-floor-in-passport-redesign-well-never-know/article_62ca9968-ce50-5ddd-874b-92f371bec84e.html) by featuring [iconic national imagery](https://nationalpost.com/news/canada/new-canadian-passports-dark-and-incredible-secrets-revealed-but-youre-going-to-need-a-black-light) that most of us should recognize. This was the first Canadian "ePassport," featuring an embedded chip and adding these fluorescent images to prevent the information being tampered with, which is why these pictures were given so much emphasis (previous passports were much more minimalist). The [2023 redesign](https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/services/canadian-passports/new-passport-features.html) changed the featured artwork to more contemporary, nature-focused illustrations of things like wildlife, leaves, or people skiing. Sean Fraser, then Minister of Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship, said in May 2023 the new design was [the result of consultation](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/canada-new-passport-more-nature-fewer-history-1.6838308) that suggested **people like celebrating Canada's natural environment**. No word on why they chose that "corporate-style" artwork as opposed to depictions of real people and places, but nor was that the focus of many people's concerns. !images oar src:media/p-old-1.png | alt:An example of a page from the old passport (pre-2023). src:media/p-new-1.png | alt:An example of a page from the passport (2023 redesign). src:media/p-old-3.png | alt:An example of a page from the old passport (pre-2023). src:media/p-new-2.png | alt:An example of a page from the passport (2023 redesign). At a glance, I easily prefer the 2013 design concept, using the passport as a backdrop for quintessentially Canadian imagery. But what pictures are faded into the background of my passport also seems terribly unimportant --- *unless* you see it as yet another symptom of a much broader problem. Indeed, a criticism one could make of the new design is that it's a reflection how it's become less fashionable to acknowledge and celebrate Canadian history, often for fear of controversy about people or events from our past that don't live up to modern standards (which they shouldn't be being held up to anyhow). And this change wasn't the only instance of that. Only a year prior, a prominent downtown Toronto university long known as Ryerson [was renamed](https://www.torontomu.ca/media/releases/2022/04/ryerson-university-changing-its-name-to-toronto-metropolitan-uni/) to Toronto Metropolitan University, ditching "Ryerson" because some activists presented a case against [Egerton Ryerson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egerton_Ryerson) (1803--1882), for whom it was named at its founding in 1948. If the school had never been named anything else to begin with, "Toronto Met" doesn't sound terrible, but why the change? Ryerson was a passionate school superintendent responsible for pushing the expansion of universal education in Ontario throughout the latter half of the 19th century. His crime? He thought trying to educate the Indigenous population was a good idea, and, as part of that, figured we should effectively convert them all to Christianity by teaching scripture and prayer as part of that education. That was part of *our* education, after all, so why wouldn't it be part of theirs? He wasn't directly involved in the residential school system, and the fact he expressed support for this idea was only one small part of a long and otherwise positive career, but this proximity to controversy was somehow enough to give people the ick. Around the same time, some people were trying to get the Toronto government [to rename Dundas Street](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/toronto-dundas-street-tory-staff-report-1.6082798), a major street running from Old Toronto all the way down to Hamilton, because they found a reason to be upset with with Henry Dundas (1742--1811), the British politician for whom it was named. His crime? Dundas was an abolitionist who fought against slavery, but after motions in the British parliament to abolish slavery had been voted down, he suggested amending their proposal to instead pursue a gradual abolition instead of an immediate one, thinking that more likely to be accepted. Taking this one fact alone and ignoring the rest of his life, activists reframed Dundas as somebody who tried to "delay abolition" and therefore petitioned to remove any references to him from public life. (The city later decided not to rename the whole street, but they did rename other things that bore his name, including a transit station, a library, and a downtown intersection called Yonge--Dundas Square, now called "Sankofa Square" --- named after some concept from an African culture in Ghana, despite the lack of any sort of connection to the area, and despite [the overwhelming majority of Torontonians opposing](https://toronto.citynews.ca/2024/01/08/yonge-dundas-square-name-change-sankofa-square/) the change.) I could go on, as there were many other examples like this. There was a broad push in those years to sanitize how we relate to history, much of it seemingly inspired by Americans trying to take down certain Confederate statues, taking that theme far beyond the point of reason. With all this going on in the background, it's understandable why somebody would dislike a government office trying to replace depictions of historical feats of national leaders with cartoony drawings of owls and people canoeing, if over-sensitive activism was their motivation. However, our passport is not the right place to centre such a conversation. First because it's so low-stakes: a passport isn't something most of us interact with often, and the images on it are intentionally low-visibility. It's not something you stop and focus on, so it's of limited salience. Second, because Mr. Fraser explicitly denied any of the changes were motivated by history being perceived as offensive, nor that any consultation indicated the public even felt this way, and I have no reason to not believe him. It sounds like they simply wanted to do something *different*; random people surveyed about it said they liked nature; and so the government hired artists to make some nature-themed visuals. It's believable that there was no political motivation whatsoever. We could still be of the opinion that historical images *shouldn't* be removed and make a case for keeping them (or rather, having them return when passports are updated again in the future), even granting that offense about history wasn't the reason for removal. But it couldn't just be about the passports; that would need to be a jumping-off point for a broader conversation about how we feel about our history and national identity, including in other cases, like renaming streets, changing symbols, or taking down statues. The result is that "having more debt" is simply not an indicator of how well a company is doing financially. Things get even more complex when talking about national debt because most countries have the ability to issue more of their own currency in a pinch, allowing them to ease their debts in exchange for inflation (this is why federal spending is different from spending at other levels of government which don't control the money supply as the feds do). When examining the debt of a private company, you might compare this against their total assets or revenues --- Microsoft is holding C$55 billion in debt, but they reportedly own over C$950 billion in total assets, and they move over C$380 billion in annual revenue --- but how do you make comparisons like this when it comes to a whole country? We could try annual gross domestic product (GDP), which is all revenue of everything produced in the country per year; or the total value of all Canadian assets (which is tricky to calculate, but we do have *some* idea). Wait, no, that figure is subtracting the value of government-owned assets. If you don't factor those out, total federal and provincial *gross debt* reaches about C$3.7 trillion. I've yet to see there's more to it than that. # The importance of an opposition party I don't want the government to be ruled only by one perspective, even if it's the one I agree with most. The **adversarial nature** of a parliamentary democracy --- the fact there are multiple sides in competition for the same seats, who have an incentive to oppose and criticize one another, within reason --- is part of what keeps the system well functioning. If the governing party introduces a bill, there should be opposition parties ready to make the argument against it, to articulate any flaws or downsides they perceive, and to tell us what they would do differently. Likewise for the actions of government departments, where it's valuable for the opposition to organize a shadow cabinet and to be attentive to what to government is doing. If you don't like the policies enacted by our current IRCC, you can listen to the shadow IRCC minister tell us what they would do differently. The official opposition should represent an alternate path --- not a radical opposite, but something at least different --- so that if things are no longer working out with our current governing party, the country *could* pass the torch to the opposition and let them form government instead. This is why the opposition party and their cabinet, organized as a mirror of the actual government and ready to take the reins if they're called upon, is called the **government in waiting**. An opposition party can influence the country through critique without ever winning a majority or forming government. If the governing party has a position or policy and the opposition party criticises it consistently, and much of the public comes to agree with that criticism, the governing party may concede and change their policy, recognizing that they stand to lose support from voters if they don't. Recently the obvious example of this happening was the consumer carbon tax, which came into effect under a Liberal government in 2019, but [was then effectively removed](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/mark-carney-drops-carbon-tax-1.7484290) by a subsequent Liberal government in 2025 because many people agreed with the opposition Conservative Party, who were criticizing that tax and calling for its removal. This back-and-forth happens all the time and is part of how democratic governments function: the governing party has to *prevent* opposition parties from gaining ground, which often means compromising with their concerns or demands. What makes a good opposition party? It's a given that it will disagree with the governing party on policy. It should also be **truthful**, **civil**, and **law-abiding** in their opposition. Its members should live up to the formality of being addressed as *the honourable* so and so. The problems I have with the Conservative Party today are not rooted in policy, but in Mr. Poilievre's dishonourable conduct. At this point the thought of him serving as Prime Minister is one I find deeply uncomfortable if not somewhat frightening. And I don't want to feel this way about our country's Official Opposition. Until Poilievre came along, I never had. It's not like there were no options but Poilievre. He's not irreplaceable. Erin O'Toole was better suited for leadership. So was Jean Charest, albeit I understand he may have been on the moderate side for the Conservative Party given what the base was looking for at the time. But Charest has a depth of experience, perspective, and connections, and like O'Toole was a better, more civil, more honourable, and more truthful person. Either men would be better leaders in terms of personality, disposition, and character. In 2021, had the Conservative Party won more seats, Mr. O'Toole may have become Prime Minister, and I felt no great qualms about that possibility as I do now about the possibility of a Prime Minister Poilievre. Even if I disagreed with O'Toole about some other issue another, he was a good and reasonable person. I could sleep well with O'Toole in government. The other side of having a bad opposition party is that it unreasonably raises the threshold of tolerance that people will have for the governing party. In other words, the worse the opposition is, the more the government can get away with before losing my support. This has a similar effect as political polarization: making it so people feel locked in to one side because they can't bring theirselves to support the other. I want the Conservative Party to feel like a viable alternative to the Liberal Party. But today, Mr. Carney has a lot of leeway to mess things up before I'd ever consider supporting Poilievre over him. I'm not happy about that. # How long will Poilievre be around?
