# Pierre Poilievre !image-span media/poilievre.png | Pierre Poilievre, Conservative Party of Canada Leader In the post-Harper years, after the Liberal Party came roaring back under Justin Trudeau in 2015, the Conservative Party of Canada took some time figuring out what it wanted to be. Andrew Scheer led from 2017 until stepping down in 2020. Then the party elected Erin O'Toole, but ousted him in 2022, dissatisfied after not making gains in the 2021 election, and on the perception that O'Toole campaigned as a firm conservative but had started going back on that, trying to get the party to move toward the centre. In the ensuing 2022 leadership race, **Pierre Poilievre** promised to take the party in a less moderate direction. Jean Charest, also in the running for party leader, criticized Poilievre for supporting the controversial trucker convoy ongoing in Ottawa, but Poilievre didn't shy away from the issue --- he leaned into it, turning around and criticizing Charest for "repeating Liberal lies about the truckers," and for being too coöperative with the Liberal agenda, like the consumer carbon tax. Poilievre went on to win the party leadership race by a large margin, and has been Conservative Party leader ever since. In the years that followed, I have been increasingly concerned and dissatisfied with the party's direction under Mr. Poilievre's leadership. He is apparently a compelling speaker, but he is not what this country, nor the Conservative Party, should be looking for in a leader. I have some disagreements with him in policy, but more visceral and important is my dislike for his conduct, his manner of personal attack, and his general dishonesty. In this post, I'll explore examples that I believe are illustrative of my point. # Passport changes The Canadian passport is periodically updated to add security features and change its design. Each version has various faded images in the background. The previous version had depictions of historical imagery, whereas [the 2023 redesign](https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/services/canadian-passports/new-passport-features.html) has more contemporary, nature-focused imagery of leaves, people skiing, and wildlife. Sean Fraser, our Minister of Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship, responsible for the department of the same name, [said in May 2023](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/canada-new-passport-more-nature-fewer-history-1.6838308) the new design was the result of consultation that suggested people liked celebrating Canada's natural environment. !image-span media/p-old-1.png | An example of a page from the old passport (pre-2023). media/p-new-1.png | An example of a page from the passport (2023 redesign). media/p-old-3.png | An example of a page from the old passport (pre-2023). media/p-new-2.png | An example of a page from the passport (2023 redesign). It's definitely not a big deal either way --- unless you see it as a symptom of a broader problem. A criticism one could make is that changes like this reflect how it's become less fashionable to acknowledge and celebrate Canadian history, often for fear of being controversial when figures in our past don't live up to modern standards. And this change wasn't the only one like it. A year prior, **Ryerson University**, a downtown Toronto school founded in 1948, [was renamed Toronto Metropolitan University](https://www.torontomu.ca/media/releases/2022/04/ryerson-university-changing-its-name-to-toronto-metropolitan-uni/) because some activists made an issue with Egerton Ryerson (1803--1882). And some people were talking about [getting the city to rename](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/toronto-dundas-street-tory-staff-report-1.6082798) **Dundas Street** because they made a problem with Henry Dundas (1742--1811). I could talk about the specifics of why I didn't agree with the activists on those two examples, but suffice to say I'm generally against this kind of historical purging, and these were only two of many examples that comprise a broad push to sanitize history ongoing in recent years (much of it seemingly inspired by Americans trying to remove Confederate statues, but running with that theme far beyond the point of reason). I don't like the feeling that some government office is trying to replace depictions of our historical feats or national leaders with cartoony drawings of owls or people canoeing if this kind of oversensitivity were their motivation. However, I don't know the passport is the right place to centre such a conversation. It's so low-stakes: your passport isn't something most of us interact with often, and the images are intentionally low-visibility. It's also worth noting that Mr. Fraser denied that any of the changes were motivated by history being perceived as offensive, or that any of their consultation indicated the public felt this way, and I have no reason not to believe that. It sounds like they simply wanted to do something *different*, and, when surveyed about it, people said they liked nature, so they hired some artists to make some generic nature-themed imagery. We could, nonetheless, be of the opinion that historical images *shouldn't* be removed and make a case for keeping them (or for having them return when the passports are updated again), small though this one issue may be, and even granting that a sensitivity about history likely wasn't the reason for their removal. And it could serve as a jumping-off point for a broader conversation about how we feel about our history and national identity, including in other cases, like renaming things or taking down statues. Poilievre was not joking. # Sovereign wealth fund In April 2026, the government announced that Canada was going to be [developing a sovereign wealth fund](https://www.cbc.ca/news/business/canada-sovereign-wealth-fund-explainer-9.7179217), which it calls the "Canada Strong Fund." This means an account of financial assets owned by the government, which can be used to guide strategic investment, among other things. It's an increasingly popular idea (for example, the U.S. announced [its intent to develop one](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/02/a-plan-for-establishing-a-united-states-sovereign-wealth-fund/) earlier this year) because it seems to offer a useful tool for governments to play a productive role in a capitalist economy. What you may immediately realize, however, is that not all countries have a budget surplus with which to build such a fund. Some countries do, but Canada doesn't. This means it must be created either by increased taxes or by debt. This is the case for literally everything the government does, of course, but the argument could be made that perhaps it isn't worth trying to hold assets in this way unless we have a net surplus. Was that conversation deemed too complex for the public to follow? #### Debt fund Poilievre led the charge, [telling reporters](https://www.cbc.ca/player/play/video/9.7178881) that "Carney has no surplus and therefore no wealth to put in such a fund. He's talking about a **sovereign debt fund**." What does this even mean? Are we also building debt roads and debt hospitals? Poilievre was among many people to suggest that it's not even accurate to call it a wealth fund unless it's owned by a net surplus nation, which would be a rule that he's just invented. The "wealth" in "wealth fund" simply refers to what it is that's being managed, and is arguably redundant (it could simply be called a "sovereign fund"). It's not a "budget surplus fund." Poilievre continued, saying Carney "wants to put another 25 billion dollars on the **national credit card**, to **gamble** on a Liberal **slush fund** that will enrich **Liberal insiders** at the expense of hard-working Canadians." His rhetoric is neat and efficient: so much is implied in so few words. But so much of what is implied is wrong. And this isn't the only time he would hit all these same points (later that day [in the House, Poilievre said](https://youtu.be/dBdlv6s1VsI?t=311) the government planned to set up an office that will "borrow on the **national credit card** to place **bets** on **Liberal-chosen corporations**," hitting the same beats as at this presser). #### Credit card First, it's incredibly misleading to liken national debt to a credit card. This is partly because the dynamics of institutional borrowing is very different from the dynamics of personal borrowing. It's not only normal for big companies to have billions in debt (e.g. at time of writing, Apple has C$116 billion, Microsoft C$55 billion, Amazon C$180 billion) --- it'd be foolish for them *not* to have debt, because they can do more with the money they borrow than it costs them in interest (in finance, this is called leverage). The result is that "having more debt" simply isn't an indicator of a company being worse off financially. Things get even weirder when you start talking about national debt. Most countries have the ability to issue more of their own currency in a pinch, allowing them to ease their debts in exchange for inflation (this is why federal spending is fundamentally different from spending at other levels of government, like the city or province, which don't control the money supply as the federal government does). And when examining the debt of a private company, you might compare this against their total assets or revenues. Microsoft is holding C$55 billion in debt. They reportedly own over C$950 billion in total assets. They move over C$380 billion in annual revenue. How do you make comparisons like this when it comes to a whole country? To start, we could talk about our gross domestic product (GDP), which is all revenue of everything produced in the country annually; or the total value of all Canadian assets (which is tricky to calculate, especially when factoring in natural resources, but we do have *some* idea). Canada's national debt is about C$1.35 trillion. In 2025, our GDP was about C$3.3 trillion. Statistics Canada [put out an estimate](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/260316/dq260316b-eng.htm) of our "national net wealth" at $19.3 trillion in 2025. But also, these values are different from the government's revenue, meaning the money they pull in from the country. The country may have trillions in wealth, but only a portion of that is made available to the government through taxes, and there's a political cost for increasing taxes, which further complicates the whole dynamic. So, are we in good standing as a country? I don't know. Our quality of life seems to be good, so probably? But it's complicated. The takeaway here is that if you think you understand how government budgeting work because you've bought things for yourself on a credit card before: you don't. Moreover, the reason credit cards are intuitively frightening for the average person is that, because credit cards are a form of unsecured loan (meaning there's no collateral for the lender), they charge very high interest rates. It's nearly the opposite of national debt, which is very secure and comes to us at low interest rates. A credit card is also something that's often used in a pinch as a last resort, not as a viable way of financing investments. It would obviously sound unwise to make investments by taking out a cash advance from a credit card and using that to buy stocks or start a business, but that's also nothing like what's happening here. If we wanted a *closer* analogy, it would be that national debt is like a student loan or mortgage --- which still neglects the differences between institutional and personal borrowing, but gets closer in spirit to what's being discussed (and there *is* such a thing as taking out too much in loans for school or property, so it's not like that borrowing is unimpeachable). #### Gambling or betting To call it **gambling** (or **betting**) is also fairly misleading. Poilievre wants us to visualize Mr. Carney as a gambler at a casino, shaking dice and throwing our money away with a low chance of getting it back. But why should we? Presumably his rationale is that it might not work out as planned, and if it doesn't then it's going to be costly for the country. That's naïvely true: there are many times a government spends money in a way that could be called gambling in that sense. If a city spends money on a sports stadium, is that gambling? Maybe, but not in the same way it is to bet on horse racing. Is Poilievre against funding any new projects? They all involve risk. If Poilievre wanted to argue there's an unacceptable *level* of risk to the proposal at hand, he could speak to that more soberly and in specific detail, rather than slipping in a word to imply as much, which we're not likely to focus on. His use of this term doesn't make any sense. A slush fund is a pool of money owned by an organization, but which is kept separate from the main budget, and probably kept hidden, so you can dole it out for personal, miscellaneous reasons without oversight, regulation, or disclosure. The idea is that it would then be used for illicit purposes. Is a publicly managed national holding a slush fund? It doesn't seem to match the common meaning of the term at all. Poilievre started using this term simply because "slush fund" has a negative connotation, and he wants the audience to *feel* negatively about the idea --- but without having to justify that negativity, because we're not likely to stop and focus on his use of this term among everything else being said. This is part of what makes Poilievre an effective speaker. He does a lot of "sneaking in." #### Insiders The suggestion that it's going to enrich **Liberal insiders** means if the government uses the fund to invest in "nation-building infrastructure," then perhaps that investment will be directed toward regions with Liberal Party representation, or given to companies that have some kind of financial tie to Liberal Party leadership. That's the fear, anyway. But what substantiates it in this case? And what could be done about it? It's certainly not a unique concern: *any* time the government spends money, it *could* be used in a partisan way that benefits those connected to the governing party or coalition. Moreover, it's not even clear how the sovereign wealth fund proposal adds to this risk, compared with the government taxing or borrowing money to fund infrastructure projects directly without the fund. To be sure, preventing public funds from being directed in a partisan manner is a constant fight in every country that tries to prevent it. It may always happen to *some* extent. We tend to create separate departments, that aren't under direct control by politicians, to award contracts for building infrastructure, for example. We also have independent auditors built into the system who can alert of instances where money is being given disproportionately for political reasons or without objective merit. As for the proposed sovereign wealth fund? The government says [it will be managed by an independent board of directors](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-finance/news/2026/04/canada-strong-fund.html), not by politicians of the governing party. If that isn't enough, why not? The result in either case is low quality discourse that fails to meaningfully challenge the government, as one should want an opposition party to do. # The CBC and Twitter In 2023, Twitter changed its policy regarding state-run outlets controlled by governments like Russia, Iran, or China, labelling them "state-affiliated media." This was to indicate their bias for readers: they're not independent journalistic companies, but mouthpieces for their respective regimes. Twitter then labelled Western publicly-funded outlets like NPR and the CBC the same way, then shortly updated it to say "government-funded media." This second label was technically accurate, but obviously misleading, especially given the timing of it being applied shortly after the "state-affiliated media" tag being applied to outlets like RT. In addition to being unnecessary, it could easily send the wrong message to readers. The CBC issued a [statement](https://x.com/CBCRadioCanada/status/1648033861152960531), insisting their "journalism is impartial and independent," that "to suggest otherwise is untrue," and stopped using Twitter entirely as a result of the label. They weren't alone. Twitter later walked this back and [removed these labels](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2023/4/21/twitter-drops-state-affiliated-government-funded-labels) --- but not before Poilievre noticed and celebrated, writing [on his Twitter page](https://x.com/PierrePoilievre/status/1647750040142876674) that the CBC had been "**officially exposed as 'government-funded media'**" and that "**now people know it is Trudeau propaganda, not news**." What does Poilievre mean by "exposed"? Nothing new had been revealed. We all know the CBC is a Crown Corporation that receives public funding. Why is Poilievre acting like this American company had taught us anything? The description "Trudeau propaganda, not news" is even more absurd. It's too reminiscent of Donald Trump calling everything fake news. There is no reasonable interpretation here that could be taken seriously. The CBC doesn't broadcast information about recent events? What are we doing, Pierre? The CBC is not controlled by Trudeau nor by the government at all. It simply receives public funding appropriated to it by Parliament. This is not by any reasonable definition "state media." The criticism that some people make of public funding for media is that it *may* create an incentive for those entities to be less critical of the governing party, or more critical of parties that want to defund them. Even if this were the case, the CBC would still be a legitimate news outlet --- it would just have a certain bias, as all other news outlets surely do as well. (How strong this bias for the governing party truly is, and whether that's enough of a downside to scrap the CBC altogether, is the meat of the debate.) The misleading labelling of the CBC and other public broadcasters was brought on by the site's owner, Elon Musk, who has an ideological bend against media whose coverage he disagrees with. Mr. Musk is not at all concerned about truth or fighting bias in journalism.
