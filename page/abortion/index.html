!meta title: A case for abortion date: 2026-02-18 see-also: substack|the-case-for-abortion see-also: tumblr|809547051047272448 see-also: patreon|155199340 include: toc The way I propose we think about questions of legality is to consider the most direct implications of the question. **Do you endorse violence being used in an instance of noncompliance?** That is, to deter an illegal action or compel a legal obligation. For abortion, imagine you're in a hospital and a doctor is about to perform an abortion on a pregnant woman who has requested one. A police officer is there and defers to your judgment: should he intervene to stop this abortion from taking place? Using violence if necessary, in the event they refuse and fight back. That is the question we must answer. And my answer is no. It would be immoral to interfere and stop them. I'm in favour of abortion being legal---always, with no limitation, and with no rationale necessary by the person requesting one. If you are pregnant and no longer wish to be, then you should be able to end your pregnancy at any time, with only one consideration. The detail people often miss is that **the removal of a fetus from its mother does not necessarily result in the death of the fetus**. It may be able to survive on its own, which is called viability. If the fetus is viable, then it's reasonable to suppose we have an obligation to facilitate that life. If, on the other hand, the fetus is not viable, then alas, we allow it to die. Either way, the pregnant person should have a right to have it removed. ## Clarifications #### How would I describe abortion as an action? Let's think of all human action as either good, bad, or neutral. Then divide good and bad behaviour by whether or not it's also legally mandated or prohibited, respectively. !table this { margin: auto; line-height: 1.5; } this td { border: 1px solid var(--grey-8) } this .col-1 { max-width: 300px; } this .col-2 { max-width: 250px; } this .row-1 td { background-color: var(--bg-cream); } this .row-2 .col-1, this .row-3 .col-1 { background-color: var(--bg-light-green); } this .row-2 .col-2, this .row-3 .col-2 { background-color: var(--bg-light-red); } this .row-3 td { filter: brightness(0.9) saturate(200%); } **neutral** behaviour that isn't meaningfully good or bad .e.g. drinking a glass of water **virtue** (morally good) good, but not legally required .e.g. donating to charity | **vice** (morally bad) bad, but not legally prohibited .e.g. cheating on a romantic partner legal **duty**, **obligation**, or **mandate** good, and required (it is illegal to *not* do this) .e.g. paying back a loan | legal **prohibition** bad, and prohibited (it is illegal to do this) .e.g. stealing from a store I don't think anybody would argue abortion is a good or virtuous thing to do. It's either considered neutral or bad. We might also argue that being pregnant, and choosing to not get an abortion, is a virtuous thing to do. However, the placement of these choices within the moral categories of good, bad, and neutral is not very important. The focus here is whether abortion should be made illegal, or, put differently, whether carrying through with pregnancy should be a legal duty. #### Pro-abortion, anti-abortion The common terms to describe a person's attitude about abortion are **pro-choice** (for abortion) and **pro-life** (against abortion). These terms are both misleading and bias their own perspective. I prefer to be most direct and say pro-abortion and anti-abortion, and I mark myself as pro-abortion. Though it should go without saying, this does not mean I want people to get abortions for the sake of doing so. To get an abortion is typically a grim decision, as unpleasant for the mother as for anybody else in society. If the number of unwanted pregnancies decreased to zero and as such no abortions were performed, that would be a good outcome. I favour abortion in the same way I favour chemotherapy. This is a fitting analogy in numerous ways. To get chemotherapy is unpleasant, but it's less unpleasant than dying of cancer, and we prefer to live in a society where chemo is an option rather than one where it's not. And if I describe myself as "pro-chemo", nobody would interpret this to mean I want people to take chemo for no reason, or that I want more people to get cancer for the purpose of maximizing our chemo consumption. It would be easily understood that I'm pro-chemo in the sense I favour it being an option for people to use in the event they have cancer. Likewise I'm pro-abortion in the sense that I favour it being an option for people who no longer wish to be pregnant. #### Fetus In discourse about abortion, we often use the term "fetus" as an imprecise catch-all for all prenatal human life. This is not technically accurate. From conception a human goes through numerous stages, typically given as zygote, then blastocyst, then embryo, and then finally a fetus, as it's designated for most of pregnancy (from before the end of the first trimester) until birth. Abortion can happen at any stage in pregnancy, and most happen during the embryonic stage, not the fetal, but we've become accustomed to talking about fetuses because it's during this stage that we perform the most controversial abortions, whose legality is most affected by policy disagreement. For my purposes here the distinction between a fetus, an embryo, or any other prenatal life is unimportant, and so any time I reference a fetus you can read this as a general reference to any such prenatal life. The term is also not intended to minimize or trivialize the value of what's being discussed: a human life, at a stage of development that we all went through ourselves at some point. I, too, was once a fetus. The fetus is sometimes referred to as "a clump of cells" by abortion proponents, which may be true in the most literal sense, but of course it also has the potential to be so much more. These medical terms are useful for their precision or specificity, but let's not be callous about what is at stake here. # The right to security of person, or, The right of bodily integrity For now, set aside any questions about the relative moral status of the fetus, as this is unnecessary for the argument I pose. I'll consider the fetus to be a full person, of equal or comparable moral weight to an adult. As you may be able to infer by this point, the argument I make is similar in approach to those posed by J.J. Thomson in her influential 1971 essay *A Defense of Abortion*. Why can the government not force you to undergo **medical experimentation**, to accelerate the rate of medical research? Or, if somebody is dying in a hospital bed from organ failure, why can the government not** force somebody to donate the organ or tissue they need**? These are both measures that would **save lives** in the grand scheme of things. And yet they're also intuitively horrific---things that we all know shouldn't be accepted but which you might find in a novel describing a possible dystopian future. We don't accept policies like these because we must consider the possibility we may one day be the ones who are subjected to them (see "the veil of ignorance" concept as popularized by Rawls), and because they conflict with a rather fundamental desire we all share, which is for **security of person**, that is, a right to maintain **bodily integrity**. (In this context, integrity doesn't mean dignity or respect, but rather the state of being whole, complete, or unimpaired---as in "structural integrity".) This principle is sometimes imprecisely called the right of bodily autonomy---but insofar as autonomy refers to your ability to act freely or make your own decisions, there are valid reasons to restrict a person's autonomy. We interfere with your autonomy when we send you to prison, for example. But to imprison somebody can be justified, and does not feel intuitively horrific in the way forcing somebody to undergo medical experimentation does. This is because only the latter affects the integrity of your body. This likely also explains why no modern country engages in sterilization or torture as forms of punishment, and why nearly all Western countries have stopped practicing the death penalty as punishment for any crimes. (The only exception is some parts of the United States---the same parts that are most enthusiastic about trying to restrict abortion, a coincidence that's probably meaningful.) To become pregnant means another human has become dependent on your body for their survival. The continuation of pregnancy causes a definite amount of bodily harm, as well as the risk of further, more serious harm, and possibly death. It's therefore something which, though virtuous, should be a voluntary arrangement only. You should not have a legal obligation to allow somebody else to sustain theirself at your expense in this way. If you express no longer consenting to the arrangement, we should allow you to detach yourself, even if this means this other human will die as a result. This is because the fact we might save their life isn't sufficient to justify trampling on your rights by imposing pregnancy onto the unwilling---just as the fact forced organ harvesting or forced medical experimentation would save lives wasn't sufficient to justify those practices either. From here, the argument becomes whether imposing a restriction on abortion can be justified. The opponents of abortion feel it can be, while those of us who are pro-abortion find their rational unconvincing. Their concern for the life of the fetus is not sufficient to justify overriding the mother's right to security of person. At this point, I find it worthwhile to note the absence of debate in our society about the wrongness of these other measures that would impose onto people similarly. It was always and without hesitation understood by all to be a line too far, that of course the government should not be able to force you to donate a kidney, let's say. The fact abortion has remained a glaring exception to this intuition for so long is most likely explained by how in the past **the people making decisions about matters of law were entirely men**. Women were not only generally disregarded but seen as instrumental in the process of birthing children for their husbands. Husbands were considered to have a right to sex with their wives, with or without their wives' consent (marital rape was therefore not a valid concept). It only seems natural that a society which does not grant women the right to decline sex in their own homes wouldn't pause for too long to consider if they should have a broader social right to security of person, and one that may interfere with men's ability to acquire children to boot. It's been said many times before, but I'll say it again, because it's no less true now than the last thousand times: if men could get pregnant, the right to an abortion would be codified immediately, and this would not even be a discussion. # Is abortion an act of killing? The simple argument for restricting abortion is that's killing a human being, which is something there's a legitimate public interest in preventing. That's why we have laws against murder. This is often posed as though it's obvious, an uncontroversial starting point we should all agree to. The implication of this framing is that people who are pro-abortion think likewise, but view abortion as an act of **justified killing**. I don't accept this. The question is not whether you can kill the fetus. It's whether you have a right to not save the fetus, or if you're forced to save it even at the expense of your own rights. This is worth understanding because there's a significant moral difference between **killing** and **not saving**. Let's imagine you walk by a large, powerful river and see a man has fallen in. If you were to jump in and rescue him, this would be a great act and you would surely be applauded---but in making such an attempt, you would put yourself at great risk of harm or death, so it's understandable and defensible if you choose not to. And nobody would say you're the one who killed this man by not saving it. Though you did choose, ostensibly, to let him die, you weren't the cause of his death, which is why you don't have a legal duty to jump in to make such an attempt. And morally speaking, this is where we find abortion, too. Some people object to this framing. They say allowing pregnancy to continue is the neutral path or the path of inaction, and getting an abortion is an active choice to interfere with pregnancy in such a way that causes the fetus to die. It's therefore absurd of me, they say, to compare abortion with standing at the riverbank doing nothing and to compare pregnancy with jumping into the river to save the drowning man. The action--inaction dynamic is reversed. In their view, abortion is more like if there were a man swimming in the river and you chose to throw a fishing net onto him, causing him to drown. The problem is the cause of death in the case of abortion isn't as simple as if you were to walk outside right now and shoot a random person with a gun. **The fetus is unable to live on its own** unaided by the womb of its mother because it hasn't yet developed the bodily systems necessary to do so (most urgently, it can't inhale and process oxygen). This is the only reason it dies---not because we make a concerted effort to prevent it from surviving. The only way to save its life would be to give it access to the bodily systems of its mother, and she's choosing to not allow that further. This is why it's best understood as choosing to not save the fetus as opposed to simply killing it. Conversely, in *some* abortions---generally performed after the first trimester and where a fetus has grown beyond a certain point---a doctor may deliberately kill the fetus before the procedure, probably through a lethal injection. This is done because in those cases, we know the fetus cannot survive on its own and death is certain. The alternative would be for the doctor to remove the fetus from its mother and allow it to immediately suffocate and die on a table in the operating room, in a way that's more drawn-out and probably more traumatic for the mother. This, particularly, may be what we call an act of justified killing, because it does appear to be kinder for all involved than allowing the fetus to die as it would have otherwise. And is that not so? Jon Snow killed Mance Rayder with a bow and arrow, but only because he was about to be burned to death by order of Melisandre. Jon didn't have the power to save Rayder's life---that was out of his hands---but he had the ability to save him from the fate of being burned alive, which is a far worse death than an arrow through the heart. So, do we condemn Jon Snow and call him a murderer for killing Rayder? I don't think anybody viewed it this way. There *is* such a thing as a justified killing. If the preëmptive lethal injection is all somebody is referring to when they say abortion is killing, they're free to argue for the alternative. Let them insist that, even if a fetus is going to die anyway, doctors must not be allowed to use lethal injection to improve the outcome. I don't know why anybody would be motivated to do this, or what it would accomplish even in their own view. The procedure that remains would still be an abortion (removing the fetus from its mother) and the fetus would still die, albeit more unpleasantly. And this allows us to distinguish between the two actions: first, performing an abortion, which is not killing more than it is neglecting to save; and second, performing a lethal injection *before* the abortion, because you understand the fetus won't survive the abortion anyway, and you feel this is a justified killing to prevent a worse fate. Even if it weren't misguided, **opposition to the latter is not opposition to abortion itself**. To return us to the drowning man in a river: a more comprehensive analogy may be to imagine there were a coil of rope nearby, that you quickly took one end and threw the other into the river for the man to grab onto; but after he does, you realize his weight combined with the force of the river is more than you expected, so you let go. Though letting go of the rope is *an action*, a decision you made, this only returns you to an initial state of inaction, prior to when you entered into an arrangement (holding the rope) that you weren't compelled to enter nor remain in. And here again, it's clear that nobody would say you killed this man. We would say you didn't save him, that you began saving him but then stopped, or made an attempt at saving him but failed---but you were not the cause of his death in the same way one would be if they had walked up to him on the street and shot him. If, unlike in reality, the fetus had been capable of living on its own until we inflicted a condition upon it that led to its demise, then we could reasonably say we're killing the fetus by doing so (throwing a fishing net onto the man so he can't swim, or something like that). But that isn't so. It's not the fault of the mother nor medical staff that the fetus doesn't yet have working lungs. It came into the world in a state of dependence. It was part of its nature, and not something any of us could've prevented. ## Counter-arguments #### But don't we have an obligation to preserve the life of a child in other circumstances? It wouldn't be okay for a mother to stop feeding her children and let them starve. This is partly true. We can enter into arrangements where we gain a duty to perform certain actions, and taking care of children can be one such obligation. But this is a different type of obligation from pregnancy, as it doesn't conflict with your right to security of person. It's just labour you agree to do, or resources of yours that you're compelled to provide. Nor is this an obligation you can't possibly withdraw from, as it's possible for somebody to stop being obliged to their children by relinquishing guardianship to the state or to some other person. I have seen arguments against abortion that ask us to imagine people stranded on a deserted island, or at a cabin in the woods when a snowstorm hits, or in some other scenario like this that isolates us from the rest of society and makes it so another person's life may be in your hands. In some scenarios, depending on the exact configuration of facts, it does seem reasonable to say people would have a duty to saves the lives of others, and one you may be justified to enforce (e.g. to make somebody share food rations instead of hoarding them all while the rest of you starve). But analogies like this all serve to remove bodily integrity from the equation, and therefore fail to inform us about abortion and pregnancy. Instead, we should have to imagine being stuck in an isolated cabin *and* being forced to undergo surgery to donate a kidney to save the life of a dying person. But at that point, how did your isolation add to the scenario? It's essentially the same as asking how we would approach the same problem in broader society, and leads to the same answer. #### Doesn't the mother have a special relationship with the fetus that justifies a moral duty, since it's her fault the fetus is in a state of dependence, as she's the one who put it into that scenario to begin with? A potential shortcoming of the analogy of the drowning man is it begins with the man already in the river. How did he get there? Some people would argue that---insofar as pregnancy *is* like saving a drowning man---a mother, by getting pregnant, is like the one who pushed him into the river in the first place, putting him at risk of drowning, but with the implied understanding that she will be the one who needs to keep him alive, say by throwing in a rope for him to hang onto while they both toward a safe zone further down the river. This justifies us imposing onto her a legal duty to continue pregnancy until completion. It's an interesting thought, though it may also expose the limits of the analogy. If you did push a man into a river and he drowns as a result, we wouldn't say the crime was not jumping in to save him. The crime was pushing him in to begin with. Even if you did jump in to save him (or throw him a rope or buoy), it would still have been a crime to push him in, as you've put him at great risk of injury or death (if your plan to save him fails). What does it look like to cast this back into speaking of pregnancy and abortion? If a woman gets pregnant and then has an abortion, would we say the crime was not the expulsion of her fetus, but the part where she got pregnant in the first place, thus putting the fetus into that state of dependence? We could argue that to get pregnant because in so doing you're putting the fetus into a situation where it's at high risk of death or injury, and dependent on others for its survival. It could therefore be seen as immoral, akin to pushing somebody into a river. But of course, this is only because there wasn't a conscious being to offer or deny agreement. It didn't exist yet. And all this risk and dependence is a necessary condition for life itself, not something being imposed for no reason. It could never be condemned or viewed negatively. To compare pregnancy with other instances of imposing risk onto somebody in this way could thus never work. If we wanted to explore this aspect of abortion while keeping it analogous with the drowning man, we may have to modify the latter by supposing it's indeed your fault the drowning man is in the river, but it was only by falling into this river that he was alive to begin with. Perhaps this is the sacred river of spawning, a holy site where a godlike being conjures new men into the world, but only at a woman's request. At this point most people would say the analogy has been tortured beyond its usefulness. There isn't anything in life that mirrors abortion perfectly. There's always going to be some way in which it's different from other scenarios if you examine the comparison deeply enough. The purpose of the analogy of the man drowning in a river was to illustrate the difference between killing and not saving, which it did effectively, despite not being identical to the dynamic or dilemma posed by abortion in every way. And it still articulates my own attitude, as the way most people would feel about your choice to not save the drowning man is roughly the same as how I feel about abortion, those differences notwithstanding. #### Isn't the mother giving implied consent by choosing to get pregnant? This is made as a retort to my saying pregnancy should be voluntary and that it would be wrong to force somebody to continue a pregnancy they no longer consent to. Some people argue the mother has implicitly given consent by choosing to get pregnant and that, once given, this consent cannot reasonably be withdrawn, because doing so puts another human's life in peril. There are some analogies for this argument. The one I've heard most is that if somebody commits to flying a charter plane for a passenger, they must be tacitly giving consent to being forced to continue flying the plane until it lands safely, because it would be unreasonable for them to take off but then claim to withdraw their consent midway through their flight, refusing to take any further action and allowing the plane to crash and their passenger to die. It's proposed---and I do agree---that it would be justified to use violence to force this person to continue piloting the plane. But how applicable is this to abortion? Like the analogy of the cabin in a snowstorm, we've removed the infringement of bodily integrity from the equation. I can't think of any other scenario where a person can't withdraw their consent from something that implicates their bodily integrity after an initial commitment. For a more meaningful comparison: if somebody agreed to donate part of their liver but later changed their mind, does this mean we can go to their house and force them to submit to surgery because they initially agreed, and we've decided that once granted this consent cannot be withdrawn? I also don't accept that we can say the mother has consented to pregnancy. If she explicitly states that she's having sex for the purpose of getting pregnant, then in theory one could believe this, though that information wouldn't be available to us when a person comes into a clinic and requests an abortion (and so we could never make policy based on this). But normally pregnancy is something that happens randomly, and often despite some effort to prevent it. What some people argue is that by having sex *at all*, a woman is implicitly consenting to any pregnancy that may occur because she understands this is a possible outcome of sex. But that clearly isn't how we talk about consent in any other scenario. That is, we don't say a person has consented to any possible outcome of something they choose to engage in. For example, I may understand that going out late at night involves a risk of being attacked by a mugger, but if that were to happen, you wouldn't say I consented to being assaulted and stolen from by leaving my home knowing it was a possibility. # When does virtue become obligation? Let's suppose a person agrees that abortion is not killing, but is instead neglecting to save. They might still argue that to continue with pregnancy should be an obligation because you should have a duty to save a fetus from dying. Let's explore this possibility more. Suppose you're walking outside and come across a large, industrial machine, like a garbage compactor, and you notice that a person has fallen inside. She explains her predicament: in a few minutes the machine will activate and crush her to death unless you save her. To do so, all that's required of you is to press the emergency stop button on the side of the machine. This will not only disarm the crushing mechanism, but also allow her to open the side door and escape. In a scenario like this, many people would argue that not only is pressing the button good, but that to *not* press the button would be morally wrong, a failing or defect on your part. It feels that we have a duty to act and press the button to save this person. It even feels to many people that we could be justified in using force to compel somebody to press the button, or punishing somebody afterwards for not doing so. To maintain clarity of thought, I'll add two subcategories of morally good action. First, those which are good but not necessary, that you would be applauded for doing but not condemned as immoral for not doing. This has been called "supererogation" (from the Latin for "extra payment"). Second, those which are morally good, and which you have a duty or expectation to perform. If you fail to do these things, you might be condemned or considered immoral for failing to do them. There's not a general answer, as all people believe in some moral duties that should be legally required and others that should not be. As for letting somebody die, it depends on where you are. In some countries, like France, to do nothing for a person in imminent danger may result in legal trouble. In other countries, like the United States, you may find there's no legal obligation to do anything at all, even if saving them were as simple as pressing a button and you instead stood and watched them die. Which approach you prefer is a matter of opinion and both opinions have merit. The problem is there's a clear distinction between, **first**, being able to save a person with the mere press of a button, and, **second**, putting yourself at great risk of harm or death in order to save somebody. This difference exists in our intuition as well as in law. Just as we cannot reasonable forbid self-defence (the law cannot compel you, in some scenarios, to simply give up and allow yourself to die), so we cannot demand you save another person's life *at the expense of your own*. In short, the law cannot punish people for acting out of self-preservation. Some people seem to forget that pregnancy imposes great costs onto the mother. For months, she endures pain and discomfort, fatigue, loss of mobility, and increased risk of various conditions and infections. Then giving birth is painful and risky, with many complications that may arise, some of them life-threatening. Even in a successful pregnancy where no particular medical complication arises and the baby is declared healthy, the mother's body sustains permanent damage resulting from the change it undergoes as part of that process. So, could we reasonably mandate that you must act to save others? Maybe, provided it doesn't require putting yourself in harm's way, and even then it's not a given. But pregnancy isn't like that, anyway. # The personhood question Say we invent a machine that can remove a fetus from its mother easily, without risk or pain, at any stage in pregnancy from the moment of conception onward. It then allows the fetus to live and grow in a medical chamber that simulates a real human uterus until maturity, at which point it can be removed, artificially "born" into the world as a baby. If this were so, some people may dislike the impersonal or artificial nature of this machine and prefer natural pregnancy, but any woman who wishes to terminate her pregnancy could use this machine without controversy, as the issue of the potential death of the fetus has been resolved. But one question would remain: at what point do we gain an obligation to reflect the life of the fetus, as we do a fully grown human? Is it from conception? If not, then when? The way many people ask this question is, at what point does a human gain **legal personhood**? How we answer has some practical implications. If a pregnant woman took a pill that killed the fetus inside her despite this imaginary machine I describe being available to her, would this be a crime, akin to if not murder? Would society have an obligation to sustain any prenatal life removed by this machine, even if we somehow ended up with billions of stored zygotes? This may also affect how you feel about in vitro fertilisation today, as the way this is done involves disposing of embryos. It does seem appealing to say a zygote, as it hasn't yet developed a brain or nervous system, is far less important than a more fully developed human. It doesn't even have a conscious experience of the world, so why does the way you treat it matter more than how you treat a desk or a chair? Then again, it also feels rather strange to speak of human life as though it has no value and could be thrown in the trash without regard. What I propose is that fetuses have great value *to us*, to humanity or society in the abstract, but without being moral patients theirselves. An analogy is that if I grew a tree in my yard, it might be valuable to me and if you were to cut it down I might be sad or upset, and it may even be immoral or illegal of you to do so because it's *my* tree and ergo you're destroying my property. But we wouldn't say you're being immoral *to the tree*, or talk about cutting down trees as though it's akin to murder. So an IVF clinic destroying embryos doesn't feel like murder to me and it probably doesn't make sense to say an embryo has rights or legal personhood, but I could imagine if, as a matter of policy or attitude, we still treated embryos as a valuable resource that one shouldn't destroy for no reason. # Bad pro-abortion arguments I've heard many arguments or phrases that attempt to say why it's important for abortion to be legal. These are often satisfying for people who agree with them, which is why they get said as slogans or one-liners, or typed into social media posts. But some of these arguments have problems that render them pointless and unpersuasive when directed at people who don't already agree with you about the underlying facts of what's being dismissed. The most common mistake is failing to distinguish between the death of a fetus during pregnancy and other instances of death, seemingly presupposing the frivolousness of the former---despite this often being the very thing in contention. #### You just want to control women's bodies. This leaves unclear why one shouldn't want to control another person's body for the purpose of stopping them from committing crime. Am I controlling men's bodies by telling them not to sexually harass their coworkers? We all face constraints by society at times. The only thing a person who's anti-abortion gets from this message is that you're trying to call them sexist or misogynistic in some way. And that's rather absurd, from their view: it's misogynistic to tell women they're not allowed to commit murder? #### Would you force a victim of rape to carry her rapist's baby? Imagine if instead of getting an abortion, a woman were murdering a toddler, strangling him in the bath. Would it matter if this woman had been the victim of a terrible crime herself at some point? No, that couldn't possibly justify killing the baby, no matter what it was. What if instead, this toddler were the child of somebody this woman viciously hated, and had a good reason to hate? Again, it doesn't matter. If you already agree that abortion is justifiable, then a case like this is even more tragic or unpleasant than usual. It feels even worse for a woman to have an unwanted pregnancy with a rapist than to have an unwanted pregnancy with her loving husband. But if you don't think abortion is justified, then how could this justify it? It's unfortunate, but it doesn't do anything as an argument. #### Would you force a 10-year-old girl to carry her rapist's baby? Ditto. It's an extra layer of tragedy, but if killing the baby isn't justified, then making the killer younger isn't going to change that. #### What if the woman is in no position to take care of a child, like if she's homeless?
