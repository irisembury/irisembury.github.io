!meta title: A case for abortion date: 2026-02-16 The way I propose we think about questions of legality is to consider the most direct implications of the question. Do you endorse violence being used in an instance of noncompliance? That is, to deter an illegal action or compel a legal obligation. For abortion, imagine you're in a hospital and a doctor is about to perform an abortion on a pregnant woman who has requested one. A police officer is there and defers to your judgment: should he intervene to stop this abortion from taking place? Using violence if necessary, in the event they refuse and fight back. That is the question we must answer. And my answer is no. It would be immoral to interfere and stop them. I'm in favour of abortion being legal, always, with no limitation and no rationale necessary by the person requesting one. If you are pregnant and no longer wish to be, then you should be able to end your pregnancy at any time, with only one consideration. The detail people often miss is that an abortion, that is, the removal of a fetus from its mother, does not necessarily result in the death of the fetus. It may be able to survive on its own, which is called viability. If the fetus is viable, then it's reasonable to suppose we have an obligation to facilitate that life. If, on the other hand, the fetus is not viable, then alas, we allow it to die. Either way, the pregnant person should have a right to have it removed. # What I am claiming, and what I'm not Let's divide actions into good, bad, or neutral, morally speaking. Then divide good and bad actions by whether they're *also* legally mandated or prohibited, respectively. However, the placement of these choices within the categories of good, bad, or neutral is mostly inconsequential. If you believe abortion is *bad* but don't want it to be illegal, that's fine by me, or at least not so significant a disagreement that we need to talk about it at length. ## Terms clarification ### Pro-abortion, anti-abortion The common terms to describe a person's attitude about abortion are pro-choice (for abortion) and pro-life (against abortion). These terms are both misleading and bias their own perspective. I prefer to be most direct and say pro-abortion and anti-abortion, and I mark myself as pro-abortion. I have also sometimes remarked that abortion is good, in a colloquial manner of speaking. Though I wish this had always gone without saying: this does not mean I want people to get abortions for the sake of doing so. To get an abortion is typically a grim decision, as unpleasant for the mother as for anybody else in society. If the number of unwanted pregnancies decreased to zero and as such no abortions were performed, that would be a good outcome. I favour abortion in the same way I favour chemotherapy. This is a fitting analogy in numerous ways. To get chemotherapy is unpleasant, but it's less unpleasant than dying of cancer, and we prefer to live in a society where chemo is an option rather than one where it's not. And if I describe myself as pro-chemo, nobody would interpret this to mean I want people to take chemo for no reason, or that I want more people to get cancer for the purpose of maximizing our chemo consumption, or that I'm going to throw chemo parties and talk about how great and wonderful chemo is (beyond its utility in saving lives). It would be easily understood that I'm pro-chemo in the sense I favour it being an option for people to use in the event they have cancer. Likewise, I'm pro-abortion in the sense that I favour it being an option for people who no longer wish to be pregnant. It is "good"---that is to say, less bad. ### Fetus In discourse about abortion, we often use the term "fetus" as an imprecise catch-all for all prenatal human life. This is not technically accurate. From conception a human goes through numerous stages, typically given as zygote, then blastocyst, then embryo, and then finally a fetus, as it's designated for most of pregnancy (from before the end of the first trimester) until birth. Though abortion can happen at any stage in pregnancy, and most happen during the embryonic stage, not the fetal, we've become accustomed to talking about fetuses because it's during this stage that we perform the most controversial abortions, whose legality is most affected by policy disagreement, and because it's the abortion of fetuses that makes the strongest case for abortion restrictions. For my purposes here the distinction between a fetus, an embryo, or any other prenatal life is unimportant, and so any time I reference a fetus you can read this as a general reference to any such prenatal life. The term is also not intended to minimize or trivialize the value of what's being discussed: a human life, at a stage of development that we all went through ourselves at some point. I, too, was once a fetus. The fetus is sometimes referred to as "a clump of cells" by abortion proponents, which may be true in the most literal sense, but of course it also has the potential to be so much more. These medical terms are useful for their precision or specificity, but let's not be callous about what is at stake here. # The right to security of person, or, The right of bodily integrity I'll begin with some questions to establish common ground about our intuitions. And for now, I'll set aside any questions about the relative moral status of the fetus, because this is unnecessary for the argument I intend to pose. That is, I consider the fetus to be a full person, of equal or comparable moral weight to an adult. As you may be able to infer by this point, if you're familiar with the existing philosophical discourse on abortion: the argument I make is not identical to, but is similar in approach, to those posed by J.J. Thomson in her influential 1971 essay, *A Defense of Abortion*. Why can the government not force you to undergo medical experimentation, to accelerate the rate of medical research? Or, if somebody is dying in a hospital bed from organ failure, why can the government not force somebody to donate the organ tissue they need? These are both measures that would save lives. And yet they're also intuitively horrific, things that we all know shouldn't be accepted but which you might find in a novel describing a possible dystopian future. We don't accept policies like these because we must consider the possibility we may one day be the ones who are subjected to them (see "the veil of ignorance" concept as popularized by Rawls), and because they conflict with a rather fundamental desire we all share, which is for **security of person**, that is, a right to maintain **bodily integrity**. (Here, integrity doesn't mean dignity or respect, but rather the state of being whole, complete, or unimpaired, as in "structural integrity".) (This sentiment is sometimes mistakenly expressed as the right of bodily autonomy, but insofar as autonomy refers to your ability to act freely or make your own decisions, there are valid reasons to restrict a person's autonomy. We interfere with your autonomy when we send you to prison, for example. But to imprison a person, while a serious measure we shouldn't take lightly, can be justified, and does not feel intuitively horrific in the way forcing somebody to undergo medical experimentation does. This is because only the latter affects the integrity of your body. This likely also explains why no modern country engages in sterilization or torture as forms of punishment, and why nearly all Western countries no longer practice the death penalty for any crimes. The only exception is some parts of the United States---the same parts that are most enthusiastic about trying to restrict abortion, a coincidence that's probably meaningful.) **What is pregnancy?** It's an arrangement whereby another being is dependent on your body for their survival, and the continuation of pregnancy causes you to incur definite bodily harm, as well as the risk of further, more serious harm, possibly even death. It's therefore something which, though virtuous, should be a voluntary relationship only. A person should have no legal obligation to allow a fetus to sustain itself at their expense. If they express no longer consenting to the arrangement, we should allow them to detach theirselves, even in the event this means the fetus will die. This is because the fact we might save the fetus's life is not sufficient to justify imposing pregnancy onto a person unwilling, just as the fact that forced organ harvesting or forced medical experimentation would save lives was not sufficient to justify those practices either. From here the argument becomes whether imposing a restriction on abortion can be positively justified. The opponents of abortion believe it can be while those of us who are in favour of abortion find their rationale for doing so unconvincing. Their case that we could save the life of the fetus is not a sufficient justification for overriding the mother's right to security of person. It's also worthwhile to note the absence of debate in our society about the wrongness of these other measures that would impose onto our security of person. It was always and without much hesitation understood by all parties to be a line too far. The fact abortion has remained a glaring exception to this intuition for so long is most likely explained by how the people making decisions about matters of law in the past were entirely man. Women were not only disregarded but treated as merely instrumental in the process of birthing children for their husbands, who were historically considered to have had a right to sex with their lives, with or without their wives' consent (marital rape was therefore not a valid concept). It only seems natural that a society which doesn't grant women the right to decline sex in their own homes would not pause to ask if women should have a broader social right to security of person, and one that may interfere with men's ability to acquire children to boot. It has been said many times before, but I'll say it once more: if men could get pregnant, the right to abortion would be immediately codified and this would not even be a discussion. # Is abortion an act of killing? The simple argument for restricting abortion is that it's killing another human being, which is something there is a legitimate public interest in preventing. This is why we have laws against murder. This is often posed as though obvious and uncontroversial, a starting point that all people should agree to. The implication of this framing is that people who are pro-abortion believe that abortion is an act of killing, but a justified one. The discourse then centres upon that justification, whatever it may be. But I reject all of this. The question is not whether you can kill the fetus, or whether you have a right to do so (you don't). The question is if you can choose to let the fetus die or if you have an obligation to save it, even at the expense of your own rights. This is worth understanding because there's a significant and undeniable moral difference between killing a person and not saving them. Let's suppose you walk by a large, powerful river and see a man has fallen in who calls for help. If you were to jump in and swim after him and pull him to rescue, saving him from drowning, this would be a great act and you would surely be applauded for it---but in making such an attempt, you would be putting yourself at great risk of harm or death, so it's understandable and defensible if you chose not to. Nobody would say you've killed this man by not saving him. Though you chose, ostensibly, to let him die, you weren't the cause of his death. Which is why you don't have a legal duty to jump in to make such an attempt. And this is the same category, morally speaking, in which we find abortion. Some people object to this framing. They say allowing pregnancy to continue is the neutral path or the path of inaction, and getting an abortion is an active choice to interfere with pregnancy in such a way that causes the fetus to die. It's therefore absurd of me, they say, to compare abortion with standing at the riverbank doing nothing and to compare pregnancy with jumping into the river to save the drowning man. The action--inactive dynamic is reversed. In their view, abortion is more like if there were a man swimming in the river and you chose to throw a fishing net onto him, causing him to drown. The problem is the cause of death in the case of abortion isn't as simple as if you were to walk onto the street and shoot a man with a gun. The fetus is unable to live on its own, unaided by the womb of its mother, because it hasn't yet developed the bodily systems necessary to do so. This is the only reason it dies---not because we make some concerted effort to prevent it from surviving. Now, in some abortions performed after the first trimester, where a fetus has grown beyond a certain point, a doctor may deliberately kill the fetus before the procedure, probably through a lethal injection. This is done because in those cases, we know the fetus cannot survive on its own and death is certain. The alternative would be for the doctor to remove the fetus from its mother and allow it to immediately suffocate and die on a table in the operating room, in a way that's more drawn-out and traumatic for the mother and medical staff than if the fetus were to die by lethal injection prior to evacuation. This in particular may be what we call an act of justified killing, because it does appear to be kinder for all involved than letting the fetus die otherwise. Jon Snow killed Mance Rayder with a bow and arrow, but only because he was about to be burned to death by order of Melisandre. Jon didn't have the power to save Rayder's life---that was out of his hands---but he had the ability to save him from the fate of being burned alive, which is a far worse death than an arrow through the heart. So, do we condemn Jon Snow and call him a murderer for killing Rayder? If this specific act is what somebody is opposing when they speak of abortion being killed, they're free to argue for the alternative: let them insist that even if a fetus is going to die anyway, doctors must not be allowed to use lethal injection to improve that death. I don't know why anybody would be motivated to impose such a restraint, as the procedure that would be performed afterwards would still be an abortion and the fetus would still die, albeit now more unpleasantly. This allows us to distinguish between the two actions: performing an abortion (removing the fetus from its mother), which is not killing a fetus more than it is choosing to allow it to die; and second, performing a lethal injection before an abortion, because you understand the fetus is going to die briefly anyway, and you believe it's justified to kill it in this instance to save it, and us, from a worse fate. Importantly, then, opposition to the lethal injection is not opposition to abortion itself. To return to the analogy of the drowning man in a river: a more comprehensive analogy may be to imagine if there were a coil of rope nearby, that you quickly grab one end throw the other into the river for the man to grab onto; but after he grabs it, you realize his weight combined with the force of the river is more than you expected, so you let go. Though letting go of the rope is an action, a decision point, this only returns you to an initial state of inaction, prior to when you entered into this arrangement (holding the rope) that you weren't compelled to enter. And here again it's clear that nobody would say you killed this man. We would only say you didn't save him. Perhaps you began saving him, or made an attempt to save him and then stopped, but you were not the cause of his death in the same way one would be if they had walked up to him on the street and fired a gun at him. If a fetus were capable of living on its own and then we inflicted a condition upon it that led to its peril, then we could say we're killing the fetus, but that's not the case. It's not the fault of the mother nor medical staff that the fetus doesn't yet have working lungs. It came into the world that way. Its dependence is part of its nature, not something we could reasonably have prevented. ## Counter-arguments **But don't we have an obligation to preserve the life of a child in other circumstances? It wouldn't be okay for a mother to stop feeding her children and let them starve.** It's true that we can enter into scenarios where we gain a duty to perform certain actions, and taking care of a child can be one of them. But this is a different type of obligation qualitatively, as it doesn't conflict with your right to security of person. It's just labour you agree to do and are compelled to continue doing. Nor is this an obligation that you can't withdraw from, as it's for somebody to relinquish guardianship of their children to the state or to another person. I've seen arguments posed against abortion which ask us to imagine a scenario where a person is stranded in a snowstorm or a deserted island, or some other scenario that isolates us from society. In some scenarios like this, depending on the exact configuration of events, it seems reasonable to say people may have a moral duty to labour in some way to save others, and you may feel it justified to use force in those cases. But the function of analogies like this is to remove the question of bodily integrity from our consideration and only talk about labour or use of resources. **Doesn't the mother have a special relationship with the fetus that justifies a moral duty, since it's her fault the fetus is in a state of dependence, as she's the one who put it into that scenario to begin with?** A potential flaw in the analogy of the drowning man is it begins with the man already in the river. How did he get there? Some people would argue that---insofar as pregnancy is like saving a drowning man---a mother, by getting pregnant, is like the one who pushed him into the river in the first place, putting him at risk of drowning with the understanding that she would be the one who keeps him alive. This act justifies us imposing onto her a legal duty to continue pregnancy until completion. It's an interesting thought, though it may also expose the limits of the analogy. If you did push a man into a river and he drowns as a result, we wouldn't say the crime was not jumping in to save him. The crime was pushing him in to begin with. Even if you did jump in to save him (or throw him a rope or buoy), it would still have been a crime to push him in, putting him at great risk of injury or death against his will with a back-up plan with no guarantee to work and that he didn't agree to. What does it look like to cast this back into speaking of pregnancy and abortion? If a woman gets pregnant and then has an abortion, would we say the crime was not the expulsion of the fetus, but her getting pregnant to begin with, thus putting the fetus into its state of dependence? To become pregnant is, one could argue, to bring a fetus into a situation of being at high risk of death or injury, and of being dependent on you for its own survival. Is it therefore immoral, akin to pushing somebody into a river? But of course, this is only because there was not a conscious being to offer or deny agreement to such events, as it didn't exist yet; and this risk and dependence is a necessary condition for life, and ergo not something that could be condemned or viewed negatively. To compare it with other instances of imposing risk onto somebody against their will simply doesn't work. To explore this aspect of abortion while keeping it analogous with the drowning man, we may have to modify the latter to suppose it was indeed your fault the drowning man is in the river, but that it was only by falling into the river that he was able to be alive to begin with. Perhaps this is the sacred river of spawning, a holy site where a godlike being conjures new men, but only at women's request. At that point, most people would say the analogy has been tortured beyond its usefulness. There isn't anything else in life that mirrors abortion perfectly. There is always some way in which it's different from other scenarios if you examine the comparison deeply enough. The purpose of invoking the drowning man comparison is not to say that it's mechanistically identical to the moral dynamic of abortion, but to provide a simple way to understand the relevant distinction between killing and not saving. And it also does help to articulate my own attitudes about abortion, as how I feel about the two scenarios is roughly the same despite the differences we've identified. **Isn't the mother giving implied consent by choosing to get pregnant?** This is generally a retort to my saying, as I did earlier, that pregnancy should be a voluntary relationship and it would be immoral to force somebody to continue a pregnancy they don't agree to. Some people counter this by arguing the mother must have given her consent to the arrangement by choosing to get pregnant, and that, once given, this consent cannot reasonably be withdrawn because doing so puts another person's life in peril. There are numerous analogies that can be drawn here, for example, if a person commits to flying a plane for some passengers, they could not reasonably decide to stop flying the plane midway and let the plane crash and its passengers die. It's proposed, and I grant, that it could be justified to use force to compel this person to fly the plane to safety and land it. However, I can't think of any other scenario in which a person cannot withdraw their consent from something that implicates their security of person. If somebody agreed to donate part of their liver but later changed their mind, nobody would propose we can go to their house and force them to submit to the operation because they initially agreed. I also question we can reasonably say a mother has consented to pregnancy. Perhaps if she explicitly states as much every time she has sex then we could say this, though that wouldn't be information we have access to if somebody comes into a clinic requesting an abortion (and we could never reasonably assume this, so it couldn't be used to make policy). But normally pregnancy is something that happens randomly and often despite some effort to prevent it. What some people argue is that by having sex *at all*, a woman is implicitly consenting to any pregnancy that occurs because she understands this is a possible outcome of sex. But that clearly isn't how consent is treated in any other scenario. That is, we don't say if a person does something which entails a risk of some bad outcome that they've consented to the bad outcome. I understand that driving a car involves a risk of accident, or that going out late at night involves a risk of being mugged and stolen from, but nobody would say this means a mugger is not imposing on me because I consented to him doing so by leaving my house. # When does virtue become obligation? Suppose you're walking outside and come across a large, industrial machine, like a garbage compactor, and you notice that a person has fallen inside. She calls out to you and explains her predicament: in a few minutes the machine will activate and crush her to death unless you save her. To do so, all that's required of you is to press the emergency stop button on the side of the machine. This will not only disarm the crushing mechanism, but also allow her to open the side door and escape. In a scenario like this, one could make the argument that not only would pressing the button be a good action, but that to *not* press the button would be morally bad, a moral failing or defect on your part. It may feel that we have a duty to act and save this person, and even that it could be right to punish somebody for not doing so, that is, for it to be a legal obligation. This could be compared with pregnancy if you believe a pregnant person has a moral duty to continue pregnancy in order to save the life of her fetus from the demise it will otherwise face. To maintain some clarity of thought, this prompts us to consider two different categories of morally good actions. First, those which are good but not necessary, and which instead represent you going above and beyond. Though you would be applauded for these actions, you would likely not be condemned or criticized for not doing them. This category has been called "supererogation" (from the Latin for "extra payment"). Second, there are actions which are morally good, but which you also have a duty or expectation to perform. If you fail to do these things, you might be condemned for not doing them. There isn't a general answer, as all people believe in some moral duties that should be legally required and others that should not. As for letting somebody die, it depends on where. In some countries, like France, to do nothing for a person in imminent danger may result in legal trouble, as you have a duty to act in some cases if there's no risk to your own safety in so doing. In other countries, like the United States, you may find there's no legal obligation to do anything at all, even if you could've saved somebody with the mere press of a button (you don't even need to walk away---you could stand there and watch them die if you wanted to). Which approach you prefer is a matter of opinion and both opinions have merit, which isn't the focus here. The problem is that even if we decided it *can* be a legal duty to save somebody's life in a situation like the one I describe, there's a clear distinction to be made between (A) being able to save somebody with the mere press of a button, and (B) putting yourself at great risk of injury or death in order to save somebody. This difference exists in our intuition (as *common sense*) as well as in law. Just as we cannot reasonably forbid self-defense, as the law cannot compel you to give up and allow yourself to die, so we couldn't reasonably demand you save another person's life at the expense of your own. In short, the law cannot punish people for self-preservation. The experience of being pregnant and giving birth imposes great costs onto the mother. For months she must endure pain and discomfort, fatigue, loss of mobility, increased risk of various conditions and infections. Then giving birth is likewise painful and risky, with many complications that may arise, some of them life-threatening. Even in a case of successful pregnancy where no particular medical complication arises and the child is born and declared healthy, the mother's body sustains permanent damage resulting from the changes it undergoes as part of this process. So, could we reasonably mandate people must act to save others, in some scenarios? Maybe, if that doesn't require putting yourself in harm's way, though that's not a given (some people would answer "no"). And pregnancy isn't like that anyway. # The personhood question Say we invent a machine that can remove a fetus from its mother, easily, without any risk or pain, and at any stage in pregnancy from the moment of conception onward. It then allows the fetus to live and grow in a medical chamber that simulates a real human uterus until maturity, at which point it can be removed, artificially "born" into the world as a baby. If this were so, then some people may dislike the impersonal or artificial nature of this machine and prefer natural pregnancy, but any woman who wants an abortion may terminate her pregnancy using this device without controversy, for the issue of the potential death of the fetus has been resolved. But one question would remain: when do we gain an obligation to respect the life of the fetus in the same way that we do a fully grown human? Is it from conception? If not, then what? The way many people phrase this question is: "at what point does a human gain legal personhood?" This has some practical implications. Would society have an obligation to keep alive any fetus removed by this machine, no matter its stage of development? If a woman were to take a poison pill that killed the fetus inside her and induced miscarriage, despite this machine being available, would this be a crime, akin to if not murder? I have conflicting thoughts, which I'll share tenuously. Like many people, I find intuitive appeal in saying a blastocyst that hasn't yet developed a brain or nervous system seems less important than a fully developed human. It doesn't even have a conscious experience of the world, so why does how you treat one matter any more than how you treat a desk or chair? Then again it also feels strange to speak of it in such terms, as though it has no value whatsoever and could be thrown in the trash without regard. In some ways this is the most precious, valuable thing there is. After thinking about it for a while, what I propose is that fetuses have great value *to us* without being moral patients theirselves, in the same way that if I grew a tree in my yard it might be valuable to me, and destroying it might be immoral or a crime insofar as it's *my* tree and ergo you're destroying my property, but we wouldn't say its destruction is immoral *to the tree* or speak of cutting down a tree as though it's murder, but the tree, valuable or no, doesn't have a conscious experience. So to me it doesn't feel like a murder to destroy an embryo and it probably makes sense to say an embryo is not a legal person, but I could also understand if, as a matter of policy or attitude, we still treated embryos as a valuable resource that one shouldn't destroy for no reason. # Recap I claim there should be no legal restriction on abortion itself. No person should be forced to remain pregnant if they no longer want to be. However, there is a duty by medical staff to try and preserve the life of a fetus if they're able to. The term we use for a fetus that's capable of living on its own is viable, so if a fetus is viable then we have an obligation to save it, but if not then we allow it to die, as we're not able to save it without imposing onto the rights of the mother. This is how I think society should be. And that's effectively how it is in many places already, including Canada and much of the U.S. from what I gather. It's a very reasonable state of affairs. # Bad pro-abortion arguments There are many popular arguments that attempt to quickly tell us why it's important for abortion to be legal, which people who are pro-abortion find satisfying to say or write onto social media to signal how they feel about the issue; but some of these arguments have problems that render them pointless or unpersuasive when directed at people who don't agree with you about the underlying facts of what we're talking about. The most common mistake often made is failing to distinguish between the death of a fetus during pregnancy from other instances of death, seemingly presupposing the frivolousness of the former, despite this being the very thing that's often in contention. #### You just want to control women's bodies. This leaves unclear why one shouldn't want to control other people's bodies, insofar as I want to stop them from using their bodies to commit crimes. Am I controlling men's bodies by telling them to not sexually harass women in the workplace? We all face constraints on our behaviour imposed onto us by society. And telling somebody how to use their body to prevent them from committing murder seems easily justified to most people. So this rhetoric is unclear, and the only thing a person who's anti-abortion can take from it is that you seem to be calling them sexist or insensitive in some way---which in their view is rather absurd, because you're saying it's sexist to tell women they're not allowed to commit murder. #### Would you force a victim of rape to carry her rapist's baby? Let's imagine instead of getting an abortion, a woman were murdering a toddler. Would it matter if the woman had been a victim of some other crime at some point? That couldn't possibly justify killing a baby. What if this toddler were the child of somebody whom this woman viciously hated and had good reason to resent? It doesn't matter. If you already agree that abortion is justifiable, then a case like this is even more tragic or unpleasant than usual. It feels even worse for a woman to have an unwanted pregnancy with a rapist than to have an unwanted pregnancy with her loving husband. But if you don't think abortion is justified, then how could this justify it? It's rhetoric that can pull at your heartstrings, but it doesn't do anything as an argument. #### Would you force a 10-year-old girl to carry her rapist's baby? Ditto. It's an extra layer of tragedy, but if killing the baby isn't justified, then surely this fact can't be what justifies it. #### What if the woman is in no position to take care of a child, so it won't have a good life? This is fundamentally a mistaken path to go down. Why does it matter what the child's life will be like? Why does it matter whether the mother can take care of it, or whether it's likely to grow up in poverty? Would we ever euthanize a child for this reason? A two-year-old infant is growing up in poverty somewhere in the world. Does it make sense to murder them, to put them out of their misery? If not, then why is it any more applicable here? To make this justified, you'd have to explain the difference between these two actions. But if you do that, then abortion becomes justified even if the baby won't be growing up in poverty. So why even talk about this? In the meantime, if somebody thinks abortion is murder, you cannot possibly justify it by pointing out your proposed victim is likely to have a bad life, any more than one could justify killing children who have been born in the same way.
