It's become commonplace in far-left communities to conflate Israel's very existence with some kind of extremism, using "Zionism" as though a nasty term for somebody with reprehensible views. A more accurate summary is that it's a shorter way of saying Jewish nationalism, originating with the Jewish independence movement that formed in late 19th century Europe. Weary of persecution being such a common feature in their historical experience, some Jews proposed forming a state of their own: a place where they wouldn't have to endure being a powerless minority on the fringes of some other people's country. Theodor Herzl (1860--1904) named this idea Zionism after Mount Zion, the hill in Jerusalem where Solomon's Temple was built in the Ancient Kingdom of Judah 3,000 years ago; and it was this movement that led directly to the creation of the modern country of Israel in the 1940s. As a political position, then, Zionism entails, at minimum, wanting Israel to exist as a place for Jews, but doesn't contain any specific instruction for how that country ought to behave or be governed. It could be a repressive theocracy, like some other Middle Eastern countries, or it could be a land of progressive-minded socialist peaceniks. There's always been diversity of opinion among Jews in the region. Still, essentially any Jewish politician who isn't actively calling for their own country's dissolution is considered a "Zionist" by default, and, similarly, anyone who supports a "two-state solution" is still considered a Zionist because they implicitly allow for one of the two proposed states to be "the Jewish one" rather than demanding Jews be forced into a single state with an Arab majority.
