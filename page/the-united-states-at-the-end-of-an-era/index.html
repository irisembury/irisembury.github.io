Though it's not America-specific, another major catalyst for political change across the world has been the internet media revolution and how the average person has reacted to it. ## The internet media revolution Let's say I go to a supermarket in my neighbourhood. For the most part I don't need to ask myself whether the food or drugs on the shelves are safe. I can buy things with high confidence because I know I'm acting within a system that has rules and regulators every step of the way to ensure the quality of things that are sold in stores like this one. Now imagine what it would be like if all our quality assurance systems disappeared, and new stores opened up selling things that had never been tested before. The possibility of food or drugs being very harmful to you suddenly skyrockets. What would be the result? If we can't rebuild a regulatory system of some kind, then normal people would need to change their behaviour. We couldn't act so freely or carelessly as we had before. We would need to become skeptical shoppers, doing more work to distinguish good from bad product, or to find reputable vendors. This happened with our media. It used to be if you heard a claim on the news or read it in an article by a large-circulation newspaper, like the New York Times or the Wall Street Journal, you could safely assume all the facts are correct. The source would never fabricate things, and they would even probably make a reasonable effort to look into the topic they're reporting on, not uncritically blurt out the first claims they came across. This is still true today, when interacting with traditional, reputable media companies. But when the internet started displacing traditional media as the way people got their news, the quality of the product changed. The standards were no longer there. There were suddenly many new sources of information without the same checks on what they put out. In response, people would need to change their behaviour. We should become more media literate, more skeptical viewers, doing more work to distinguish good from bad reporting, to find reputable writers and commentators. It didn't happen. They've failed to adapt to these changing circumstances. People still believe things they see as though they were reading reputable, fact-checked articles by traditional publishers, even if it's just some guy with a YouTube channel or an image of text you scroll by on Facebook. We're living through a transitional period where everybody is constantly getting sick from eating bad food and taking unsafe drugs, because they're still buying things carelessly as though the store is still regulated when it's not. Not anymore. There's been an internet media revolution, and it's ruined people's ability to navigate and understand the world around them. They can't separate truth from fiction. It doesn't work through free association alone, without institutions and rules and enforcement. The good quality product will rise to the top. This hasn't happened. Instead, the people who lie simply succeed at lying, and millions of people believe them. I want to be optimistic, but the rest of the world can't safely assume and bet our futures on the assumption America will wake from its stupor and things will turn out fine. This might well be the end of the American period. And even if America does regain its sanity, you must understand our reluctance to bet everything on it remaining that way for good. For the United States I now feel a similar skepticism as we've often felt for unstable, developing countries that appear to be on the right track, but which still have problematic elements to their political culture that give us pause. Turkey was like this a few decades ago, and then, as feared, they underwent democratic backsliding and fell under the control of Erdoğan. European countries have their own military forces, but they're still largely dependent on the infrastructure and logistics of the American one. This needs to change. We need significant military investment to be able to operate independently. Not for the drastic case that a war breaks out between America and other Western countries, but for the less drastic case where America chooses to cripple other militaries by simply withdrawing all support for them. We need to reduce our dependence on America's continued support, because we can't trust it will always be there for us. An area that I find concerning is technology, especially information technology on the internet. Look up a list of the most-used websites, apps, and services used globally. Google, YouTube, Facebook, Instagram, Twitter(X), WhatsApp, Reddit, Pinterest, Bing, Quora, Discord, Amazon, Netflix, eBay, Yahoo, all the popular AI clients like ChatGPT or Gemini. It's all headquartered in America. Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta, Amazon, X Corp, OpenAI, and so on. We've allowed our countries to become too dependent on American companies and their platforms, which are in turn beholden to the American government and legal order. It's an issue more obvious now than ever before that politicians in other countries are dependent on using American-owned websites to communicate with their own constituents. There's a long road ahead of us in this respect, but we need to think about investing in non-American media and platforms.
