--- title: On the AfD victory in Saxony-Anhalt date: 2026-09-08 mirrors: substack:214741964, tumblr:827199160807817216 --- The right-wing AfD (Alternative für Deutschland, Alternative for Germany) just had [a good election result in Saxony-Anhalt](https://www.cbc.ca/news/world/afd-germany-election-saxony-anhalt-9.7334357), which is a state in Germany. Though all the reporting does *say* that it was just a state election, I've seen a lot of people reacting to the news on social media who are clearly mixing it up with a national election result, so perhaps that hasn't been made as clear as it should've been. Yes, like the United States, Germany is actually made up of small regions called states, which all have their own governments. There was a [national German election in 2025](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_German_federal_election), and now this one region just had [a state-level election on September 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Saxony-Anhalt_state_election), a couple days ago. !images src: media/saxony-anhalt.png | caption:The German state of Saxony-Anhalt The AfD has generally performed better in eastern Germany (Saxony-Anhalt and the regions east of it), but in Saxony-Anhalt the CDU (the Christian Democratic Union, a moderate right party currently in government) was still the largest single party in the state as recently as 2021. But with the election on the 6th, the CDU lost a bunch of seats and the AfD gained. Here's the before-and-after (black is CDU, light blue is AfD): !images 300 src: media/saxony-anhalt-election-results.png | alt: Comparing the Saxony-Anhalt election results in 2021 and 2026 The AfD got about 40 percent of the vote, which is a significant gain. It seems like they may also be forming part of government, whereas in the past a coalition of other parties would work together to keep them out (in Germany "in government" means having your members become part of the executive branch as cabinet ministers, as opposed to only having seats in the legislature). Is the party poised to take over Germany federally? Not likely, considering the party is much less popular in the rest of the country. In the 2025 election, about 20 percent of Germans voted AfD. However, depending on how elections and party politics plays out, if the AfD gains enough support, then keeping them out of the governing caucus may stop being possible. To satiate people and prevent them from turning to the AfD, we're now seeing [reporting about Friedrich Merz](https://www.telegraph.co.uk/world-news/2026/09/06/friedrich-merz-germany-immigration-afd/), the current German chancellor and leader of the national CDU, attempting to address some of the concerns raised by AfD supporters. The main area of interest, of course, is immigration, identity, citizenship. !images float src:media/merz-01.png |caption:Reporting by The Telegraph This is a good example of how you can get what you want in a democracy even if your party never wins a majority or gets to form government. The threat of your party winning creates an impetus for the governing coalition to listen to you and solve the problems you're addressing. What does the rising popularity of the AfD tell you, if you're the German government? That a lot of Germans are so upset about the state of immigration, they're willing to vote for the AfD, because the AfD is the only party willing to speak to that concern. It's great to see that German politicians understand this and are willing to adapt instead of digging their heels in, better late than never. Frustratingly, there are some people elsewhere in the world who look on the news of Merz saying that his government will tighten immigration and citizenship rules and react with despair. For two decades or so now, there has been a pernicious belief held by moderates, progressives, liberals, social democrats, and leftists, maintained against all evidence: that immigration doesn't actually matter, that it's not really a problem, that nobody really cares about it. It's all just a distraction, a trick, a scapegoat. No, what people really care about is the economy, or cost of living --- and nothing more! It's as though they have some sort of fundamental, psychological inhibition that prevents them from taking this topic seriously. Immigration isn't a made-up issue that nobody cared about until reactionary parties tricked us all into thinking it matters. It's actually something a huge swathe of normal people have an issue with. They simply don't like it. When they go outside and walk down the street and go to a market or a transit station and they look around and feel like they're surrounded by immigrants, it makes them feel discomfort and alienation. It makes them feel like their neighbourhood is no longer theirs, that they no longer identify with the place they live, that what used to be home is now foreign to them. It's a deep human problem that you can't hand-wave away with some stupid gotcha question about how not all immigrants are criminals or by showing us a bunch of line graphs. I personally don't want to support these new right-wing parties like the AfD or Reform UK (or Restore Britain) or National Rally (Rassemblement National) or Sweden Democrats (Sverigedemokraterna). Parties like these are often corrupt and have members who are former Nazis (actual ones) and who are therefore not the kind of people I want to trust in power. Farage in particular I find highly objectionable. What I want is a recognition of reality from mainstream parties and for them to meaningfully shift gears on immigration and citizenship policy, as Merz is now hinting he may do. But moreover I want an end to the progressive delusion that immigration doesn't matter and can be ignored. That is the ideal outcome --- not an AfD government.
