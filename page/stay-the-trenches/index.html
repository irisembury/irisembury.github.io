Nobody knows how the audiences don't get bored or see through it. Perhaps the point is to provide so much variation that everybody will find at least one variant that fits them well. So what do you do? Stop using these platforms? Jump ship? That's a tempting thought. I wish there was something identical to these websites in core function but owned by better people. It's relatively easy to create a Twitter alternative. The hard part is getting the millions of people who are accustomed to the current site to make the switch. Which you're not going to do. Especially not all those power-users who have big follower counts and don't want to start over, who unfortunately are an important part of the ecosystem because they're the ones who drive a lot of traffic. The problem is most of the value of a social media company is its **network effect**, meaning the number of people already using it. Twitter still has over 500 million monthly active users. Tumblr, which you might've forgot existed, supposedly still gets about 100 million. Bluesky is at 20 million, and we'll see if it can maintain that considering it reached this high with a big influx of recent attention. When you post onto Bluesky, it's like you're shouting into a crowd of 20 people, whereas posting on Twitter is a crowd of 500. If your goal is to reach a lot of people, Twitter has the bigger pool, and there's basically nothing Bluesky can offer to outdo that size difference. When I open Twitter today, it feels like the whole website is garbage. It's rage bait, crypto scams, half the people who are being pushed talk like Nazis, and everybody is arguing about some contrived culture-war moment of the week that it's hard to believe anyone seriously cares about. Is Bluesky, its upstart competitor, any better? At a glance, yes, though perhaps because it's not being targeted by all the same manipulation as the big sites. It's possible the winds will change again, and eventually the dominant narrative on these platforms will shift, the way these companies are being run will change, they'll stop trying to appeal to the right, maybe some of them will even ban outright Nazis again, and maybe things will be different. But when that happens, you want to already have a presence in these places. In fact, your presence in these places can be part of what causes those shifts to happen. It might seem hopeless at the moment, because these platforms are controlled by private actors, private interests. In the case of Twitter, it's not even publicly traded. Elon Musk is allowed to completely manipulate it, even to the disadvantage of Twitter as a business. There's no shareholders that he's accountable to. And it doesn't seem like Elon himself is going to change course. He's an ideologue, and a rather proud, stubborn one at that time. But he could surprise us. It's not outside the realm of possibility. As for Zuckerberg, who knows. For how much attention they've both received, neither of these men are easy to predict. It's possible something could happen to these companies. Elon or Zuckerberg could randomly slip on a banana peel and die tomorrow, or two years from now, or ten, and then who knows what happens after they're gone. They could also get less active and move on as they get older, have a change of mind, take a step back. Bill Gates did. Then in their absence, more sane people who work at these companies could step up. Not becoming left-wing necessarily, but being more neutral, banning bots, allowing the platform to shift in a more normal direction. There are so many things that could happen to change the tides. And again, think of the situation the right was facing in 2014. It probably also felt somewhat hopeless. Silicon Valley, in the San Francisco Bay Area, where all these companies were centred, was seen as a home of progressive attitudes, which had permeated throughout all of these companies' internal culture, to the employees who worked there, the HR departments, everything seemed like it was oriented around progressive politics. And yet they continued trying to use these platforms and carving out a place for theirselves on them. It's hard to predict how things might turn out. So it can be unpleasant in the moment. I don't like browsing Twitter. It sucks. But this is why I'm not in full boycott of these places, and I still post on them, even though I try to also support alternatives growing. But I think it's better in the long run to not totally abandon and cede ground, and instead stay and have your voice be part of the website, and who knows, maybe one day things will change for the better.
