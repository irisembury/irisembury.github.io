--- title: Medical assistance in dying date: 2026-09-14 mirrors: substack:215626100,medium:378a520d05e0,tumblr:828961026747809793 --- ### The situation as of now The concept of physician-assisted death isn't new, but **medical assistance in dying**, or **MAiD** (often rendered as MAID in legal or journalistic contexts), is [the modern lingo for it in Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/health-services-benefits/medical-assistance-dying/legislation-canada.html). It's also called physician-assisted suicide or euthanasia. The first country to allow it was the Netherlands, and today it's legal in a number of European countries, Australia, Canada, and some U.S. states, with more on the way. In the past if you asked your doctor to kill you, the answer would always be no because they weren't allowed to oblige. Even if they had uncontested proof you requested it, they would be prosecuted for homicide. According to [section 14 of the Criminal Code](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-46/section-14.html), "No person is entitled to consent to have death inflicted on them, and such consent does not affect the criminal responsibility of any person who inflicts death on the person who gave consent." It is further prohibited, according to [section 241(b)](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-46/section-241.html), to "aids a person to die by suicide." Together these prevented assisted dying in Canada. That's how it was from the 1980s (when the Canadian constitution was written) until June 2012 when [the Supreme Court of British Columbia decided](https://web.archive.org/web/20160428153704/http://www.courthousenews.com/2012/06/18/47546.htm ) these prohibitions conflict with a plain reading of [section 7 of the Charter](https://www.justice.gc.ca/eng/csj-sjc/rfc-dlc/ccrf-ccdl/check/art7.html ). This was appealed to the Supreme Court of Canada, giving us a 2015 ruling called [Carter v. Canada](https://scc-csc.lexum.com/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/14637/index.do ), in which they held the decision, writing: The **right to life** is engaged where the law or state action imposes death or an increased risk of death on a person, either directly or indirectly. Here, the prohibition deprives some individuals of life, as it has the effect of forcing some individuals to take their own lives prematurely, for fear that they would be incapable of doing so when they reached the point where suffering was intolerable. The **rights to liberty and security of the person**, which deal with concerns about autonomy and quality of life, are also engaged. An individual's response to a grievous and irremediable medical condition is a matter critical to their dignity and autonomy. The prohibition denies people in this situation the right to make decisions concerning their bodily integrity and medical care and thus trenches on their liberty. And by leaving them to endure intolerable suffering, it impinges on their **security of the person**. As a result of this decision, the section of the criminal code that would prevent doctors from killing their patients would no longer be enforced. If a doctor got arrested for it, the court would find them not guilty. Parliament had a few options to respond. They could try to amend or clarify the meaning of the constitution in some way. They could notwithstanding a bill that outlaws it. They could try to prevent physician-assisted suicide from happening through other, less direct means without criminalizing the act by doctors. Justin Trudeau, then leader of the Liberal Party in opposition, [said in the House](https://liberal.ca/remarks-by-justin-trudeau-on-physician-assisted-dying/ ) "the court has ruled, and now it is up to us, as legislators, to act." Peter MacKay, Justice Minister, [said](https://www.canada.ca/en/news/archive/2015/02/statement-minister-justice-regarding-supreme-court-canada-ruling-carter-al-v-attorney-general-canada.html ) the government "will study the decision and ensure all perspectives on this difficult issue are heard." In July, the government [announced a panel](https://www.canada.ca/en/news/archive/2015/07/government-canada-establishes-external-panel-options-legislative-response-carter-v-canada-.html) to study the issue in greater detail. The Liberal Party became the governing party that October, and in April 2014 introduced [Bill C-14 of the 42nd Parliament](https://publications.gc.ca/site/eng/9.820039/publication.html) to amend the criminal code and lay out a legal framework for physician assisted dying. They tried to be restrained in the beginning. It was only for end-of-life care, and only if the physician assessment was that you were definitely going to die anyway. That was the idea everybody seemed to agree on: we should save people if we can, but if they're beyond saving, then dying should be an option if they request it. A terminal cancer patient, or somebody who's 96 and experiencing organ failure of some kind, then we won't make you wait it out. Then a few years later [a court in Quebec further decided](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/montreal/medically-assisted-dying-law-overturned-quebec-1.5279067) it's not constitutional to require "reasonable foreseeability of natural death," nor for it to be required to be end of life, prompting even more legal adjustments. In March 2021, [Bill C-7 of the 43rd Parliament](https://www.parl.ca/DocumentViewer/en/43-2/bill/C-7/royal-assent) removed the requirement for end of life care if the patient is suffering from a "grievous and irremediable condition." This means the condition doesn't need to be life-threatening if it's something that causes suffering and has no foreseeable end. The bill explicitly asserted that "persons whose sole underlying medical condition is a mental illness are not eligible for medical assistance in dying." In the February of 2024, the federal government delayed consideration for lifting that restriction until 2024. In August, that decision [was challenged in court](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/assisted-dying-lawsuit-1.7298749) by an organization called [Dying With Dignity Canada](https://www.dyingwithdignity.ca/), demanding the government lift the restriction immediately. And that's where things are today. We still require a person to be an adult (18 years of age or older) to apply, but who knows how long until some court decides that's no good either. In other countries that requirement has already been stricken. In Belgium for example, a 2018 report showed [two cases of lethal injection being given to minors](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/world/europe/children-euthanasia-belgium-youngest-killed-terminally-ill-cfcee-a8481311.html), aged 9 and 11, because they had deadly diseases (cystic fibrosis and a brain tumour) and doctors were confident they were beyond saving. This would not be so disconcerting if not for the fact every line we have attempted to draw thus far has been fought back against and it's not clear where this is going to stop. A simple rule that keeps this program in the bounds of reasonableness for most people is that you must never offer it, promote it, or even mention or bring it up with a patient.If a patient requests it, then you can say that it's a request you're able to oblige, but that's it. Moreover, a physician shouldn't be forced to participate if they don't want to. I assume even people who are in favour of medical assisted dying should agree with these two lines being drawn, and when either of these lines are crossed is when MAiD has faced the most public backlash. There were a few stories in recent years about medical practitioners, mostly nurses, mentioning it to patients and presumably being reprimanded, if the stories were even accurate. If this program is to exist, that must be taken seriously. ### What should we think about all this? First, I don't like social progress being made through a court suddenly finding new meaning in old text. In general, social progress should be made by Parliament as an expression of public sentiment. That isn't the role of judges. Moreover, I don't agree at all with the rationale provided by the court that because some action may result in some person choosing to kill theirself "prematurely" (earlier than they would have if not for MAiD), this action therefore conflicts with a right to life. If Parliament were to outlaw suicide (not assisted suicide, but all suicide), then we could reasonably argue this conflicts with a right to bodily autonomy perhaps, but it seems absurd to suggest the Charter was intended to protect the right of doctors to kill their patients. This whole situation is an instance of what people call "legislating from the bench." Now to be sure, I wouldn't support a law which defines suicide as a criminal act. However, I don't believe suicide is something we should affirm or legitimize, for people with or without medical issues, either socially or through our institutions, much less through licensed medical professionals. I believe the involvement of a medical professional legitimizes suicide as an option, causing it to be viewed in a different light than if doctors were not involved, while also causing the doctor to be viewed differently. The positive aura that doctors have is being lended to suicide, to the detriment of the former. I understand nobody is meant to raise it in conversation with you at the hospital, but it's hard to not to still be aware of it as a possibility when your government is telling you that it's your right, and your doctor has a special medical term for it on request. If a person seriously wants to die and is committed to bringing that about, we probably can't realistically stop them indefinitely, and they can figure out how to do it on their own without needing involvement from a doctor. Even then, society should discourage such an outcome and affirm that people are meant to fight to live as best they can, life being the most precious of all things. When somebody is trying to jump off a bridge and an emergency worker talks them down, we're meant to celebrate that, not ask why they didn't let them do it because it was their choice. To be ambivalent to suicide and embrace death as a viable alternative to hardship is a sort of spiritual and cultural decadence and doesn't lead to the sort of community that I should want to live in. I don't want my neighbourhood to have "suicide clinics," as the court notes are now established in Switzerland. There is indeed an ideal case in which suicide is most sympathetic. Say there's a man, aged 92, diagnosed with cancer and Alzheimer's, half the time unable to remember his family, given only a few months to live. Now suppose he says to you that he's had a long and fulfilling life, but now is his time to go, and he intends on returning home and falling asleep in his garage with the car running. I feel most of us, even if we feel somewhat conflicted or sad about it, would look the other way and let it happen in such a circumstance. In fact, some number of people could even bring theirselves to help him do it, if he asked. This is because so little is being lost, being that he's at the end of his life, is losing his memory and sense of himself. But what about in other situations? Say a man is in his 40s and gets diagnosed with cancer, and finds his predicament so depressing that he requests to die through MAiD and is accepted. It's possible he wouldn't have done it if not for the choice being legitimized through MAiD, and that could've survived cancer and lived another 50 years if he didn't choose to give up. Even though it was his choice, this wasn't a good outcome nor something anyone should feel good about participating in. So far in Canada,we've killed people as young as in their 20s (examples [here](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/calgary/calgary-maid-father-daughter-court-injunction-judicial-review-decision-1.7154794) and [here](https://globalnews.ca/news/11644100/ontario-family-changes-son-maid-bc/)), and in other countries they've killed people who had no physical ailment aside from psychological troubles, like the disturbing [case of Shanti De Corte](https://www.lemonde.fr/en/international/article/2022/10/10/2016-brussels-attacks-victim-granted-euthanasia-after-years-of-ptsd_5999805_4.html), whom Belgian doctors killed at age 23 because she experienced post-traumatic distress disorder. These are not all going to be sympathetic deaths. Some of these people, if not for MAiD being available, would have survived and lived, and then have been very glad they didn't go through with it. We must understand that by creating a MAiD program, the result will be more suicide of both sorts. There will be more ideal, sympathetic suicides but also more dubious and regrettable ones. Is this worth it? Are we comfortable with accepting this combination of outcomes? The answer I give is, of course, no. Even setting aside the abstract cultural issue I express above, it's not clear that the number of sympathetic suicides would outnumber the regrettable ones, and if we're not sure, then we have no reason to take this risk. Moreover, even in the most sympathetic cases, I'm not convinced it's important you have a right to request a doctor bring death upon you instead of taking the matter into your own hands and leaving theirs clean. !images src:media/maid-graph.png Overall, physician-assisted death provides a conceivable upside, but also real and conceivable downside. I very much prefer we simply not tread this ground. Medicine is and ought to be a naturally conservative field where we stop and ensure we have our footing before we proceed, and that has not been the case here. It would only be reasonable to proceed, as a sort of test, if we could be very specific and restrained about which people we allow to apply, and our experience thus far has been the opposite. Though I don't know the necessary legal argument, I would in principle support an act of Parliament that somehow restores *status quo ante* for the foreseeable future.
